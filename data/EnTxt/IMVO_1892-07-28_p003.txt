no one can say that he has had
anything to do with his being born
by white or black parents—by the
poor or the rich—the titled or the

and exigencies of birth is utterly
beside the question. Let him glory
in accidental circumstances who
knows otherwise. That in what we
are proud to call our Mother
Country (England) the diffusion of
education is rapidly chasing away
impalpable delusions of this sort
has been made patent and clear by
the adoption as candidate, and the

by the Liberals—Liberals in deed

and the great Englishman will have
“ the black man ”—contemptously

hearts” in numbering a black
man among his followers in the
House of Commons, when Mr GLAD-
STONE comes to deal with the case
of Ireland. Those who have been
to England invariably tell us that
instead of the black colour being a
drawback there it is a passport to
the best of circles ; and we have read
enough of the way things are man-
aged in Europe to believe them.
Nearer home we have recently
had instances of how the gaunt
spectre of prejudice against mere
colour is hiding its diminished
head. Lately at Breidbach, a
German settlement in Kingwil-
liamstown division, there came off
an election of members of the
Village Management Board. To our
agreeable surprise one of the two
native voters in the ward, Mr J. MA-
CLEAN MKOBENI, was nominated and
placed second on the poll by his
German neighbours ; and during
the ensuing year he will transact
business at the Board with two
white colleagues. At Mngqesha,
among another German community,
Mr TAKI MBAMBO has been likewise
placed by—we doubt not—a white
vote on the Board. These are
signs of the times, and hopeful
signs they are for the union of
hearts—and we desire no other—
among sections of the Kaffrarian
communities. Its value lies in the
fact that what has happened in
regard to the return of these our
countrymen to positions of respon-
sibility among their fellows is
perfectly spontaneous on the part
of white men themselves. There
has been no manoeuvring to push
the Natives forward. In the face
of facts like these it was a sad
and bitter disappointment to us on
turning to another sphere to find
an estimable Christian gentleman
also an office-bearer in the Church
giving vent to views such as those
Mr. JOHN MACKAY, M.L.A., is

THE EXAM. CRAZE.

THE publication of the results of the various Examinations, again calls
to mind the enormous amount of physical energy that is being put forth
in schools to score victories on the list of passes. It reminds one of
the “ cram and sham” inseparable from a system of education in which, as
Mr. FULLER, M.L.A., once expressed it, every­body was either examining
or being examined. It is well known that in most cases real, sound,
solid education was Being sacrificed in the scramble for a place on the
Exa­mination lists, the result being seen in the ineptitude of the
so-called passes for the most elementary duties of life in which
education has to be put to the test. In this hurry-scurry business no
oppor­tunity is allowed for taking a calm philosophic view of the
educational landscape. Necessarily the educa­tion runs in a nut. The
recipient of such education is much to be condoled with, as while it
does him no good in a sense it inflicts a lot of harm. Bitter experience
in after life teaches him this when he finds that his education ended
exactly where it should have begun. Apropos of this view of the
Examination Craze are the weighty observations of the new
Superintendent-General of Education. Speaking at the Victoria College
(Stellenbosch) closing last term, Dr. MUIR made the shrewd suggestive
remark to the effect that he hoped in this country pupils were not, as
in Europe, prevented from seeing the wood of education by the trees of
examination Let us hope the new Superintendent-General of Education will
begin in his own Department to minimise the rigidity of the preparation
for pas­sing cut and dried examinations for Standards, and if pupils
will proceed to examinations before they are perfectly familiar with the
field to be traversed by such tests let them be plucked and plucked
again, and it will do them no end of good.

  SAUER, INNES and MERRIMAN ; and
  had it been sponsored by Sir GORDON
  SPRIGG they would have violently
  resisted it. There is so much of
  human nature in Natives, that we
  dare say the surmise may be correct.
  Sir GORDON having bitten the Natives
  several times before they naturally
  would twice fight shy of any pro-
  posal- of such a nature eminating
  from his quarter, while on the other
  hand, Messrs. INNES and SAUER
  never having deceived them, would
  be in a better position to practise
  deceit on the people, at all events,
  for the first time. This is not our
  view of the position, however.
  Natives are not children, and be-
  sides politics is to them a second
  nature. They quite understand the
  difficulties of the situation that has
  resulted in the Compromise. By
  the measure of 1887. they could
  see clearly the attempt to strike off
  the Register those who had been
  there; and they were quite pre-
  pared to protest and proceed with
  their case to the foot of the Throne,
  disregarding even the constitutional
  cobwebs about going behind
  the decision of a scratch
  majority in the Cape Parliament.
  The Dual Vote was another propo-
  sal of pure disfranchisement, its
  object being to immediately nullify
  the votes of the Natives. This
  our people were ready to resist as
  strongly as they did the legislation
  of 1887. The Compromise, how-
  ever, does not aim at taking off
  anybody’s name; and it thus dif-
  fers from the Disfranchisement
  Act of 1887 resulting in the
  loss of seats which Natives
  could influence ; and from the
  Dual Vote which would have
  had even a worse result. In 1887
  the SPRIGG attempt awakened such

an interest among the Natives that while the legislation of that year
removed a large number from the Roll, quite as many who possessed the
qualification, but were indif- firent, were awakened to the impor­tance
of their rights which they had long allowed to remain dormant. The Dual
Vote would have crippled our people outright. The Natives know all this
; and are quite thank­ful to the gentlemen who have arranged a
Compromise that has averted such dire results, and spared them the
turmoil of a lengthened, costly agitation. Natives have nothing to do
with the necessities of party politicians. They have no ambition to
wrench the Govern­ment from the whites ; but all they insist upon is
that they should be allowed an outlet whereby they can make their
feelings known in the Legislature. At present they have not a single
seat that they can look upon as their own in the Houses of Parliament,
but they are quite con­tent to influence a few, as they have done in the
past, and as we hope they will do in the future, under the Compromise.
But any­thing calculated absolutely to de­prive them of a say in their
own Government they all and always will resent by every constitutional
means at their disposal.
