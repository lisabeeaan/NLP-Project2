NEW BLOOD.

ONE chief result of Mr. ROSE-
INNES’S powerful speech at

  Woodstock has been a longing on
  the part of constituencies, hereto-
  fore returning indifferent politicians to Parliament, to have new
  blood in their representation. This
  is as it ought to be. Whilst, no doubt,
  there are constituencies, like Victoria
  East, George, Aliwal North, and
  Namaqualand, which are repre-
  sented by a brace of effective mem-
  bers of whose appearance in the
  House and on platforms they can
  have no reason to be ashamed,
  there are others, and Kingwilliams-
  town and Queenstown are among
  these, which really can take no
  credit in the appearance of their
  members. An untampered repre-
  sentation of a wide expanse of
  country, such as is covered
  by King Williamstown and Queen-
  stown for example, by your
  FROSTS, WARRENS, SCIIERMBRUCK-
  ERS, and DU PLESSIS, can give
  satisfaction to no right-minded
  man. Thus, while in districts,
  such as those we have given in the
  first group, a change in the members
  would hardly improve matters, yet
  in divisions like King Williams town
  and Queenstown the representation
  leaves much room for improve-
  ment. To expect that every man
  in the last class can be dropped
  and a better take his place, would
  be too tall an order: but it is easy
  to see the reasonableness of the
  wish of a considerable number of

the electors, more particularly the younger men and the more ad­vanced
thinkers of the older gene­ration, in places like Queenstown and King
Williamstown, for the introduction of a dash of new men. And he is no
politician who cannot see that the substitution of a capable and
prominent frontier statesman like Dr. BERRY for, say Mr. M. J. DU
PLESSIS in Queens­town ; and of a gifted representative of the younger
Afrasians like Mr. GEO. HAY, whose daily occu­pation induces a ceaseless
study of public questions, for either Mr. SCHERMBRUCKER OT Mr. WARREN,
would raise the tone of the repre­sentation of these two important
Frontier divisions in the eyes of Parliament and of the country. They
are both earnest men of definite views and acknowledged ability to
impress others. The same cannot be said of the old members, fortunately
it is in the power of each individual voter who understands his duty to
himself and to his country to bring about what is wanted. The old
members have had a trial for ten or fifteen years with the result that
the politics of the country have become a tangled-skein, while they
stood helplessly by—if they have not actually assisted to bring the
state of things we deplore. And although it would be too much to hope
that “the old fogeys” should be all cleared, yet it is in the power of
each voter to help to introduce new blood, so that, at any rate, people
may reasonably look forward to witness a departure, no matter however
slight, from the old style of politics which has left so much to be
desired.

MUNICIPAL NATIVE POLICY.

WE have always held in these
columns that if ever there

  was a likelihood of the Native people
  becoming prosperous and good
  citizens by being in the firm grip of
  the Europeans, the result should
  first show itself in connection with
  Municipal Corporations, where al-
  ready the whites do pretty much
  as they like with the Natives with-
  in their boundaries. We say this
  because, in connection with the
  wider politics of the country, the
  fact that the Native chief or head-
  man is, as it were, a buffer between
  the Government and the people,
  has often been lamented; the
  impression being that if Govern-
  ment could have the direct and full
  control of the Natives, without
  having to take into account the
  chief, all would go well. That this
  is merely an excuse for doing no-
  thing is demonstrated by the utter
  break down of the policy of the
  unaided control of Natives by
  Europeans in Municipal areas.
  There the Native is found in bulk
  to be worse than he is in his pri-
  mitive location—steeped in all the
  vices of the dregs of civilization,
  the exceptions merely proving the
  rule. One need not go far to seek
  the cause of this. It is the result
  of the combination of neglect and
  repression. The municipal policy
  has been to squeeze out of the
  Natives as much as could be got
  out of him ; while a number of
  repressive regulations to render
  life as hard as possible, were deemed
  to be all the Natives wore entitled
  to expect.

  We rejoice to remark a disposition
  to change this old poisonous policy.
  The idea seems to be fairly taking
  hold of those entrusted with
  Municipal affairs that the Native,
  to be a useful adjunct to a town
  community, should have a few of
  his wants studied, and be treated
  as much as possible as one of the
  citizens. This will conduce to a
  certain amount of self-respect in
  the Native, and he will be the
  better fitted to take his proper
  place in the body politic as a pro-
  ducer, a consumer, and, therefore,
  a contributor to the sum of
  the prosperity of the community
  among whom he lives, and moves,
  and has his being. That this
  is the true Municipal policy
  everybody will acknowledge; and
  it is a satisfaction to us, as
  has already been observed, to see
  a move on the part of certain
  municipalities to address themselves
  to this long-neglected duty to a by
  no means unimportant section of
  their community Thus at Queens-
  town, under the benevolent guidance
  of the Hon. T. BAILEY, M.L.C.,
  the town has resolved to go to the
  expense of £1,000 to extend the
  water supply to the Native Location.
  Wo are sincerely thankful to Mr.
  BAILEY and to the Europeans of
  Queenstown, for thus interesting
  themselves in the prosperity of the
  Natives in their immediate vicinity.
  It is bound to react on the pros-
  perity of the town; and is not
  labor lost. At Grahamstown, too,
  a forward movement in Native
  municipal matters is showing itself

among some of “the Saints,” which we hope and pray may soon be realized
in action. Commenting on Mr. J. E. WOOD’S political meeting in the
Location of Grahamstown the Journal makes observations which we could
wish would find their way into every municipal chamber in the land. We
cannot do better than close this article with them. Our contemporary
writes:—

Another subject on which much com­plaint was made in the stale of the
Location, which is not supplied with water, nor planted with trees, as
the inhabitants think would be the case. We certainly agree that it
would be not only justice but good policy to make the Location a popular
place of residence for respectable, improving Natives. We should like to
see a scheme for helping them to build square houses, which they desire
to do, but are unable for want of means. Might not the Building Society
develop a small section with a reduced subscription for this purpose? At
all events it is a duty of the Town Council to find these people water
for the purpose of food and cleanliness. Never mind if the undertaking
cannot be made remuner­ative. It would pay, in the bettering of the
position of Natives in the location, and in the improvement of business
to our stores. This is a matter which should by no means be neglected.
We mention these things because they were elicited during the meeting,
and it clearly ap­peared that the Natives, who have little power to make
their wants known, never­theless do strongly feel that they are
neglected and slighted.

In Grahamstown, through the wonderful foresight of Sir GEORGE GREY we
believe, the Natives have titles to their small allotments; and some
have therefore been able to put up decent and substantial dwel­lings,
and by means of Building Societies, they can be encouraged to do a great
deal more in that useful direction. Will not the Councilors in King
Williams town, East London and Queenstown take the hint and go and do
likewise ?

UITENHAGE. —The Bond have set their minds upon securing to themselves
the two seats for Uitenhage and have nominating two candidates, Dr.
Vanes and Mr. Rautenbach being the elect. Mr. Mackay must be shunted
although he has borne the burden of representing the constituency alone
for two or three sessions. As non-Bondmen do not seem to move, why does
not some patriotic young man either from Port Elizabeth or in Uitenhage
offer himself to stand with Mr. Mackay. If would be no disgrace to be
beaten fighting in such a cause. We should think Mr. Edgar Walton would
make a very good member. Uitenhage might do worse than ask him to stand.
However, extraordinary apathy seems to be the distinguishing
characteristic of the Independents in Uitenhage. If, for all that,
mighty coaxing can affect anything we should hear soon that a good man
has at last been secured either in the district or in Port Eliza­beth to
fight with Mr. Mackay. The Eastern Province Herald comments on this
subject as follows: —Of the move­ments in Uitenhage one hears but
little. It seems as if the people were content to allow the Bond to
return the members for the division. Mr. Mackay, of course, will stand
again, and the electors are not likely to forget his services to the
consti­tuency. Mr. Rautenbach is practically an untried man, but it is
understood that he also will stand. The town of Uitenhage itself,
however, should have some say in the matter, and yet it has said very
little. The interests of the consti­tuency are important, the
constituency itself is one of the largest in the Colony and should be
worthily represented. There are several prominent townsmen who might
begin to think of serving their country. To mention only two, the
friends of Mr. Gubb and Mr. A. de Villiers might sound those gentlemen.

Mr. Parker has intimated the withdrawal of his candidature for
Grahamstown. This will materially increase Mr Grocott’s chance who
stands to win on the second votes of the supporters of Messrs. Wood and
Douglass.

The Rev. Adriaan Hofmeyr has announced his withdrawal from the Somerset
East contest; and the Bond are now trying to get the Hon. F. F. Wienand,
M.L.C., to retire from the Council to fight, in conjunction with Mr.
Abrahamson, the sitting members — Messrs, Palmer and Hockly. The
arrangement will not strengthen the Bond hold on the electors, as no
enormity had been perpetrated by the sitting members to require such
Herculean efforts to put them out.

The sitting members for King Williams town—Messrs. Warren and
Schermbrucker —have opened the electoral campaign by getting their
requisitions out; and the con­stituency has nothing to thank. Them for
precipitating matters at so early a date, seeing the election does not
come off until February or March next. Such tactics well become, members
who doubt their hold on the suffrag’s of the many among the electors;
but such a line of action often results in inconveniences those who
pledge themselves to men whom every right thinking voter feels can
without much trouble bettered.
