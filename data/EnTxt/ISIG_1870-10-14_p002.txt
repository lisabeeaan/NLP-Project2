THE EUROPEAN WAR.

THE news by the Nelly, confirmed and supplemented by the arrival of the
Saxon at Table Bay yesterday afternoon, is start­ling enough to make men
speak with bated breath. In another column our telegram from Cape Town,
gives the facts of the latest phase of this frightful struggle. On the
last Thursday of July, the Emperor Napoleon left Paris, and passed by
railway to Metz amongst the vociferous cheers and acclamations of the
people at the various stations on the line. In the beginning of Sept, he
appeared before the King of Prussia a crest fallen prisoner. His
splendid army numbering over 250,000 men has become in part, a shattered
mass of disorganised regiments and of wounded men filling the houses and
hospitals of France, while the remainder are a dispirited crowd of
prisoners in the hands of the Prussians. We need say nothing of the
thousands now deaf to the roar of battle, and quietly sleeping on the
slopes of Alsace and Lorraine. In six short weeks, this bloody drama has
been so far played out. The Emperor, in his pro­clamation at Metz, said
the war would be a “long and serious one.” We may well wonder what he
thinks of its duration and its consequences now—as he leisurely reflects
over these events in his retirement at Wilhelmshohe near Cassel. Twenty-
two years has the now ex-Emperor been employed in building up an empire,
and drilling an army which was supposed to be the most formidable in
Europe. Twice that number of days have been sufficient to shake his
empire into dust, and dis­perse his army beyond the possibility

of reconstruction. Another army may arise—for France, south of Paris, is
rich in men, and money, and patriotism, and we may believe, that if time
can be gained to raise new levies, this bloody work is only half
finished.

But Napoleon, how—“ sick in the world’s esteem, -wretched and low ”—must
he feel when he contrasts his position now with what it was in the end
of July. But as yesterday he ruled in the Tuil- leries over thirty-six
millions of men, and wherever he turned his eyes, they fell on the
symbols and splendour of his imperial power. He was not content with
that, and to-day he is probably the occupant of some fortified dingy old
German schloss, bereft of even the sweet gift of personal freedom.
Emperors are no exception to that law of righteous retribution—what men
sow they shall reap. He sowed the wind, and in the whirlwind of war,
dynasty, and empire, and army have alike disappeared. One would have
thought a man of sixty should have had more sense than to play the part
Napoleon has done during the last few months.

What the ultimate results to Europe may be, it is vain to conjecture.
But already there are signs of coming change and more trouble.
Napoleon’s captivity will probably be Rome’s freedom. And Russia seems
already to embrace the present opportunity, and is filling her arsenals
and fitting out her ships, for no good purpose to Europe and mankind, we
may be certain. This item of news is perhaps the most serious of all,
and may be the application of the torch that will spread the
conflagration of war through the east of Europe and draw Great Britain
into the struggle.
