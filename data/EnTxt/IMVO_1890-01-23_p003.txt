NATIVE OPINION

IT was, we confess, with no small surprise we observed that the
Kaffrarian Watchman deemed it a duty devolving on
its shoulders to lecture the Civil Commissioner of King Williams town on
the legal points involved in the Registration cases decided last week.
Our contemporary has displayed so deplorable an amount of ignorance of
the real points of the cases that it is a mystery of mysteries to find
out what can have induced it to undertake a free lecture on the subject.
The Civil Commissioner, to begin with, is charged by the Watchman with
having enacted a farce, because he accepted the claims to be registered
which had been rejected by Field Cornets. Most people would be unable to
discern a farce in this simple act of justice, any more than it would be
possible for them to extract sunbeams from cucumbers. The Legislature
very wisely allowed an appeal from the Field-Cornet, an officer
unlearned in the law, to the Magistrate, who breathes in the legal

atmosphere all the days of his life. It would be a very foolish act, in
our opinion, to change the law in the manner suggested by our
contemporary of one eye, by instituting an appeal from the Civil
Commissioner to the Field-Cornet.

It would, in fact, be a one-eyed arrangement. So much for the Watchman's
farce.

In the miscellaneous damning by the Watchman, occasioned by the result
of the Court, Native Opinion comes in for its share. We are rated
because we appear to believe that the rejected claimants really
possessed the qualification, and were
entitled to be registered. Of course we did, as we still firmly believe.
But our contemporary is most unfortunate in instancing the Tyumi
registration of 1887 to show what we would like to see prevailing; for
we verily believe that the Tyumi people, as occupiers of premises,
together with ten-acre lots held, not under communal or tribal tenure,
are fully entitled to be enregistered.
But, says the Watchman,

The fact that the Magistrate on Tuesday accepted the claims of those
rejected by the Field. Cornets did not go to show the incapacity of
those officials, especially when it was pointed out by His Worship that
the claimants had neglected to give the Field. Cornets sufficient
evidence of qualification, through stubbornness or something else.

From the above extract it will be seen that the Watchman admits that the
Natives proved their claims before Mr. CHALMERS, and holds the Natives
blameworthy that they did not take the Field-Cornets into their
confidence. The fact of the matter is, however, as our people stated in
Court, the Field-Cornets would not listen to what they were told, but
elected to follow the bent of their
inclinations, which, in Mr. CROWE’S and Mr. LANDREY’S case, as al! are
aware, are in the direction of disfranchising the Natives. What could
our countrymen do, under the circumstances, but to appeal to the Civil
Commissioner. As to the argument that the Field-Cornets, because they
reside amongst the people, are better able to judge of the validity of
the claims, it would be worth something if the Field-
Cornets’ decision were not vitiated by the biased mind already referred
to, and by the fact that, as in these cases, they took no trouble to
become personally familiar with the properties of the Natives, as they
rejected their claims off-hand. The valuation of the Government Sworn
Appraiser is next questioned, and the Magistrate blamed for not
slavishly following that of the Divisional Council. But, we submit,
there is abundant reason to set aside the opinion of the valuator of the
Council. There is no evidence that he is a duly Sworn Appraiser. For
years past he has fixed the values of Natives’ holdings at £25, until
our people began to claim the Franchise on it, when he suddenly and
unaccountably lowered it to £8. Moreover, the Council valuation is not
in accordance with what Natives’ lands fetch in the market. With
reference to the house property, those who know anything about it would
hold that the value set upon the average Native’s hut by the Sworn
Appraiser—£3—is ' lower
even than the £5 Government places on it. As a matter of fact, these
buildings cost more than either of these valuations. The Watchman calls
in question the value of KAMA’S farm, we see, but on this both the
Field-Cornet and the claimants agreed to take its value at about £1,000.
By virtue of this amount only four men were enregistered, and yet the
Watchman demurs ! As to the Peelton claims the Court was satisfied that
the men had occupied for the last five years 10-acre lots ; but where

before it is through the Section it will see that the fiasco and all the
rest of it,
over which it has expended printers’ time, printers’ ink and paper, is
nothing more nor less than a mare’s nest.

A MOST startling piece of news came to hand during last week, reporting
thedeath of the Right Reverend Doctor Mc-Kenzie. The Zululand Diocese
has received a severe blow in the loss of this

most energetic prelate. The election of Dr. McKenzie to the Bishopric of
Zulu­land seems to have been attended with most pleasing results. Though
not so long in the country, he appears to have acquired the language to
such a degree that he was able to preach in Zulu. He had held posts at
St. Andrew’s, Grahams- town and Bloemfontein, successively; and it is no
doubt that his ability in the management of these Institutions won for
him the Bishopric of Zululand. Since his arrival at Isandlwana several
important points in the way of improvement in Mission work have taken
place. A press has been established, which has done good work. We hear
that parts of the Zulu Prayer Book, based upon that brought out by Dr.
Callaway, the former Bishop of St. John’s, was sent out some time last
year, or the year before. His Lordship seems to have been heart and soul
in his work. He was a good walker, in tact he was suited for the work to
which he was called. He had just completed a journey to the Zambesi. We
join in sympathy with our Zululand people.

IN our last, occasion was taken to compliment the Governor’s Reception
Committee of King Williamstown on the perfect temper with which
suggestions respecting the demonstrations of the Natives were received.
The scene, how­ever, has since changed, and a clear ma­jority have
caught an idea from one of the members that such a demonstration was not
desirable for invidious reasons, which cannot be given in a print which
lays claim to decency. Of course, the character of those who raise these
objections can very easily be inferred from their style of speech; and
well may His Excellency pray to be saved from such admirers.
Nevertheless, we are glad to know that the fervid desire among our
countrymen to show their loyalty to the Queen’s Rep­resentative is not
likely to be affected by the scowls and grimaces of miserable
narrow-minded men. The Native demonstration, if the Governor is willing
to see it—and on this point Sir Henry Loch's farfamed courtesy and
gentlely demeanour leave no doubt—will go on independently, and will be
appreciated by the Governor all the more since it will be a
spontaneous,' unassisted, and genuine exhibition of feeling of affection
and loyalty on the part of the Natives of this country.

THEY do things differently in India. The Native Indians have a Congress;
and its fifth session took place about the end of December last. We read
that great enthusiasm characterised the proceedings, at which there was
present Mr. C. Brad­laugh, M.P., the well-known member of the House of
Commons, who is distin­guished, although a reputed atheist, by a
Christian and practical love of justice to the Natives, not only of
India but also of this country. He was most cordially re­ceived by the
Indian Congressmen in recognition of his services in their cause. This,
no doubt, redounds to the honour and credit of our Indian friends in
mak­ing those who stand up in their behalf in and out of Parliament,
feel that they are not unconcerned spectators. Let us trust this can be
said of our countrymen in South Africa.

IT is understood that, subject to any modification that may be forced
upon the party by circumstances, the following programme will be carried
out during the tour of His Excellency the Governor and Lady Loch. The
viceregal party will leave Cape Town on Thursday next for Port
Elizabeth, travelling by rail; will reach that port on Saturday, and
remain there over Sunday and Monday. On Tuesday, the 28th, they will
proceed to Grahamstown, remaining there until the 30th, when they will
go on to Fort Beau­fort. travelling thence to Alice on Friday, the 31st.
His Excellency will visit Love­dale, and, on the 1st of February, he
will arrive at King Williamstown, where he will remain until Tuesday
morning, the 4th February, on which day he will go on to East London.
Here he will stay until Thursday, February 6th, on which day he will
proceed to Queenstown, making a stay there of a couple of days. On
Satur­day, February 8th, he reaches Tarkastad, and rests there for
Sunday, leaving on Monday morning, the 10th, for Cradock, in which town
he will stay until Wednes­day, the 12th. The return journey home­wards
will be continued, and the party hope to reach Capetown in safety on
Thursday, the 13th February.

  ON the hurry in which the work of the
  Drink Commission was done in the Frontier Districts, and on which we
  animadverted at the time, the E. P. Herald very aptly remarks: Now
  this perfunctory discharge of a very important duty is not worthy of a
  Commission numbering among its members such names as those of Judge
  Maasdorp, Mr. Hofmeyr, Mr. Marais, and Mr. Wilmot, and we feel very
  strongly that they ought, before presenting their report to
  Parliament, to devote a little more time to some of the places we have
  named, and perhaps others, in order that their views may carry the
  weight legitimately due to opinions formed upon well-sifted evidence
  and impartial judgment. This is a duty which they owe to themselves as
  well as to the country which has to hear the expense of their official
  existence. As we have already stated, we wish to make no insinuations
  as to the object of the appointment, we believe that the Commission
  can do, and has done, good work, but the impression exists and has
  found utterance already, that all its work is not of equal excellence,
  and that its later proceedings have done much to discredit, in
  advance, its utterances, and render its usefulness exceedingly
  doubtful. If this impression gains ground the public will undoubtedly
  conclude, and will have ample grounds for the conclusion, that the
  intention of the Government was, from the very beginning, to hoodwink
  the country, and that the expensive Commission was appointed, not with
  the honest desire of obtaining reliable information, as it should have
  been, but for the purpose of shelving an awkward question and eluding
  plain pledges and responsibilities. These are the two interpretations
  of the Government’s action, and it is the part of the Commission to
  show, by a conscientious discharge of its duties, that it will be no
  party to what practically amounts to a fraud upon the country. It is
  not too late to do so.
