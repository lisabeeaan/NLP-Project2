Native Opinion,

NATIVE CENSUS AREAS.

  F the forthcoming Census has
  not taken hold of the popular

mind, it will be through no fault of
the Government. No stone has
been left unturned to popularise the
Census among the various sections
of the population. Of course, the
services of the Press have been
largely resorted to for this purpose ;
although, whenever there is a business matter between the Government
and the Press to be transacted, there
is no more close-fisted customer to
deal with than the Government. It
is quite refreshing, however, to observe that the services of the Press
are appreciated when, as on the
present occasion, the sympathies of
the people are to be enlisted.

The Census that will be taken on
the evening of the 5th April next
is, we believe, the most detailed
that has been over been taken in
this Colony. But in Mr. HENRY DE
SMIDT, the directing genius of the
Census, the Colony has an officer
quite equal to the occasion. His
plans to reach the people—and here
wo speak of the Native people- have
been the most liberal and feasible.
The Government has been well advised in urging that in Native areas
the work of enumeration should be
done by official, semi-official agency,
or by such unofficial persons as can
be thoroughly trusted efficiently to
do their work without causing need-
less irritation to the Natives ; and
a great step in the relation of the
Government and the Natives has,
in our opinion, been taken when the
suggestion was adopted that school
teachers, if suitable and competent,
should be employed as enumerators.
Our only regret is that in certain
districts this has not as in King-
williamstown—been generally adopted ; in others, policemen, and
other white men of no standing, in
the estimation of the people, have
been entrusted with these delicate
duties. What is sure to result from
this is misunderstanding and bitterness, defeating the objects of the
Census—a state of things which
might have been easily avoided had
more tact been shown in selecting
agents ; and no difficulty would have
been experienced in getting a number of intelligent young natives to do
the work in a peaceful way
among their own people, assisted by
the headmen. As things are we
are not so sanguine as we otherwise
might have been that information
from Native areas will be as complete as could be wished. The
people are very suspicious of white
men going among them, and by a
great deal of patience in explaining
could those of their own race succeed
in dispelling suspicion and fear that
no evil was intended. But it devolves upon all, enumerators or no
enumerators, to help the Government in this matter by removing
any misapprehension that may exist.
Towards this end we are ready to
contribute our share.

AN INTELLIGENT VOTE

THAT the Native vote is intelligent, is evidenced by the
kind of gentlemen it assists in
returning to Parliament. There
is no getting behind the fact that,
if a comparison were to be instituted between the representatives
for whom Natives vote and those
returned by other sections of the
community, the member supported
by Natives is invariably the better
educated, the more enlightened,
and in every way the superior of
the rejected of the Natives. Why
this should be the case is not for us
to say. But it is just possible the
Native of this country has bone-
fitted in these matters by the long
course of experience acquired under
his own institutions where the
system of sending representatives
to the Councils of the Chief was
not unknown. Then they under-
stood well enough that it was not
men of no calibre or position
among other Councillors that they
were to vote for ; but those who
would add to, not detract from, the
dignity of that august body. It is
because they strive to maintain
that ideal to-day that they are
always to be found on the side of
the most enlightened candidates;
and candidates possessing education
and manners which are sufficiently
passable at Court. We welcome
with deep satisfaction the good
opinion of so influential and enlighted a journal as the Port Elizabeth
Telegraph respecting our people. Our contemporary, referring to the
elections just over, has the following observations on the Native vote:—

  In the Eastern Province the feature of the election was the strong
  support which was given to Messrs. Peacock and Bradfield by the
  Natives. At Kingwillianistown, at Herschel, at Bensonvale, and at
  other polling places the Natives showed
  that they thoroughly understood where their interests lay. In the
  southern divisions of the vast circle they seem to have given three
  votes in favour of Mr. Peacock, while in the northern districts they
  gave almost unanimous support to Mr. Bradfield. It was, no doubt, felt
  that by this plan the Native voters could give their votes with
  greater effect. We do not doubt that there will be a good deal of
  soreness over this matter of the Native votes, and that the question
  of the franchise, which is to be discussed at the next meeting of the
  Bond at Kimberley, will be seriously discussed. But we think that the
  vast
  majority of the European voters are determined that nothing should be
  done which should tamper in any way with a franchise which the Natives
  have used intelligently.

  This opinion is valuable, especially at the present time when the air
  is full of the idle vapourings of the superficial in the community
  muttering disfranchisement for the Natives simply because they have
  used their votes not only in their own interests but in the best
  interests of the country at large.

  THE Farmers’ Congress has just held a four days annual session at
  Queenstown, under the able presidency of Colonel BRABANT —who has, we
  are convinced, been able to show the hon. Mr. BAILEY that he is
  something more than a mere military man, and a dweller
  amongst ticks. We always read the proceedings of this Congress with
  great interest; for the farmers and our people are very intimately
  connected. They live side by side in various relationships, and the
  welfare of the country depends upon rural Natives and Europeans
  understanding each other, and promoting each others welfare.

On some of the resolutions come to by the last Congress we can speak
with considerable satisfaction. The police force, created by the SCANLEN
Ministry, has, we are glad to sec, won the good opinion of the farmers.
In earlier days there were some wars of words between police and
farmers, arising principally from Commissioner DAVIES insisting that
stock reported stolen was not always surreptiously conveyed away. Truth,
which was on the Commissioner’s side, prevailed finally ; and now we
observe it openly stated in the Gazette that sheep reported to be stolen
had been miscounted, and that cattle the owner did not miss had been
driven home. For a short period it seemed as if the police were to be
annihilated by the fierce blasts from
Kei Road; but the force held on its way, and now has its efficient
services recognized by those it was intended to serve. The Congress
desires, however, to have both the Police and the C.M.R. (when doing
police work) made more efficient by having detectives attached. This we
have ourselves advised. There
arc numbers of Native young men who can do such work, and we trust
Government will now see its way to meet the wishes of both farmers and
Natives by employing some of the latter to detect, and so put down stock
thefts.

  The number of these thefts is not
  however so large as some imagine. Mr, TAMPLIN in his recent address at
  Alice stated that “ at the last Circuit Court at Umtata there were
  only seven criminal cases to be dealt with from among so many
  thousands of Native people.” One
  of the judges quite lately, after going on Circuit, said every crime
  he had dealt with at the Circuit Court was due to drink !

  Nevertheless, there are Native wrong-doers. All their crimes, from a
  monetary standpoint, are not equal to the results of the conduct of a
  few white bank directors or Company promoters, some of whom we expect
  to find C.M G.’s
  shortly. But the Native, being a retail “ conveyor,” as SHAKESPEARE
  has it, naturally secures punishment. “ Lot right be done ” is all we
  have to say. But we
  must not fail in this connection to thank Mr. HURNDALL of Zwaar
  Ruggens for his resolution ; or the Congress for endorsing it
  unanimously. The resolution is as follows :— As the absence of meat
  from rations of Native labourers is one cause of stock thefts,
  Congress would urge upon the Government and Divisional Councils the
  necessity of feeding their Native labourers with sufficient animal
  food, and make it imperative upon their contractors to adopt the same
  rule.”
  We, as of course, assume that the farmers will do what they urge the
  Government and the Divisional Councils to imperatively perforin. It
  has been suggested that every Judge and Magistrate trying a farm
  servant for theft should be bound to put to the prisoner these
  questions : What wages do you

  get? and, What food do you get ?
  For the legislation necessary for
  such interrogatories the country
  must wait. But for the present
  wo are content to express our accord with the declaration of the
  Farmers’ Congress that the
  ''absence of meat from the rations
  of Native labourers is one cause of
  stock thefts.” When opportunity
  offers we may return to the proceedings of the Congress, which are
  interesting and valuable.

EVERYBODY is naturally THE FARMERS angry and disgusted at the AND THE
horrible murder of Farmer Pienaar’s daughter OUTRAGE, near Alicedale;
and no one will regret if the per­petrator of the ghastly deed is
removed out of the way. It was to be expected, too, that the inhabitants
of the vicinity of the scene of the murder should assemble to express
sympathy with the family of the deceased ; but wo put it to our friends
whether it was right to inveigh against all black men, and to propose
vexatious laws for Natives generally, because of the heartless crime of
a double-dyed scound­rel. No one would object to any legisla­tion to
deal with the criminal class ; in­deed, if consulted, Natives would be
only too ready to assist in proposing such mea­sures as may appear to
them necessary to cope with evils of this kind. But it would result in
more harm than good to punish the law-abiding Native because of the
criminal, by the introduction of harsh laws, suitable enough for
application to rogues, but scarcely just for well-to-do order-loving
people, whether their colour be black, brown, red, or yellow.

THE adventurous heroism MISS FRANCIS of this kind hearted lady COLENSO.
is most astounding. It seems that her heart is burning with advocacy of
brotherhood and sisterhood of mankind irrespective of colour and
descent. We quote the following lines which have been sent to us for
publication : “ The Zulu Defence Committee aims at obtaining justice for
the Zulu chiefs and others who were charged before a court of special
Com­missioners sitting at Etshowe, in Zulu- land, while attempting to
subvert the authority of the Queen, and offering armed resistance to the
authorities, and upon whom the court passed heavy sentences, which, it
is considered, are undeserved, and, if persisted in, must undermine the
confidence of the whole Zulu people in British justice. It is considered
that the business of most immediate importance is to arouse public
interests in the subject, and especially to bring it before the
attention of Par­liament during the present session, in the hope of
obtaining justice for the unfortunate Zulus. Contributions to the fund

Committee is formed principally of M. P.’s., Sir R. N. Fowler Bart, M.P.
