The Cape Mercury states A that, near Keiskama Hoek, SHOOTING last
Saturday, a very res- CASE. pectable young German farmer, named Hessel,
shot a Fingo named Mtuyedwa. The headman reported the matter to the
A.R.M., who sent out the District Surgeon to attend the wounded man, and
had Hessel arrested. A preliminary exami­nation was commenced on Monday
morning, and the case remanded for fourteen days, when it is thought the
wounded man will be able to attend. The Natives allege that they were a
party of six of them passing close to the boundary of Hessel’s farm,
looking for a lost goat, when he (Hessel) came out with a rifle and
asked them what they were doing there with dogs, and fired—the ball
striking among them. They ran and he reloaded and fired again, shooting
Mtuyedwa through the calf of the right leg, and also wound­ing a dog.
The man fell and was carried home by his companions. Hessel is now out
on bail. The general impression seems to be that accused intended to
shoot the dogs.

  KAFFRARIAN politics have
  always offered a prize puzzle

to us, by reason of being invested
with an air of unreality. We had
therefore felt compelled to give up
both Kaffrarian politics and the
popular political leaders as a hope-
less job. The description “the
Colonel ” gave of himself and, no
doubt, his following, at their meet-
ing on. Saturday last furnishes the
key to the situation. Mr.—or, to
give him the courtesy title he
dearly loves — Colonel SCHERM-
BRUCKER was giving an account of
political parties in the House. Now,
we are well aware that, while the
Colonel is not taken seriously in
the country he still succeeds by
some magic influence to impress a
large body of Kaffrarian electors
who lay a claim to intelligence as
the veritable “ Great Diana of
the Ephesians.” But our own
estimate is that exactly placed upon
him by the country generally ; and
so are unable to attach any im-
portance whatsoever to the
Colonel’s utterances which we re-
gard as vapid and profitless, useful,
perhaps, in tickling the ears of the
groundlings and people who have a
hazy notion of politics; but, as for
containing any sound or saving
principle of political doctrine, being
absolutely worthless. But, as the
saying goes, even in a bushel of
chaff may be found a grain of
sense ; and we verily believe that
the Colonel has never in his politi-
cal ramblings stumbled over the
truth as he did on Saturday night
in the Town Hall of Kingstown
when he described himself, amidst
the plaudits of his supporters, as
one of “ the party of free lances”
in the politics of the land; Mr.
WARREN, his colleague, did not
disavow this appellation; and he
could not, seeing that “ he fully
“ agreed with his honourable friend
“ with regard to the state of politi-
“ cal affairs at the present moment,
“ and the effects of the late election
“ upon parties which existed in the
“ present Parliament.” Not to stul-
tify themselves the Kaffrarians who
returned the “party of free lances”
proceeded to vote themselves also
“ political free lances ” by voting
confidence in their representatives.
As belonging to the Kaft’rarian
electorate we of course regret that
the constituency, in the hands of
those who run its politics for the
time being, should have fallen in
evil days as to glory in its shame.
Free lances are political despera-
does, acceptable to no organised
party of men. They are men with-
out principles, or of easy political
morality, who may side with any
party for the nonce but cannot be
depended upon. They are thus
without influence on affairs. In a
word, they are the scum of politics.
You never can tell what a free lance
may be to morrow. He may be this
now ; that to-morrow ; and the other
thing the next day. Your free
lance is, in a word, “ everything by
“ turns and nothing long.” Thus,
then, we have seen the Colonel an
ardent Bondman at the Bond Con-
gress at Cradock at one time; a
virulent opponent of Mr. RHODES’S
at another; a docile supporter of
the same gentleman at the late

General Election; and now—well, w do not know what, perhaps, his own
description of himself—a “ freelance ”—suits better than anything we can
think of; and it is to this depth fair Kaffraria has fallen, led by the
blind leaders of the blind in regard to its political conscience. The
position is a most revolting one and let us hope the eyes of the more
moderate people in this constituency who have heretofore played the part
of camp-followers to the Colonel and his “freelance” bri­gade, will be
opened, and that the will identify themselves with those who are
labouring to purify the institutions of the country so that it may reap

HAY and the cause he represents; but it is a growing cause. The Colonel
him­self—the swashbuckler free lance that he is, ready to raise his

Colonel’s following at pre­sent, who would not like that their beloved
constituency should be known in the country as the nest of free lances.

Current Opinion.

THE NATIVE ACT AGITATION.

The Natives intend appealing to the Imperial authorities on the matter
of the Glen Grey Bill, which was forced through Parliament and received
the assent of the Acting Governor. The Natives claim that the Bill
legalises the alienation of lands pledged to Natives for all time by
Proclamation of Her Majesty the Queen. IMVO claims that the consent of
the Ad­ministrator has been improperly given, as the Bill interferes
with the treaty engagements of the Imperial Government with the Natives,
and that, therefore, the Administrator was bound by his instruc­tions to
refer the Bill to London, It is generally thought that the Glen Grey
Bill was rushed through Parliament with indecent haste. The Natives
appear to have made out a case for the considera­tion of Imperial
Government, and the result will be watched with the keenest interest.

The Christian Express, after acknow­ledging that it looked at the
mat­ter through rosy spectacles in at first judging the Glen Grey Bill
and Mr. Rhodes generously, proceeds to severely criticise the Bill as
finally passed. We have room to-day for its closing remarks,

The other thing is—What next? That very agitation which Mr. Rhodes
pro­fessed he was avoiding by passing the Bill. He knew it would be
otherwise. The first endeavour should be to get the Queen to refuse her
assent lo the Act. She may well do it as the protector and Paramount
Chief of the Natives, and they should all unite in both cabling and
peti­tioning to that end. But she may not do it, as this is a purely
internal matter. Another remedy remains. An Act of Parliament may be
amended. A temper­ate but persistent movement should immediately be set
on foot to get this Act amended next year, if possible, but at any rate
as soon as possible. The three great points we have noted should be the
ob­jects of attack.

The Cape Argus, under the title “ Native Rights,” writes:—

The best friends of the Natives will sincerely hope that the petition to
the Crown against the Glen Grey Act will not be persisted in. It is
difficult to say how much importance may be attached to the agitation
against the Act, which is being raised in the columns of IMVO. We must
perforce pay somee attention to the opinions expressed by our Native
contemporary, because it is the only channel of the kind which there is
in this country. Never­theless the conviction is forced upon us that
what appears in IMVO can only represent the opinions of a very small
knot of educated Natives, and possibly not their united opinion at ad
times. If all our hundreds of thousands of Natives, or even any
considerable portion of them, had attained to the civilisation of the
writer in IMVO it would be as important to pay heed to the opinions
expressed therein as to anything to be found in the Colonial press.
Between the nature’s gentleman who gives us in IMVO some of the best
newspaper articles to be found in this country, and the Natives whom we
have to bear in mind when legislating for the general good, there is a
gulf which can only be measured by centuries. It would be the last folly
of legislation to make laws for the few dozen highly educated Natives
whose views may be represented correctly enough in IMVO, and not for the
mass of the people whose interests have been conserved and guarded in
the Act, against which it is now scught to raise Native agitation. The
point now taken in IMVO is that the Glen Grey legislation is in
violation of the treaties affecting land settlements with Kama, the
Peddie Fingoes, Ngangelizwe, and many other Native tribes, Glen Grey
inclusive In all these treaties we are told there is a clause, following
the des­cription of boundaries, in which the following words occur:—“
Which terri­tory shall be held by the said chiefs and tribe, their heirs
and successors, in perpetuity, never to be reclaimed by, or on behalf
of, Her said Majesty, except in case of “ hostility committed, or a war
provoked.” As to some of the occupants of Glen Grey we might remind our
contemporary that there have certain­ly been acts of hostility, since it
is well known that some of the people who are now located in Glen Grey
were engaged in the Tembuland rebellion. That, how­ever, is a bygone.
Parliament has very wisely waived all questions as to how the people
came to Glen Grey and has dealt with all upon a broad, equitable basis.
The main point, however, to be borne in mind, is that the Glen Grey Act
is not a restrictive Act as to land, but is really designed to protect
the people in their holdings, and to put an end to the agitation for the
removal of the Glen Grey allottees, which it is well known has been
going on lor years. It would have been strictly in accordance with
treaty obligations if the Act had laid down all the provisions as to
allotment which are to be found there, and had said nothing whatever as
to alienation. The fact that certain restrictions as to aliena­tion
supported by the Opposition did not

ultimately find their way into the Act is no more a violation of the
treaty than if

there had been nothing in the Act as to ; alienation at all. Whatever
Mr. Rhodes chose to put into the Act in restriction of alienation—and we
know that for this i purpose he copied the mission titles already in
existence in the location—was as / it were much in favour of the
Natives, . added to whatever guarantees they may , have under the
treaties. It is a very curious charge to say that the treaties are being
violated because there were not still further restrictions introduced.
There are, however, other questions to be considered than the agreement
of the Act after a particular treaty, We suppose it will be admitted by
the champions of Native rights that whether the Act meets with their
full approval or not, it does afford a settlement of the question of
Native land occupation, The dangers to which that occupation is subject
by the inability of Natives to stand against the influences which we
will agree to call civilising, though some of them scarcely deserve the
name, are well known. It was felt upon all hands that unless some steps
were taken, Native occupation of land would be in danger, since the
dis­orders of an overcrowded location might at any time afford excuse
for doing away with the location altogether. A great effort has now been
made to cope with the whole group of difficulties that arise out of this
location question. We would put it to our Native contemporary whe­ther,
seeing that the people are to be secured in possession of their land, it
would not be better to give the measure a fair trial. Mr. Rhodes is
accused of put­ting the cart before the horse by getting his measure
passed before it had been fully discussed amongst the Natives
themselves; but is it not also putting the cart before the horse to
agitate against an Act before it has been experienced whe­ther it is
working for the benefit of the Natives or not? If the Natives were even
as ourselves, and able to enter fully into European political methods,
we might regard the agitation as only that robust expression of
political opinion which really marks a healthy state of things. But it
needs no laboured argu­ments to show that with the small excep­tion of a
Native editor and a very few others the Natives of this Colony are not
in a position in which the agitation now sought to be promoted can do
them the slightest good. If IMVO would read the signs of the times
aright, it may on the contrary do them a great harm which will not be
readily mended.

The East London Standard observes : —

None could have objected more earnest­ly than we did to that feature of
the Glen Grey Bill whereby it was left possible, under conceivable
circumstances, that allotments of land in such locations might pass into
the hands of white men, But we were compelled to recognise, also, that
other provisions of the law made this eventuality in many ways an
un­likely one, and that if, by reason of the occupants pledging their
lands against credit, it should, in the discretion of the Governor, at
any time seem right to permit the transfer of sections to white
creditors, they would in all human probability only be held by such
white men with a view of leasing them again to Native tenants. We should
gladly have seen even this rendered impossible; but there is nothing in
the Act as it stands to justify the exaggerated manner in which it is
spoken of by IMVO and some of its correspondents; and they would do more
towards obtaining a hearing for any reasonable objections they may have
to the measure if they avoided overdoing the thing. To speak of the Act
as IMVO does, as one “ not only avowedly but eminently calculated to
have them turned out of lands pledged to them in the Queen’s name; ” or,
as the Rev. Chas. Pamla does, to say that it “ drives them from their
homes, and de­prives them of land and liberty,” is simply egregious
misrepresentation, which cannot possibly result in benefit to those
whose cause it is sought thereby to advance.

The Kafrarian Watchman has written two successive articles on this great
question. Of these we have to-day space for the first:—

It is quite clear that violent efforts are being put forth from the IMVO
office to get up an agitation amongst the Natives with a view to
impeding the work initiated by the Glen Grey Bill of last Session,
having for its object a solution of the difficult problem of settling
Natives on lands occupied by them in their respective locations under a
system of individual title, We strongly suspect that this is in the main
a Party move intended to hamper the Ministry in the carrying out of what
is a very anxious experiment. But supposing that idea to be unfounded
there is no doubt that the IMVO exists under an overpowering impulse to
appeal to the suspicions of the Natives whenever any measure relating to
them is passed by the legislature of the Colony. And probably we ought
not to feel surprise at a racial bias which con­sorts with the stage of
development at which the Natives of this Colony have arrived. It is
however to be sencerely regretted that an organ which seeks to lead them
should never experience the smallest promptings to be generous in its
representations of Colonial Native Policy, It would be phenomenal to
find the IMVO rising above the level of dark uncompromising suspicion,
and so we find it reading into the Glen Grey Act of last Session, and
urging the Natives generally to do so, that this is not a measure for
settling them on the lands of their locations on a plan of individual
title and establishing a system of local self-government by the people
themselves in such locations, but that it is only _(a) thinly-veiled
authorization of a design to rob the Natives of lands occupied by them
by virtue of treaties entered into by the Crown with their fathers.
Seizing upon the word “ alienation ” in the statutes the IMVO at once
invites the people to accept the theory that here is the little break in
the fence by which white men are to creep in and rob them of their
ground. They are told that the absence of any mention in the Act of
treaties making over certain locations in perpetuity to certain tribes
is a proof of the dark conspiracy which is hatching against them. But if
there were the smallest disposition to be generous in the interpretation
of acts of the Legis­lature of this Colony the IMVO would have pointed
out that the Act in question is to be read in conjunction with the terms
of those treaties, inasmuch as it is not to be supposed that the
Parliament of the Colony would lend itself to an attempt to override
solemn obligations entered into by the Sovereign or that Her Majesty’s
Representative, if con­stitutionally so advised, would place the Crown
in such an invidious position And besides judged by the whole tenon r of
the Act, there is no justification for attributing to the Legislature
and the Governor this ignoble intention. We are sorry to see the Rev.
Charles Pamla identifying himself with this mischievous endeavour to
inflame the Native mind whose suspicions are so easily aroused’ Men of
bis class should be workers to­gether with the Government in its anxious
task of administration, instead of lending themselves to co-operate with
those who stir up dangerous agitation We ask where are the proofs that
the Colony is not to be trusted with an observance of treaty
obligations, on in its dealing with the tribe, which’ was the last to
come under jurisdiction, was there any lack of generosity ?
