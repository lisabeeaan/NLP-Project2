Ths Anglican organ—the POLYGAMISTS Southern Gross — returns AS VOTERS,
with vigour to the ques­tion of the disfranchise­ment of polygamists,
and goes so far as to state—what is wholly andabsolutely false— that Mr.
Charles Molteno has been re­turned by a solid polygamist vote. In this
our ecclesiastical contemporary is supported by the Bond which passed a
similar resolution at its last Congress. Apart from the objection that
the pro­posal is one involving mere tinkering with the franchise there
is the fact that these rabid zealous are after a will-’o the- wisp, as
no polygamists ever improve their tenements to the extent of the voting
qualifications. Nor do they care a dump about the franchise. But it is
satisfactory to find the Cape Argus speaking well and to the point on
this subject. Writing on the matter, our contemporary thus ob­serves
with truth and force:—“On the lower ground of expediency, we should say
that although polygamy does away with the ‘odd women’ of the western
system and the still more unfortunate class which is indirectly the
victim of others’ respectability it brings grave evils of its own. We
cannot, however, turn the franchise into a weapon for the punish­ment of
the Native polygamist. In the eye of the law the polygamist is simply
living with several women in a state of concubinage. A more effectual
step has been taken by the Law Courts recognising one wife only as
legal. Marriage has its roots in property and inheritance; and if the
Natives find that their property can descend only to legitimate children
they may gradually come into our way of living. It would have simplified
all such questions enormously if from the first we adopted the Natal
system, and provided some means by which Natives could ‘come out of’
Native law, after which they would have the franchise and trousers and
one wife and all the other blessings of civilisation.”

PARTY BUNGLING.

So much depends upon Party organisation in carrying out one’s views in
Parliament that we have looked forward with the deepest interest to the
development of the Farmers' Progressive Party. The Bond is a standing¹
illustration of this proposition. With a com­pact Party of little over a
third of the House of Assembly, and readily called together by the
loaders to discuss any question of moment that is to come before the
House, the Bond has exerted an influence in the country quite out of
propor­tion to its numbers. When, then, two years ago, the Farmers
decided at Cradock to take a leaf out of the Bond book, the more liberal
minded people in the country re­joiced at the prospect of a Party coming
into existence to counter balance the Bond. The disappoint ment and
disgust is equally great at seeing the Farmers’ Congress, after two
years, hopelessly wobbling over the matter : and it is a blow to such as
pinned their hopes to Captain BRABANT as a gentleman able to make up his
mind finally, and believed him courageous enough to follow his
convictions to their logical results, to find him also no better than
his weaker brethren. True, the subject has been put off until next year
; but some of us are very sceptical as to whether the Farmers will have
got rid of their timidity in the face of any Ministry that may for the
time be installed in office; and summoned sufficient courage to stick to
their convictions At present they seem to be in an awful plight between
their con­victions on the one hand and on the other the hereditary
tenden­cy to fall down in a swoon be­fore the Cabinet of the day for
favours. The whole thing is disheartening, and is discreditable to the
men whom the Cape Times has in sheer disgust called “ the Frontier
Wobblers.” From the lucid account given by Captain BRABANT of the past
of the Party, it was and is so much in sympathy with the Opposition in
its views and objects that it left no stone unturned to secure Mr. KOSE
INNES as its leader. The consideration whether Mr. SAUER, Mr. MERRIMAN
or anybody else should take the box seat is really a minor one when once
Mr. RCSE-INNES’S views which he shares with his ex colleagues are
accepted; and that they arc ac­cepted by the Farmers’ Party is clear
from Captain BRABANT’S own observations directed at Messrs. SAUER and
MERRIMAN when ho complained that “ they had appro­priated the whole of
his programme.” What is unexplicable to us is that the gallant Captain
should seem displeased at getting such powerful men as ex ministers to
help him to

fight for his programme! We can­not believe it is Captain BRABANT who
has taken up a position so in­consistent with what we have known him to
be, and w^(T)e can only console ourselves with the hope that he is
misreported. In the awkward dilemma in which they find them­selves the
Farmers are driven to the expedient of forming a Party Inde pendent of
the existing Parties. On what programme of principles, apart from that
already “ appro­priated ” by Mr. INNES and the Opposition, it is not
stated; but all the friends of Party Government will lament to see this
endeavour to render Kesponsible Government yet more unworkable by the
intro­duction of what has elsewhere been fatal to that system—the
division of the House into a litter of Parties. We do not give up the
hope that the Farmer members of Parlia­ment will think better of the
mat­ter, and continue to live in alliance with a Party formed on lines
which in the main they approve, so that a powerful front may be
presented to the common enemy—the Africander Bond.

DEAR MR. JABAVU,—I received your very outspoken letter of 29th this
morn­ing, and have just time—before starting on Circuit—for a few lines
in reply. You have certainly misunderstood me if you think I am going to
be another Colonel Griffith or Douglass in the House. What I look
forward to in the near future is the formation of a moderate party, and
if that can be found I shall certainly join it, if elected. My
dissatisfaction with the Opposition party, as at present working, is
that they seem to alienate the moderate non-Bond Dutch, who while in
their hearts agreeing with the Opposition cri­ticism of Bond proposals,
resent much of the language used and feeling evinced. Now, believing—as
I do—that the Bond can only be fought by such a Moderate Party, and that
the growth and organiza­tion of such a party, combining the best men of
both nationalities in the House and country, is the only remedy for the
chaos which now reigns, I do not wish to pledge myself to you to
join—straight away—men who may not wish to fall in with my views,
Perhaps at this distance off I do not understand things as clearly as I
should do in Cape Town, and if elected a single session might put me
straight on many matters; but at present I am sure that it will be best
for me to go in and see for myself. But you may be perfectly certain
that I should not long be the cypher in the House you venture to
predict.

Yours truly,
