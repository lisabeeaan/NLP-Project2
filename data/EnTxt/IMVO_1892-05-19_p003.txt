INSCRUTABLE.

  AFTER the Victoria East
  nomination on Friday
  last Mr. Joseph Wood,
  one of the candidates,

repaired to Sheshegu a stronghold of the
Native voters in the district. He re-
deemed the occasion by savage denun-
ciation of Imvo and its Editor. In answer
to the question as to what he had done for
the Natives during his previous Parlia-
mentary life, he mentioned that he had
fought their wars. The Natives retorted
that they also were in the thick of the
tight, and no special credit was due to
Mr. Wood in this respect. He then men-
tioned his support of education. But it
was pointed out that long before Mr.
Wood was in Parliament the arrange-
ments for the due education of the
community had been fixed. So that ho
could not claim this either. But there
were Natives in the meeting with long
memories who drew from the candidate
the confession that far from being their
friend in Parliament he was a supporter
of Mr. Sprigg’s “ Vigorous Native policy ”
of the disarmament of loyal Natives.
One old man thanked God ho had
spared him to see the man who while
they were still in battle took their
guns. In reference to a statement
by Mr. Wood that Natives follow the
Imvo like sheep, the candidate was asked
to name a single occasion Natives have
done so to their detriment. Mr. Wood
named the present occasion when they
were sure to prefer Mr. Hay because Imvo
supported him. Mr. Hay’s services to the
cause of those people were placed side by
side Mr. Wood’s by Mr. Tengo-Jabavu,
who was present, and the candidate asked
whether in the face of facts he would not
think Natives fools if they voted for him
and turned their backs on Mr. Hay. The
meeting camo to an upbrupt close.

THAT invariably at election time the bulk of the white popula­tion of
this country should be identified with the least capable man and a
candidate whose presence would probably not be felt in the House, is one
of those things which, as Lord DUNDREARY would say, “No fellah can
understand.” As a case in point take the contest now going on in
Victoria East and Ped­die. There three gentlemen — Messrs. BALLANTYNE,
WILLIAM HAY and JOSEPH WOOD—arc before the electors. It is fully,
frankly, and generally acknowledged throughout the country that, of the
three the candidate whose presence in Parliament is most to be desired
is Mr. WILLIAM HAY. This by reason of his great ability in mind, speech,
and the possession of vast stores of information on affairs ac quired in
the position he held as Editor of one of the leading organs of the
Colony Mr. WOOD has been in Parliament before, and for the number of
years he spent inside the Legislature he cannot be said to have achieved
a position above the mediocre. As to Mr. BALLANTYNE, his best friends
dare not lay their hands on their breast and say that he possesses one
single qualification for the post to which he aspires. He cannot speak,
so goes the saying; and his information on subjects of public interest
is exceedingly limited, and consequently of no value whatever. In these
circum­stances Mr. BALLANTYNE’S only re­commendation is said to consist
of the locality of his dwelling house ; and those who have been at
public gatherings know that this is a con sideration which never crops
up in the course of a discussion. But in the face of these patent facts,
strange to say, among the whites in Victoria East, Mr. HAY’S
candida­ture is poorly supported. He has been dubbed the candidate of
the Natives—why, we cannot tell—al­though it cannot be shown that he has
any designs to curtail, impair, or hamper in any way the develop­ment of
the white community. On the principle of “ give a dog a bad “ name and
hang him,” Mr. HAY, with all his parts, robust views, and the position
he has made for himself in the country is unaccept- able to the bulk of
the white electors of Victoria East. To his honour and credit, however,
be it said, he is adjudged the support of all the more intellectual and
educated classes among the Europeans in the constituency; while those

ORIGINAL POEM

To us a son is born,

We gladly blow the horn, And bid our friends with us to scorn

The day of hopes forlorn !

  On Sabbath night he came (10 4-92), This man without a name;

With shouts and yells he made his claim
And that with might and main.

  Some say his name is Bill, But this is doubtful still;

Some Native name, fresh from the mill, Would need no little skill.

And yet ’tis hard to tell

A name that will not sell, Or one that rings so like a bell

As that of William Tell!

  I must not keep you long, This is no Easter song;

Yet all good gifts to Him belong Who never will do wrong.
