I crave space in the national paper, Imvo Zabantsundu. I cannot compre-
hend the conduct of the Bacas in crossing the line daily and levying war
upon the Pondos, while the latter cannot go over to Bacaland. This
violation of the boundary is unjustifiable. I now appeal to the Native
public whether there would be any mistake if a Pondo crossed the border
to-day ? I put this question be- cause we have constantly protested
against this daily violation of the boundary by the Bacas. The boundary
between the Pondos and the Bacas was fixed at the request of the latter,
the Pondos did not wish for it. Government appeared on behalf of the
Bacas and asked the Pondos to fix the boundary. What are the Bacas doing
now? Again, where now is the Chief that interposed in their behalf? I
address you native races and you white people. Tell us what we, the
Pondo people, should now do. That is my request to you. Houses are
ablaze, people are being killed, and stock cap- tured. All that
Government are doing is the sending of messengers to us asking
permission to construct roads from River Mouths, reporting that the
Bacas and Xesibes are being armed to protect the party constructing the
road from the ocean. This is the version the messen- gers of Government
give of the arming of the Bacas. What do they mean by so saying ? Has
war been declared between the Government and the Pondos ? I am led to
ask this question by the message which Government messengers convey to
us. Is this a message of peace or of war? How is the altitude of the
Bacas and Xesibes to be accounted for? How are we to account for the
message of Govern- ment that if the Pondos cross the line they will be
declaring war against Gov- ernment? That is the message. “Let the Pondo
sit still or flee,” Government seems to say, “ While the Xesibe and the
Baca slay him. Failing to do this he will be guilty before Government.”
These are some of the things that puzzle men’s minds in Pondoland. Can
it be supposed for a moment that we are satis- fied with such
proceedings? The nego- tiations promise to be long; I shall give you
more information later on. Another thing: after the disturbances 25
horses and a lot of cattle have been stolen from the Pondos.

Abolition of the A BILL has been in-

MARAIS, member for Paarl, to do away with the Government tax on brandy.
The tax at present is two- pence per bottle. Its abolition will make
Cape Smoke cheaper by two- pence. From the tax Government was getting
over £70,000 a year. Last Friday Mr. MARAIS carried his Bill by a
majority of 37 to 31 : the Government being in the minority. It is now
stated that Mr. SPRIGG purposes to introduce another tax in lieu of the
tax on brandy. We doubt if the people of this country will tacitly agree
to

this. In most countries Govern- ment derives much money from an excise
tax. It is a good tax, for it tends to make spirits dearer, and the
dearer spirits are the better for the community in many ways. The effect
of the abolition of the excise is this—that people, especially natives,
will make themselves beasts with Cape Smoke. Gov- ernment should have
rather allowed Mr. MARAIS and his friends take their places and take the
consequences of their Bill than con- sent to help him by proposing other
taxation in lieu of the excise. We shall be surprised if the Ministers
are not cried down by all reasonable people in the country for meekly
accepting the very humble pie offered them by Mr. HOFMEYR and Mr.
MARAIS.

OUR friend the Port Elizabeth Telegraph- commenting on the documents
published in these columns bearing on the deposi- tion of Mabandla,
wisely says:—“ The Natives on the border are again agitating for a
reconsideration of the case of the deposed chief Mabandla. We have
already referred to his case. All the light that Christianity can throw
on the Native mind, all the good influences that educa- tion can impart
are at present, and for at least another generation will be inade- quate
to root out their veneration for the feudal system and their regard for
chief- tainship. This chief Mabandla was deposed by Mr. De Wet. On
further inquiry he was ordered to be reinstated. On representations from
a clerk in charge the order for re-instatement was cancelled and the
decree of final deposition was signed. The Natives are complaining of
this fast and loose way of the Government in its transactions with them.
Fifty-seven, of the petty chiefs, headmen, and others have now
petitioned Parliament that they may be informed as to the real ground of
the dismissal of their headman from the headmanship, in order that they
mav know what to avoid in future, and also if necessary that they may
know how and for what to ask the Government to pardon their headman.
Every criminal has a. right to a copy of the charge against him, and
Mabandla’s should not be an excep- tion to the rule.”

WE fear that our people are forgetting that the present is the season
for sowing wheat. From all we hear, and our own observation bears us
out, the Native people are too much taken up with the abundance of their
maize and millet crops, and are giving the wheat the go by. This is a
suicidal course, and we trust much energy will forthwith be shown in the
sowing of wheat.

PUBLIC indignation has been aroused throughout the country by the
Ministry electing to remain in office after the crushing and ignominious
defeats they have sustained in Parliament on the question of per centage
reduction of Civil Service salaries, and the two on the abo- lition of
the Excise, involving a loss of revenue to the tune of a trifle over
£70,000 per annum in these bad times. The country were thinking that ou
such a life and death matter Ministers would vacate office and leave Mr.
Hofmeyr the task of equalizing the revenue. It was, more- over, expected
that in case the Ministry resigned the Sprigg and Scanleu party would
have united to resist further tax- ation in other directions, and place
a tax of 3s. or 4s. per gallon on Spirits to show the Western farmers
that the consumer pays the Excise, and that their position is not one
whit better than when they have no Excise to pay.

To show that the Ministry have lost touch with their supporters we cull
the following from the local Watchman:— “We are reluctantly compelled to
state that in our opinion Mr. Upington and his colleagues should place
their resigna- tions in the hands of Her Majesty’s Repre- sentative and
advise him to send forthose who aim at being the virtual though retain-
ing the position of the irresponsible leaders of the majority in the
House of Assembly. The Ministry it seems to us can neither usefully nor
with anything like self respect retain a position in which they may be
dragged hither and thither against their own settled convictions to give
effect to the behests of other five men who will not come forward and
publicly accept the responsibility of putting their policy to the test.”

THE Queen's Town Free Press says:— “ the sweets of office must be very
dear to our Ministry if they can cling to their posts in the face of
three rebuffs from their very dear friends, the Dutch party. To be
outvoted on two such important matters as the Retrenchment Committee’s
power and the date of reading the Excise Tax Abolition would have been
enough for an ordinary Ministry to resign office, but to take, without a
murmur, defeat on the Excise tax itself, is sufficient to make the
country stand aghast at the attitude of our rulers. The usual honourable
course pursued by Governments seems totally foreign to the views of our
present Ministry; perhaps they are waiting for a motion of want of
confidence to complete their humiliation iu the eyes of the country.”
