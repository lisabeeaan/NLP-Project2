Native Opinion

THE season naturally opens up a sphere for reflection. When whole
civilised nations such as those of Europe and America intent on work and
making money as fast as they can, at this time agree with one accord to
hang up business anxieties for the season, and will not be drawn into
the vortex of trouble; when all, except among the ill- natured, is peace
and good will, it is impossible not to go back in thought and seek for
the primary cause of Christmas. There can be no question that, thousands
upon thousands among those availing ing themselves of this breathing
time know nothing, and care less, about the momentous event which gave
the world this joyous time. But be the attitude of peoples what it may,
in regard to Him to whom the birth of the custom is to be traced, it is
a source of pleasure to know that the great European kingdoms have as
such never wavered in their fidelity to the cause Christmas serves to
remind the thoughtful of. It is worthy of note also that the nations and
individuals who have identified themselves with the cause continue
always to be the better for doing so, so much so that it is a remarkable
fact of the day that the nations, as nations, who have acknowledged the
Christian standard of religion are now, without question, the lead­ing
kingdoms of the world, while those who have clung tenaciously to other,
and perhaps older, systems of worship continue to recede back­wards, and
many have already been entirely eclipsed by the brilliant achievements
of lands under the Christian influence. Such being the case, then, we
may be permitted, even apart from mere con­ventionalism, to wish those
who read these observations

Out and About.

In proceeding from the Border dur­ing the Kimberley Exhibition by
railway in the beginning of the month, the most noteworthy incident cn
route is the fearful six or seven hours the Border passenger has to
spend waiting at Middelhurg-road. The station is put up right in the
centre of a bleak wilder­ness of “ Karoo veldt,” to be kept in which,
for any length of time, is, to our mind, unlike the poet Pringle’s
experi­ence, excruciating in the extreme. It is freely asserted that
many people from the parts served by the Stormberg-Midel- burg junction
were deterred from visit­ing the Exhibition by this horror; certainly
the Gaurantors of the Exhibi­tion had little to thank the railway
authorities for if it he only for this blot in the management alone.

However, if report is to be believed, the various Traffic Managers have
been in solemn conclave assembled ; further the most important matter
which their combined wisdom was to sec righted was this monster of a
delay. It is satisfactory to hear the difficulty has been somehow
resolved, but, alas! too late for the Exhibition. The report goes that
some passengers of Hie daring kind, to avoid the agony, adopted the
route through the Bethulic by Spring- fontcin to meet their train again
at what may be described as the psychological moment at Naauwport
junction. The detour had the further merit of enabling the thirster
after knowledge the opportunity of knowing more of the _(c)ountry.

The present writer with another passenger hit upon a different solution
of the matter. Between the station and the village of Middelhurg, a
distance of six miles, run cheap carts, and for two florins the jaded
railway traveller can run into and from town, enjoy a few of the
luxuries of a settled community—in the shape (for the more privileged
colour) of a delightful bath, a hearty breakfast and a hasty stroll
under the heavy foliage of the pretty little village; and still be in
time for his train. O, what an oasis in the desert is Middelhurg to such
an one! The town, which is mainly Dutch, has the enevitahle tall Church
with its roof and spire peering above the thickly-planted trees close by
so interwoven with the associations of this class of the Colonist. What
one could not help remarking is the fact that most houses appeared to be
tenanted by Natives—principally Basutos, and such is the progress this
class has made that it dwells in substantially built brick-houses—the
beehive-shaped Kafir hut being entirely discarded by the coloured
classes here. Thus even in Middelhurg the writer was taught a thing or
two, in respect of the comfort­able accommodation Natives provide for
themselves.

There is not much worth while re­cording along the journey until one
reaches Kimberley; that is, if the stuntedshrub betokening that the
veldt is karoo, all along the expanse, bounded at a distance by hills,
and the big Orange River spanned by a railway bridge, arc assumed as
ordinary physi­cal features of this country.

Arriving a little past midnight in the land of diamonds the stranger not
used to mining work is met by an impres­sive spectacle of fires and
lights, some moving, others stationary at a respect­able distances. The
disciple of Mr. W. T. Stead Yvould at first thoughts imagine that within
purview are the veritable ghosts; but later on he is assured that what
he saw were but lights at mining centres—the work being done by day and
by night—Nature itself being thus defied by man. The fires were first to
he seen at Beaconsfield, but by and by the train brings the astonished
Kafir­lander, near those of De Beer’s when the brilliant electric light
disperses whatever fears one may have entertained about meeting
hobgoblins for once in one’s life.

To one seeing Kimberley for the first time the large town presents a
very busy appearance indeed. But those who have known it long and well
say it has had its best days. Of course, De Beer’s Company are made
responsible for the ills that have befallen the fields, espe­cially
Beaconsfield, which is un­doubtedly running to seed. On the other hand,
it has always been con­tended, with considerable force, that but for De
Beers—that is Mr. Rhodes—the mining industry would ere now have been
relegated into the category of things that were, and by another
course—and one the most downward—Kimberley would have gone to ruin. Our
far-seeing Premier is, however, credited with hold­ing the belief that
the place has seen its worst days; and from the bustle and activity one
saw, despite the doleful prnphecies, there exists every indication that
the Premier is right.

The most notable thing at this time in Kimberley was, of course, the
South African and International Exhibition. After the descriptions upon
descriptions that have been given of this stupendous affair in the
various newspapers of the Colony during the three months the Exhibition
was open, it would be super­erogatory to enter here on an attempt to
describe it over again. Hardly any­thing of importance was not
represented there ; and enormous, indeed, must have been the amount of
work and money ex­pended in getting up the innumerable ar­ticles
exhibited. The entertainments in connection with the event were
numer­ous, and good. To show the extent of the anxiety evinced to
satisfy the public taste a Band was introduced all the way from Vienna,
under Herr Josef Kopet- sky, and discoursed excellent music at stated
intervals during the magnificent, show. For the rest we may say ex pede
Herculem.

But by far the most impressive spectacle which the present writer had
the pleasure and privilege to enjoy was the grand work done by De Beers
Com­pany. The work had been described to us time and again; but a day
and a half’s look round and—shall we say below ?—convinced us that “ the
half was never told.” We had been re­peatedly assured that no black man
could ever be introduced into the De Beer’s works because he of that hue
could not be trusted that he would not steal the diamonds. This could
only have been on the supposition that the black man’s heart is as black
as his colour, while that of the wearer of the white colour was
necessarily as pure. The absurdity of this proposition has only to be
stated to be seen. To the courteous and kind Mayor of Kimber­ley, Mr. J.
Lawrence, and to the General Manager, Mr. Gardener Williams, we are
under a debt for arranging that we should look over the various
depart­ments of the great De Beers Company.

It had never been our privilege to be in Kimberley before, and this
enhanced the importance of the important things we saw. We shall never
forget the goodness and extreme generosity of Mr. F. Mandy, Manager of
that famous institution, the De Beers Compound, in taking us round not
only in and out of his own department, explaining matters, but also over
all that is of interest on the surface; and heartfelt thanks are due to
Mr. W. Nichol, the manager of the mine for showing us what is of special
interest in the works “ down below.” Certainly these two gentlemen
between them were instrumental in in­troducing us to the grandest and
most magnificent undertaking we could ever expect to witness, even if wc
lived during the next one hundred years. To us their kindness and
attention will ever be associated with it.

A close and searching study of the Native Compound system — its
management and working—decided us in its favour as about the best thing
that, in the mul- Itiplicity of interests involved, could be done.
Naturally where promiscuous Na­tives are employed hy thousands one would
expect to find such a place dirty

and unhealthy ; but the authorities of De Beers have successfully
grappled with this, and it was a delight to us to see the Natives kept
in a tolerably clean yard and quarters; and this cleanliness seems to
pervade everything but the wretched little place where the services are
held for such of the Natives as are religiously inclined. It would be a
credit to the heads and the hearts of the Directors if they showed by
building the Natives a worthy place for holding Divine Wor­ship in the
compound that they appre­ciated the principles of honesty and
rgiht-living inculcated by Christianity. The Hospital for Natives under
Dr. Otto is a model of order and tidiness and is of great value to those
for whom it is kept up. The number of patients is low at forty taken in
conjunction with theMhousands who are employed. The Company provides the
Natives with wood and they^huy their own supplies from the well
conducted shops on the place. There is a nice bath for Natives in the
compound, and the arrangements, on the whole, appeared satisfactory,
casual inquiries from the men them­selves confirming this.

It was very gratifying to gather from some of the heads of departments
that they had no reason to be dissatisfied , with the way the Natives
work. The “boy”—for that is the name given to the Native labourer in
these parts—from the Colony has made a reputation for himself as the
best worker, as compared with Natives from the interior and the East
Coast. The Kafir and the Fingo are strongly built with a fine physique;
while the interior man does not snow any one of these to advantage. It
was complained, however, that the Colonial Native was on an average not
to be trusted in the matter of stealing diamonds. The reason for this is
not far to seek ; for, as a rule, the “ boy ” who is engaged here is the
ne’er-do-weel at his home who goes to Kimberley to spend his life in “
notions living.” There has, as far as experience goes, as yet been no
attempt to properly obtain Native workers possessing some self-re­spect
to work here and this may account for the bad name “Colonials” have
received as a class.

We must confess that we had no idea of down-right hard systematic work
until we saw what was doing on De Beers. The work which is done among so
many machines of the heaviest description of necessity imposes on the
worker the greatest vigilance and agility of motion. It was a pleasing
sight to witness that our countrymen could be trained up to the pitch of
activity attained hy those engaged on De Beers; and we trust that habit
in this case also will lead to the formation of that character which
shall last even when they have left De Beers. We are happy to say in
work beset with so much danger alcholic drinks are rigorously kept out.
Hence the absence of frequent accidents. It was our good fortune to be
shown over the floors, the washing, the pulsator, and the sorting of
diamonds; and it may be mentioned that the work in this department is
done by convicts who appear to he well-fed and looked-after^ and do
their work with a Avill. From this a drive to the very pretty village at
Kennilworth built by the Company for their white employes, and hack hy
the iron factory of the Company which vies with such public works as the
railway works at Uitenhage closes the inspection of the surface workings
of this truly well-organised undertaking which has shed so much lustre
on Mr. Cecil Rhodes.

On the following day the writer and a Native clerical friend were taken
down below by the very obliging Mining Manager, Mr. W. Nichol, who
diligently explained the workings under ground. We went down to the
1000th level the lowest at the present moment; and the feelings that
surged within the breast on what was one’s first experience of life in
the bowels of the earth may be better imagined than described. The works
on this level are dry and some­what sultry, and the Natives employed
appear happy. It is a singular thing to find on inquiry from the
so-called boys that they are so much devoted to this kind of work that
they prefer it to working on the surface. Where the most important work
is done under­ground is at what is called “ the table,” where the cage
drawing up the diamond- iferous soil is taken up. It was a sight to look
at the men adjusting and re­adjusting the trucks on smooth surface with
remarkable quickness. Although accidents happen they are not so extant
as might be expected.

The men work perfectly free from bad treatment. It would appear work is
en­trusted to a number of overseers under whose charge the men are at
the various points. The check against brutal treat­ment lies in the fact
that the brutal master is soon deserted by his men, and the company
manager simply says to him that as he is unacceptable to the labourers
they have no further work for him. This seems a good check. Indeed, the
general treatment of subordinates by the company seemed to reflect great
credit on them ; and we could not help thinking on going away that they
are benefactors in various ways tu the country.

It so happens that at this time Kim­berley was on the throes of a
bye-elec­tion, which happily has been decided in favour of Mr. R.
Solomon, Q.C., by the largest majority ever known in this Colony. There
were attempts to set the town of Kimberley and De Beers by the ears; but
most providentially they failed. It was a matter of surprise to us that
a Company which was doing so much for the good of the Fields and of the
land, could have earned for itself such anathemas as were heaped on it
by the more ill-conditioned and cantanker­ous of the town; and just then
it was being urged as a matter of complaint that De Beers as a
Corporation had been so wise in their interest as to back Mr. Solomon’s
candidature. Mr. Solomon was denounced as the mere employe of De Beers,
and so forth ; but the convic­tion forced itself on us that on
consider­ation of a Redistribution of Seats Bill Government might do
worse than give to a Corporation so important in the land some means of
having its two or more representatives in the Parliament of the country
quite independent of the town of Kimberley.
