ESTABLISHED 1884. TENTH YEAR OF PUBLICATION

Topics of the Day.

This Picture and that.

Mr. Brabant (son of Captain Brabant, M.L.A.) has been appointed by Mr.
Rhodes Chief Commissioner of Natives in Mashonaland—at a fat salary no
doubt. By the way we notice that Capt. Brabant is the only one among
those usually regarded as more dependable politicians in the Colony who
has so far eulogised unreservedly Mr. Rhodes’s Glen Grey and Native Act,
which at first he says he was not clear about accepting.

The Truth about the Act-

It is very remarkable that Mr. T. P. Theron, M.L.A., a responsible Dutch
leader in the country, speaking to his constituents at Richmond the
other day, did not hesitate to give his opinion about the Glen Grey and
Native Act which is exactly that held by the Natives. He is reported to
have said that “ the land tenure of the Glen Grey Kafirs was as insecure
now as it had been secure before.” The Natives are thankful to Mr.
Theron for his robust frankness.

Re distribution of Official Business-

In certain quarters much is being made of what is ordinary routine
business of the Government departments, not affect­ing the country in
any way. The Attorney- General is to be relieved by some officer of a
part of the work that fell to him, while the appointment of Magistrates
is to be in his gift; the Colonial Secretary, who had it, taking over
from him the Prisons.

The dinner arranged by the members of the Cradock Farmers’ Association
to Mr. Cronwright-Schreiner, to-morrow, is a tribute to the honest and
just opinions held by that gentleman and honestly and fearlessly stated
at all times on matters relating to the well-being of the Colony, and
this country will never be what its best friends would wish it until it
is able to enumerate in its Legislature a consider­able number of men of
Mr. Cronwright- Schreiner’s robust convictions boldly avowed.

The Glen Grey Act in Fingoland.

A large meeting of Fingoes, numbering 700, took place at Ngqamakwe on
Satur­day last. A draft Proclamation, based on the Glen Grey Act, with
39 Clauses, was read. The land is untouched at present. It seeks to
introduce taxation—Hut Tax 10s, Council Tax 10s, Labour Tax 10s. All the
people said was that they only wanted power to collect, say, 5s. Tax for
Schools in lieu of Fees, and for Roads. They asked for time to consider
the Proclama­tion, which Major Elliot agreed to give. We understand that
the Fingoes are puzzled and generally dissatisfied. They see that their
Mission Schools are doomed by this Act. Let us hope that, inasmuch as
they have been consulted, they will not cloak their feelings, whatever a
domineer­ing Ministry may do.

A Commendable Proclamation-

The steps Government are taking in regard to the matter of liquor in the
Transkei are commendable—that is, so far as they go. Prohibition to
Natives has always been decreed there, but the way it has been carried
out has been a disgrace to the Government. A new Pro­clamation with
apparently workable re­strictions has been issued, as the result, no
doubt, of the short and lively discus­sion raised by Mr. Wm. Hay,
M.L.A., in the House last Session. All sorts of penal­ties are provided
for those attempting to break the law. Kafir beer is put down for the
first time as a liquor. Well and good, but the crux of the whole matter
is, as usual, the vigilance or otherwise of the Police. Will they do
their duty? We shall see.

SIR J. C. SPRIGG AND THE ACT.

SPEAKING at a public banquet at Capetown last week Sir JNO. G. SPKIGG
said enough on the subject of the agitation against the Glen Grey and
Native Act to show that the Government were somewhat affected by qualms
of conscience. Hence the “lengthy Minute ” to Her Majesty’s Govern­ment
in the Mother country, and “ the strongest possible repre­sentations
made,” as also the “ hope ” expressed that the Bill would not be
disallowed. But the TREASURER is a little too previous. All the Natives
want at present is the withholding, of Her Majesty’s assent, which does
not necessarily imply the disallowance of the Act, until Natives have
peti­tioned the Cape Parliament for the insertion of essential
safeguards as regards their land tenure. If they fail in this, then they
mean to carry their point to the Mother country; meantime Her Majesty’s
assent should not be given and thus estop the Sovereign from further
fairly considering the representations of her Native subjects, who have
not even a say in the Cape Legislature. In suspending action Her
Majesty’s Government would be doing no new thing. As far as we remember
Her Majesty’s Assent has never been signified to the Native
Disfranchise­ment Act of 1887 to this day. That measure had to run the
gauntlet of the three years with Her Majesty’s veto, like the sword of
DAMOCLES, hanging over it. The same course may very properly be pursued
in regard to the Native Act. Sir JNO. G. SFRIGG’S reference to the
sub­ject was as follows:—

His friend Mr. Graaff referred to the Glen Grey Act, which had excited a
good deal of attention throughout the country. The Glen Grey Act, as
they were aware, had, been proclaimed, and preliminary steps were now
being taken to carry out the survey of the lands in the Glen Grey
district, and the laws which were to pre­vail under the new Act were
about also to be proclaimed, not only in the Glen Grey district but also
in the district of the Transkei, notably in Fingoland. A meet­ing was to
be held at the end of the present week by the Chief Magistrate, to which
all the principal people were to be called. The Chief Magistrate had
been furnished with a copy of the laws which they pro­posed to introduce
there for the carrying out of the parts of the Glen Grey Act which was
to be applied to that large district, and he would be careful to explain
to the people the character of the new measures which were about to be
introduced. There was no doubt whatever that anyone ac­quainted with the
character of the Fingo people would readily understand that they were
people who took the deepest interest in the matter, and that they would
discuss the various details of the measure which was about to be
introduced amongst them, and he doubted not that under the careful
guidance and administration of that very able Chief Magistrate, Major
Ellliot—(hear, hear)—they would readily assent to the proposals
contained in the new measure, and would regard it as a measure
introduced for their advantage— (hear, hear)—which he had no doubt they
would find to be the result of a practical experience of it. They were
aware that many petitions had been presented against the Glen Grey Act,
and that petitions had been sent tc England advising Her Majesty to
disallow the Act, which she had, he would put it, a theoretical right to
do. Perhaps it would be more proper to say constitutional right, but he
would say that he had not the slightest idea that that right was going
to be exercised, because when the first petition was sent in they also
sent along with it a lengthy minute explaining fully the character of
the Act and what would be the alteration. They explained particularly
that it was in itself a beneficent measure, and that it was dis­tinctly
a measure for the advantage and well-being of the Natives themselves,
and they pointed out in the strongest manner the reasons why they had
advised His Ex­cellency to give his assent on behalf of Her Majesty to
that measure. They also expressed the hope that the petitions which had
been sent in would not find acceptance on the part of Her Majesty’s
advisers. There had not been time for them to hear the result of those
represen­tations, but for themselves they rested with full confidence
upon the beneficent character of the measure, and they could not for a
moment suppose that Her Ma­jesty would advise that a measure of that
importance, which was carried through the Legislature by such a large
majority, would be disallowed. (Hear, hear).

THE NATIVE LANDS AGITATION.

[The Kokstad Advertiser.]

A lot of drivel is still being written in Colonial papers about the Glen
Grey Bill. The proposal to send a deputation to England to appeal to the
Queen against the Bill has been harped upon, and no doubt a few sleek
Natives will be found anywhere to go through the Old Country complaining
of the severity of the measure, or anything else that is new to them. It
is to be hoped, however, that the Natives will not be so foolish as to
subscribe money to pay the expenses of a trip to England for a few of
their lazy countrymen. The Bill has now become law, and it is time
enough to shout about its defects alter it has had a fair trial, but
surely Colonists can be trusted to remedy whatever is wrong with it
better than the people in the Old Country. No Native who has the welfare
of the country at heart can regard the Bill as oppressive in any way.

[The Eastern Province Herald.}

Colonel Schermbrucker’s explanation
of the result of the Glen Grey Bill is to
say the least of it disingenuous. As to
the alienation of their land, the Natives,
he says, are in no worse a position now
than they were before, since Parliament
always had the right to alienate, The
Natives, however, are in a worse position
since Parliament has now delegated its
right to the Government of the day, and
any Native Minister who feels inclined,
may now alienate the land whenever the
opportunity arises, without any appeal
to Parliament.

GLEN GREY MISSION TITLES.

[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

SIR,—In the present discussion on the
Glen Grey question I find that on several
occasions the survey of the Wesleyan
Mission Stations in the Glen Grey District
has been brought forward in support of
the titles to be issued under the Glen Grey
Act. As I was the principal mover in
bringing about the survey of the five
principal Wesleyan Mission Stations in
Glen Grey, it is only right, I think, to
state the simple facts of the case. At that
time, as is well-known, the Bond party
was doing all in its power to get hold of
the Glen Grey lands. It was then that I
strongly urged the Tembus to secure their
land by obtaining individual titles. But
as a tribe they simply refused to take ray
advice, saying that they had done nothing
to cause the Government to take away
their lands and they preferred to remain
as they were rather than accept survey of
any kind. The Station people, however,
and some others of the more advanced
and civilized of the Natives, were very
anxious to secure individual titles. It
was then we decided to apply to Govern-
ment for the survey of the principal Mis-
sion Stations in Glen Grey.

The people themselves urged the neces-
sity of obtaining restricted titles, so that
the land could not pass out of their hands.
But I told them that this could not be
done without a special Act, and if the
question once came before Parliament
there was no knowing what would hap-
pen, and I feared they would not get
what they desired. It was only then that
they decided to take the titles offered to
them by Government, and which they
now hold—not because it was their choice,
but because they feared they would get
nothing better, and probably by waiting
lose their lands altogether, as some had
already done. It must be remembered,
too, that there is no comparison whatever
between’the liberal survey of the Mount
Arthur and other Stations, and the pro-
posed survey under the Glen Grey Act.
And I am fully convinced that if the
people of these Mission Stations had been
offered the survey now proposed they
would without the slightest hesitation
have refused to accept it, and preferred
remaining as they were. Under these cir-
cumstances it is therefore unfair to quote
Mount Arthur and other Stations to sup-
port in any way the Glen Grey Act-
more especially as the said Act is no
longer for Glen Grey alone (which was
then a disputed question), but for the
whole of the Native population.

In fairness to myself and others I con-
sider it necessary to make the above
explanation.

E. J. WARNER,
