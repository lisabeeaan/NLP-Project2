The Magistrate of King- A BRUTAL Williamstown had before ASSAULT. him on
Tuesday one of those cases, which, wo are sorry to observe are becoming
common on this Frontier, of brutal assaults on Natives by white men. A
well-to-do Native from the Transkei came across one E. H. Salzwedel, was
struck a blow on the face, tied to a tree, kicked while blackmail was
being extracted, a promise being exacted that the Native should hush it
up, and for this savage act accused was sentenced to pay £4. This case
decided Salzwedel brings a charge against the Native, which, viewed in
its true light, is to punish him for bringing him to justice, for
damaging the road and the man is fined £1. To our mind this seems hardly
the thing. The magnitude of the case, we should have thought, demanded a
preliminary exa­mination. It is high time Government made up its mind to
deal severely with such crimes on the person, for violence begets
violence.

CONSOLIDATION OF PARTIES.

TO those who are really anxious .

to see the Cape reap the highest good from its institutions the news of
the appointment of Dr. TE WATER, Mr. DU TOIT, Mr. VENTER and Dr. HARRIS
as Gov­ernment Whips will be most wel­come That the number of
Gov­ernment Whips should be four— exceeding even the number of this
useful body of men, deemed necessary for party purposes at
Westminster—can only be taken as mean­ing that the heterogeneous
elements who keep the Ministry in office, if not in power, must require
a great deal of handling and nursing. It is sig­nificant that three—Dr.
TE WATER, Mr. DU TOIT and Mr. VENTER—out of the four Whips are Bond­men.
The step thus taken, how­ever, indicates that our public men are only
now beginning to under­stand what Responsible Govern­ment is ; for even
as comparatively recently as the General Election a solemn asseveration
was made by no less a politician than Sir GORDON SPRIGG, a gentleman who
was in the thick of the fight for the system, who should know what it
was he was advocating if nobody else did, that Responsible Government
did not necessarily imply government by party. The open election of
party Whips by, and on behalf of a Government of which Sir GORDON is a
member, argues either that that gentleman has changed his mind since the
elections or those with whom he is associated do not share his views on
this question which is of the very essence of Responsible Government. We
say it is of the essence of Responsible Government. That in this view we
are far from being singular may be cited the recent utterances of Sir
WILLIAM HARCOURT in the House of Commons on this most interesting
subject. THE CHANCELLOR of the EXCHE­QUER, in stating the case of the
Government as to why it should have more time for its business the other
day, had reason to dilate on this question of party. The right
honourable gentleman, in the course of his remarks, observed :—“ Party “
government in this country is of “ the essence of Parliamentary “
institutions and of the action of “ the House of Commons. I have “
always believed that the House “ of Commons could not exist “ except
through party government, “ and I have never joined those who see in it
anything to condemn] “ It is a condition of party govern- “ ment that
each party in turn “ must have certain measures “ naturally representing
the senti- “ ments and convictions of the “ majority for the time being,
and “ it is absolutely essential that the “ Government should bring
forward “ great controversial measures “ which they strongly support and
“ which those opposed to them aUo “ strongly resist.” Now, our Cape
Parliamentary institutions are framed after the pattern of the House of
Commons; and it is therefore not amiss to recommend Sir WILLIAM
HARCOURT’S observa­tions, which, by the way, were not disputed by other
authorities in the House, to the attention of our own legislators. There
have been those

who have vehemently maintained that there were no marked party lines in
the politics of the Cape. The PRIME MINISTER has again and again
endeavoured to emphasise this in some of his speech, although in
fulfilment he has thrown his whole influence to keep out of Parliament
politicians like Mr. RICHARD SOLOMON and Mr. PATON on party grounds. But
the effort to thoroughly consolidate the Government party by the holding
of a party meeting at which Whips were appointed, following on the wake
of the Opposition meeting which elected its Leader and most probably its
Whips also—these movements, we say, must for ever get rid of the easy,
comfortable assumption of invertebrate politicians that there were no
parties, and that therefore they are justified in their contemptible
course of following, regardless of convictions, whosoever may be the
bottle- holder jfor the time being. The complete organisation of parties
being effected, we may now expect each party to hoist up its party
shibboleths. The Ministry have so far introduced measures on which in
the main all parties are agreed; but it is, as Sir WILLIAM HARCOURT
points out, incumbent on them to bring for­ward other legislation in
which they, as a party, believe, which their opponents may resist. It is
doubt­ful whether the present Ministerial party have any such measures
on which they are agreed among themselves; but this should not prevent
the Opposition from bring­ing forward those proposals, embodied in the
Capetown “Ticket of Four” to emphasise their particular sentiments. This
they can do by bringing forward resolu­tions to test the feeling of the
House, not, perhaps, because they can carry them, but to let the country
know their raison d'etre.

The Section in the Medical Act which prohibits all medical treatment
except by a regularly licensed European doctor is harsh and cruel in its
action on the Native people, and was enacted without consideration of
their case, though they form half the population of the Colony,
numbering as they do three-quarters of a million. A case or two will
show how the Act is cruel in its application to them.

A Native near A, sent to his master entreating him to come and see him,
as he was very ill, The master went and found him in a dreadful state
with diph­theria, and his boys also were ill. Ashe could do nothing for
them himself, he sent to fetch a doctor, who gave them a bottle of
medicine, A Native doctor also came and treated them. It is not known
whether they took the European doctor’s medicine or not, but the boys
recovered of the disease and the old man died. What are Natives to do,
who have no humane masters? They cannot call a European doctor, whose
fees, especially for travelling, are ruinous to a poor man, When they
are down with disease, they must either get help from a Native
specialist or die. But if the Native doctor who comes to their help is
inform­ed upon, he is either heavily fined or has to go to jail.

A case was also stated to the Labour Commission by a Dutch farmer. It
will

probably not appear in their Minutes. He went from home, and on his
return found a faithful servant of bis in one mass of foul mcers from a
malignant disease. He sent for a doctor, who saw the man, and said to
the farmer his case was beyond redemption; he had better dig his grave.
The man, however, asked permission to go into Kafirland, and consult a
Native doctor there. He got leave and went. This doctor said to him,
Your case is a very bad one, but I have cured a worse, only yours will
take time. He then treated the man externally and internally, and after
six months he re­turned to the farmer, who was astonished to see him
perfectly cured. But, said the Kafir servant, will you give me two
beasts to pay the doctor with. By all means, said the farmer, and he did
so.

No inquitision was made by the Labour Commission for the Kafir doctor;
but another case will show what might have happened. A Native doctor was
brought up before the R. M. under the Medical Act.

R.M. You cured this man?

Answer. Yes.

R.M. You received so and so.

A. Yes.

R.M. (Very reluctantly, condemning the Act he must enforce), Well, I
must fine you two pounds, or you’ll have to go to prison.

The money was paid. It is very often subscribed by the Natives, whom the
Act is supposed to protect!! Curiously enough, it is al ways, Did you
cure? for with Native doctors it is—No cure, no pay. The harshness of
the Medical Act needs no comment. It cuts the Natives off from their own
source of help, and gives them no substitute.

The Natives cannot get doctors, cannot in most cases pay for proper
attendance, may be desolated with fever or some other epidemic, and
those who would help them are in terror of the law. In many diseases
they have no confidence in European doctors. The Native doctors are
specialists. They spend a lifetime in the study and care of one disease,
some­times of two or three, and they become known and trusted by the
people.

Why should not the Act be altered to leave them and the Natives to
settle matters in their own fashion. There are Native quacks, as well as
European, but the Act does not reach them, as they have no repute and
are not caught, except when they do irreparable mischief, and then they
are deservedly punished as criminals.

There is a difficulty to meet in altering the Act, with regard to
unlicensed Euro­pean empirics. It would perhaps meet their case, to
punish all such who set up a druggist’s shop and make up pre­scriptions,
or who advertise. Quacks cannot get on without selling drugs or
advertising.

It is not the Idea that this toleration of Kafir specialists should go
on always. This system will be removed gradually. The first step will be
to have teaching Medical missionaries to train a body of Native young
men in medicine and surgery. That has been carried on with great success
in India, China, at Nazareth and numerous other places. The next thing
would be a Native Medical School, but in the meantime modify the Medical
Act to relieve the Native people for the present,

ABOUT PARLIAMENT.

MAY 21.—Pondoland is discussed in the Lower House, and we glean the
fact, elicited by Mr. Sauer, that it was not so much the so-called
barbarities as Natal pressure which brought about the annexation. The
Opposition, through their Leader, assert Government could not have
adopted any other course.

MAY 22.—The election of the Chair­man of Committees brings out the
strength of parties, which exactly cor­responds with the figures we put
for­ward at the close of the elections. We then estimated the strength
of Parties as follows:—Opposition, 29; Bond, 28; Wobblers, 18. The
voting for the Chair­man gives the Opposition 27 for Sir T. Scanlen;
while the coalition of the Bond and the Wobblers casts 42 votes for Mr.
Theron. The Opposition are, therefore, not a minority to be despised.

Dr. Berry gets the papers giving authority to the Magistrate to stock
Glen Grey with 1,300 people, after it had been reported by Commission
and by Minister of Agriculture the settle­ment was overcrowded and, what
is more, promises a warm debate on the subject.

MAY 23.—Mr. W. Hay, sarcastically showing up the defects in the proposed
machinery of that most necessary mea­sure of the Registration of Births
and Deaths, unearths the fact how the father of an illegitimate child is
the only per­son who is to have easy times of it. His name is not to be
divulged. This is legislating in the interests of impurity with a
vengeance.

MAY 24.—This being Queen’s Birthday the Houses take a holiday.

MAY 25.—The Payment of Jurors and Official Stamps engage the afternoon.
Mr. Schreiner seizing the opportunity to regain the confidence of the
House lost last Session, by a learned disquisi­tion on the abolition of
trial by jury, a subject hardly germane to the question before the
House.

More about the interesting topic of the seats members choose for
themselves e ther on the Government or on the Opposition side. As was
said last week they may not definitely fix the views of the member but
they indicate their direction. Mr. Orpen has shifted from the
Opposition, and sits immediately behind Sir Gordon Sprigg and alongside
Dr. Smartt. Dr. de Water has chosen his place between Mr. Hofmeyr and
Sir James Sivewright, and then comes Dr. Vanes and Mr. Abrahamson, on
the back bench on the Government side. The curiosity we expressed last
week is settled. Dr. Berry is on the Opposition side, while Mr. Vintcent
now occupies a seat lower down the Opposition benches, opposite that
which he formerly held on the Government benches.

On the whole the proceedings of the week show the Opposition to be in
high feather.
