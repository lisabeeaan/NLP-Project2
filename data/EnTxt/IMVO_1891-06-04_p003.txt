OUR friends of the A-borigines’

Protection Society shall very soon have their work cut out for them in
South Africa—at all events , in the Afrikanderised portions of South
Africa ; as it would be , grossly unfair to include the purely English
Colony of Natal in the category of States dominated by Afrikander
influence. Ever since the results of the Census were made known, the
atmosphere has been saturated with suggestions from leading statesmen,
relating to the extermination of the African race in South Africa. The
Hon. J. II. HOFMEYR, the great Afrikander Leader, hinted, in the course
of a speech at the Bond Banquet in Kimberley, at the wisdom of a policy
of doing away with the Na­tives of this country by means of the
deleterious drink popularly known as Cape Smoke ; and a mem­ber of the
Government, the Hon. JOHN X. MERRIMAN, has declared his adhesion to a
policy that should operate in the direction of getting rid of the black
man, leaving this a white man’s land. We have been used to regard the
Zuid Afrikaan in the Press as the sole and harmless legatee and
repository of such senti­ments, and we own we were not pre­pared to find
them advocated in the columns of the Star (Johannesburg) with a brutal
candour for which we should never have given it discredit. The remarks
of the Star are made in connection with comments on Mr. MERRIMAN’S
recent Lecture on Africa. Wo have taken the op­portunity to italicise
portions of the quotations from the Star article, which seems to claim
attention. We read:

Mr. Merriman either lacks that saving brutality of disposition which is
indispens able to the statesman, or the frank cynicism which would have
led him to boldly state ,that the question on which our politicians for
the next few generations will have to occupy themselves, is, how to
preserve South Africa to the white man, or more plainly, how with the
maxim, mum of expedition, and the minimum of inhumanity, they shall get
rid of the black.

Further on in the same article it is said:—

By force of some obscure social law the existence of which Mr. Merriman
frankly acknowledged, the African black is able, so far as has hitherto
been seen, to accommo­date himself to any known social environ, ment.
The case of Hayti, which he (Mr. Merriman) quoted is perhaps the most
significant. When the Spaniards occupied the island, they exterminated
the native population with the characteristic celerity which is one of
the few claims they possess to the grateful remembrance of prosterity.
The normal increase of the black [in South Africa] as compared to the
white population is as about two to one, and the plain fact stares us in
the face that either artificial checks will have to be resorted to, or
civilisation in its highest aspects will become an impossibility in
South Africa.

The “ artificial checks ” to be resorted to are explained in the
following extract: —

It is superfluous to insist that antagonism between white and black is
inevitable. Sir Thomas Upington’s illustration of the two men clinging
to a plank, was in a sense unlucky, but it did not exaggerate. If the
Caucasian be not played out, it of course goes without saying that the
ultimate victory will be his; but it is no less certain that his method
of warfare will have to change. He will have to rid himself of some of
his humanitarian sentiments, and he will be forced to recognize frankly
that the duel is between his wits and intelligence, and the blind forces
of Nature, and must be fought out, not in kid gloves, but a outrance. So
far, he has chivalrously guarded the black against influences which were
thought to be adverse to his well being. In South Africa, legislation ae
a general rule has been directed towards prohibition laws, ignoring the
plain teaching of experience that in alcohol is to be found the only in.
fluence which may be trusted to sap the fund of seemingly infinite
vitality, which will overcome civilisation if civilisation does not
overcome it. It is perfectly easy to denounce such a suggestion as
cynical, but we are reaching the point at which the truth cannot be
concealed much longer, and Mr. Merriman, though an expert in euphemisms,
possesses sufficient penetration to perceive it. Today concealment may,
in the case of our governing men, be wisdom ; very shortly it will be
universally recognised as the sheerest and blindest folly.

Of course all this is tantamount to saying that our countrymen have no
right to live and breathe GOD’S air. Fortunately for the Natives there
are
bers of Parliament; Advocates, Attor­neys, J.P.’s, Agents-at-Courts;

VETERAN MISSIONARIES FROM papers out by the last English mail we gather
that Rev. John Walton, M.A., who held so prominent a position among
Wesleyan Missions in this land not many years back, has been compelled
through ill- health to intimate his intention to resign his position as
one of the Foreign Mis­sionary Secretaries in London. He has not long
held this post of honour in his Church, but such have been its anxities
that they have told on his con­stitution. We but express the wishes of
those who know him out here that rest may restore him to his wonted
vigour, so that his matured counsel and eperience may still be at the
service of the church for which he has spent himself.— We observe also
that Rev. Dr. Bryce Ross of Pirie has reached Scotland safely, and are
glad to learn that reports go far to show that he is a little better
than he was when he left these shores.

AGRICULTURAL MINISTRY THE intimation in the THE Governor’s speech that a
Bill for the creation of a. Minister of Agriculture and Crown Lands
would be introduced this session has been favourably received by the
country. The P. E. Telegraph alone has so far taken exception to it on
the ground of expense; but an economically minded Ministry like the
present can be depended upon to have viewed the matter from all sides,
from that of expense as well, and after a careful sifting of the facts
of the case assured themselves of the compensating good to be derived
from the creation of the office. Just now interest centres in the future
appointment. Whether it will be made from the Bond wing of the
Ministerialists or from Mr. Sauer’s fol­lowing. If it is to be from the
Bond wing, the choice of men of Ministerial rank being limited,
speculation points to Mr. Hofmeyr himself entering the Ministry as the
first Minister of Agricul­ture, and to his appointment no possible
objection can be made. In case Mr. Hofmeyr should decline, except Mr.
Marais, there is no one available if Mr. Neethling’s expressed readiness
to take it is to be laughed out as in the Bond. For Mr. Theron has
scarcely been able to conceal his antipathy to the change of Ministry
made last Session. If however the appointment is to be made from Mr.
Sauer’s party, then, the difficulty will be as to who to select, there
being such a number of eligible men. Among farmers Mr. Douglass and
Captain Bra­bant may be mentioned ; while of mer­chants whose extended
reading and experience may be utilised in devising plans for the
successful working of the Department, such gentlemen as Mr. L. A.
Vintcent, Sir Thomas Scanlen, Mr. Wiener or Mr. C. T. Jones would, each
in his own sphere, add to the strength of the Ministry. So much for
speculation. It is not to be expected, however, that no attempt will be
made in certain quarters of the House to get one of the present
Ministerial Offices abolished; viz., the Office of Secretary for Native
Affairs, to give way to the new creation. For our­selves we should not
regret it, as Native affairs would then come under the guidance of a
strong mind, possibly the Premier himself; and Natives and all concerned
would reap considerable ad­vantage from the change, as their in­terests
would not, as at present, be con­fided to the admittedly least capable
among Ministers, who naturally becomes clay in the hands of an Under
Secretary who may be more devoted to tricks in governing the people,
instead of studying the best means of ameliorating the con­dition bf the
governed, and rendering them happy and contented.

FOR its first week the PARLIAMENT. Cape Parliament has a record of solid
work to show. With a business-like Ministry to lead it this was quite to
be expected. The very fact of the summoning of the Legislature for
Tuesday of all days of the week, instead of Friday the favourite time of
the previous Ministers, has been read by the initiated as an intimation
that as far as the Rhodes Government was concerned the time of the puHic
would be economised. Tuesday was taken up with the essential
preliminaries such as the placing of Notices of Motion on the Paper, the
laying of Blue Books and other Parliamentary Papers. Mr. Laing managed
to secure from the Colo­nial Secretary a promise that as far as
practicable such returns and Blue Books as were completed before the
assembling of Parliament would be posted to mem­bers—we trust the Press
would not be overlooked—to be studied at greater leisure. On the second
day of the Ses­sion, Wednesday, the Notices of Motion again poured in.
Among the more in­teresting being one from Mr. Tamplin who, for his
first session, seems bent upon verifying the proverb about the new
broom. Mr. Tamplin wishes to see Native Marriages registered, and the
recommendations of the Native Laws Commission carried out to secure
recog­nition after a period of years of one wife only ; and he will ask
the Secretary for Native Affairs whether he will act in this matter. The
question is in the right direction. The other notice of special interest
is put by Mr. Sehermbrucker re the expenses of the Bechuanaland and
Pondoland Commissions. The object is to show how corrupt the Ministry is
in appointing its followers in Parliament on Commissions, but it will be
retorted that Government could not ask those who did not see eye to eye
with them as agents to deal with such matters. Be­sides it does not lie
in the mouth of a member of the Government that sent Mr. Frost as
Commissioner to Glen Grey, appointed Mr. Theron on the Prisons
Commission, to inveigh against others for following precisely a similar
course. Several Bills were read the first time, viz., the Banking Bill,
the Pound Laws, Dairies Bill, Convict Stations, Ballot, Life Assurance.
Sympathy with the Queen of Holland was voted on the Premier’s motion. On
Thursday Standing Com­mittees were appointed. Mr. du Plessis, Martinus
of that ilk, shows what interest he takes in the Lands of the Natives of
Kamastone by a question as to the leasing of derelict lands and the
paying of arrear quit-rent. The Convicts Bill passes the second reading.
We bring the review of the 1st week’s proceedings to Friday (29 May),
when, besides the receiving of Notices, not of any consequence to our
readers, Ministers were engaged in answering questions on such subjects
as the Wesselton Mine, the Registration of Brands, Basutoland Customs,
Sea fishing. Mr. Innes brings in a Bill for the better repression of
stock-thefts, which though short, may yet rank among the best Acts ' of
tlie year. Buyers are to satisfy them­selves that stock hawked round for
sale is not stolen, and thieves may be fined as well as imprisoned for
theft. An amendment legalising the compensation , of the party from whom
stock has been ; stolen would complete the measure.

Meetings have been held at various centres of the agricultural districts in favour of the step, with the result that a deputation has been sent to His Excellency the Governor of Natal and of Zululand. The Governor’s reply shows a becoming solicitude for the rights of the Natives who have come under Her Majesty’s rule; and Sir Charles Mitchell frankly avows that the first care of Her Majesty’s Government is the safe-guarding of these interests. Such a declaration might well have been expected from His Excellency who, in the government of Zululand, is directly responsible to a humane and generous government that studies the happiness of the governed before the insatiable claims of the land sharks. If every piece of land, already hemmed in by Europeans, is to be disposed of after the wishes of the Natal farmers, who are hankering after Zululand, then the millions of our people must soon be without a footing anywhere in their own fatherland,
