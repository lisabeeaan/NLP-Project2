I LAWN TENNIS.

NATIVE OPINION

OUR last impression had already
gone to press when the news of Mr. ROGERS’ withdrawal from the contest
for a seat in the Legislative Council reached us by wire. There was thus
no opportunity either to retract or otherwise qualify the terms of
disapprobation we applied to what seemed a rash determination on the
part of that gentleman to risk the prospects of the return of two
gentlemen of his way of thinking, who had a fair chance,
because they had more general support, of being returned, if their other
supporters were allowed to concentrate their strength on two. But we may
mention that our comments were in good faith, based as they were on the
very best authority, to the effect that Mr.
ROGERS was prepared to do his worst to baulk the party of the two seats.
However, “ all’s well that

ends well.” It is with the utmost alacrity, therefore, that we wholly
and unreservedly withdraw the ob­servations made in our last issue
touching Mr. ROGERS’ public conduct in this respect. We are convinced
Mr. ROGERS has done himself no end of good by this graceful act of
self-sacrifice in the interests of his friends and of the cause. He has
enhanced his prospects of future election very considerably, not only
with his Native friends, but also with those who, in this contest, have
espoused the candidature of Messrs. PEACOCK and BRADFIELD. We be­lieve
it was in his power so com­pletely to paralyse the Natives as to render
them unable to put in two men, well-affected towards them, whose
prospects of being returned would otherwise, but for his timely action,
have been slender indeed. The hearts of the people have been drawn more
towards him ; and we are sure they will repay, with in­terest, the debt
of gratitude under which he has laid them.

the Circle. Quid nunes may therefore be met with at every corner, if not
in every street, inquiring after the state of the poll; and certainly
the poll for the Eastern Province has been as curious as it has been
keen, at all events in so far as the lower districts are concerned. Up

Without some explanation, these figures may mislead superficial
observers. Hence a word or two on them may not be out of place. It would
be as well to state that these numbers relate to 62 out of 167
polling stations in the Circle ; but it would be misleading to imply
that from the places from which no returns have been received there are
as many votes as appear in the places which have produced the tabulated
results. Although the number of voters in the unknown centres may not be
as large as represented by the published results, yet it must not be
forgotten that these places will follow exactly the lead of those we
know. For instance, the talk now is that Mr BAILEY has polled his
strongholds
—the towns, the lower districts, and the C.M.R. On the other hand, there
are places like Glen Grey, portions of Tembuland, as also Ox Kraal,
Mount Fletcher, and sub-districts of the Transkei, with very heavy
votes, from
which no information has been received. These places are as much the
strongholds of Messrs. BRADFIELD, PEACOCK, and DE WET as
Kingwilliamstown and East London may be said to have been Mr
BAILEY’S. In Tembuland 356 voters of Dutch extraction, or 1,068 votes,
have not been heard of. New England, in Aliwal, has a solid Dutch vote
of 405 ; and 474 Bondmen—presuming every Boer to be one—have yet to be
reckoned
with in the Wodehouse country stations. It will thus be seen that Mr. DE
WET has 2,583 votes at his sole disposal, of which we have not heard. If
1,500 of these have been polled for him, then his total must
be 3,233, and he does not even come up to Mr. PEACOCK. We are not, of
course, in a position to say how far the Bond split has gone. It
certainly seems to be a genuine thing in the Queenstown district, where
Mr. VAN HEERDEN and Mr. BAILEY appear to be dividing what otherwise
might be Mr. DE WET’S.

In Wodehouse we see that some Bondmen, sick of the yoke, have kicked
over the traces, and plumped for Mr. BRADFIELD. Many appear to have made
it a point to give him one vote. If this has been generally followed,
then Mr. DE WET will be rendered impotent, and Mr.
PEACOCK and Mr. BRADFIELD will come in. We calculate that Mr. PEACOCK
has secured about 800 from places unaccounted for in Tembuland, which
will bring him up to a position near Mr. BAILEY, who may possibly get a
few more votes—but not many—from Dutch farmers; but it is clear by
getting these
votes Mr. DE WET’S chances have been minimised. With 3,314 more votes,
judging by the way his supporters in the upper parts have been voting,
Mr. BRADFIELD should head the poll, leaving Mr. PEACOCK and Mr. BAILEY
to scramble for second seat, Mr. DE WET being a

good way off. That Mr. VAN HEERDEN will congratulate himself on his
success if he shall have kept Mr. DE WET out, is certain, for he never
had a chance during the whole campaign. But whether Mr.
BAILEY or Mr. DE WET dropps out, wo should still be content if Mr.
PEACOCK and Mr. BRADFIELD come in ; although we should much rather have
seen Mr. DE WET in if a Bondman or anyone with Bond sympathies must
needs be returned.

  “ Rowdysm ” is the general description given to it. Whether this is
  the correct description of the disorderly and noisy way the business
  was conducted, there can be no question of
  the disposition of Mr. Bailey’s supporters to turn a meeting, at which
  their idol was absent, into a pandemonium. Yet we think they did not
  score by these tactics which rather went against them in the
  estimation of quiet reflecting people. The weakness of the Mayor to
  control the elements was only too apparent, and his decision to put a
  vote of confidence in Mr. Bailey moved by a supporter of one of thanks
  was in explicable. Whether it was carried or not opinions vary
  although it was declared carried. To tell the truth, Mr. Andre is a
  warm partisan of Mr. Bailey’s, and many did not wonder at many things
  he left undone to render a meeting of his opponents unsuccessful. But
  the candidates both spoke well; Mr. Peacock being in good form. Mr.
  Bradfield s
  speech and manner impressed the meeting favourably, and we have no
  doubt he bettered his chances immensely.

Matelile Training (Private) School,

Whereas yearly reports of school work are, perhaps, to the general
public, very interesting reading, we therefore send to the Press the
following lines of the report of the above school. The school is wholly
a Native one, that is, it was proposed, started, supported and
in­structed by Natives. In comparing the past year with 1889, there is
every reason to bo thankful for what has been done, and to take courage
for the future. Up to the present time serious care has been felt for
the building of a larger chapel­school room hereat. Matelile, owing to
some slight defects in the original con­struction, as so alarming cracks
are de­veloping in the walls of the present building; it has been
proposed to raise funds for this object.

A larger sum has been received than during any previous year, that sum
including Government grant was £127 17 6 and we have a debt of £12 13 3.
