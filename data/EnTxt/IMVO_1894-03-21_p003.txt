who would attend. The following Coloured gentlemen spoke : —Messrs. Jas.
Currey, J. Geldenhuys, Wilson, Boyce and Fortunatus Brckers- teth. Their
speeches seem to us to be a vain lament over the colour line which
should be abandoned for something practical. Of the European friends Mr.
Shaw sympathised with the coloured speakers as to their claims to hive
their children admitted to English Schools. In this, as in most things,
the theory is good, but it is, up to the present stage of Cape human
progress, inapplicable; and coloured people as well as gentlemen of Mr.
Shaw’s sympathies should recognise this, and refrain from knocking their
heads against stone-walls. It is an in­disputable fact that the
introduction of a coloured child to some of the schools of the low class
whites would lead to such on exodus of pupils that it would be unwise to
attempt the experiment; while the schools of the high class whites like
Stellenbosch and Universities at Home have taken and are still taking in
Native students without the least effect on their efficiency. Let
therefore the Coloured people avoid worry by quietly complying with
public regulations for graduated schools, assured that for those of
their children who really wish to graduate, the high class schools in
this country and in England are open to them—provided they are able to
pay. We know whereof we affirm.

Native Lands question which affects

well aware that Native lands as a
whole will come within the Prime

with the Glen Grey lands as
typical of the rest, and as a case
that has comparatively recently

extent that a Special Commission
had to be sent to examine into
matters on the spot. Thus what-
ever may be said on Glen Grey
applies with more or less force—if
not with greater force—to the
other lands now held by our people
in this Colony, in regard to which
the Government has undertaken
the very serious responsibility of
legislating on. In these circum-
stances it is well that we should
attempt a clear and final statement
of the Native case with a view to
secure to them the comparatively
small patches of land in the Colony
now in the occupation of this
relatively large portion of the
people; and we therefore propose
to consider the question somewhat
move minutely than hitherto in a
series of well graduated articles.

Now, in regard to Glen Grey,
it will facilitate a good understand-
ing of the case to use the recent
Commission Report as the key of the
situation. On the very threshold
of the inquiry we find that the
first question submitted by Gov-
ernment to the Glen Grev Commis-
sion was ;—

“ The nature and condition of

CART’S two proclamations both

Further on the country is described

Further on the Commission finds
“ and these lands we find that the

of others ¹¹ they had inherited these

Thus the finding is absolute in
regard to the people being “ law-
fully entitled to ” and in “ legal
possession ” and having “ right to ”
and “ inherited ” the land in
question under a certain collective
and otherwise definite tenure called
“ tribal tenure.” There is, how-
ever, by a manifest confusion of
ideas on the part of the Commission,
one little mistake in the foregoing
finding—a mistake which is logically
and absolutely excluded by its
fundamental terms. This mistake
lies in the words “ given ” and
“ handed over ” as applied to the
effect of Sir GEORGE CATHCART’S
proclamation of the 22 November
1852; for, if, as the finding states,
“ they hold their land prior to the
proclamation referred to,” then
those lands were neither “ given ”
nor “ handed over to them ” by
those proclamations, which must
evidently have only recognised some
pre-existent tenure. Reference to
the proclamations themselves and a
study of them ought to have shown
the Commission that this was the
literal fact, and that when they
concluded to “ consider it unneces-
sary to go into the history of these
people and the terms on which
they held their lands prior to the
proclamation referred to,” they
omitted something of serious
importance in regard to the pri-
mary question submitted to them,
as is generally the case when people
ignore prior history in such

questions; and it will be seen that this mistake helped to becloud their
judgment and made them make other misstatements, inconsistent with its
fundamental terms and utterly illogical. For we after­wards find the
Commission, after most solemnly finding that the Glen Grey lands are the
lawful property of the Tambookie people, and the legal possession of
these people as of right and inheritance, turning round and telling the
same people that the same lands are Crown Lands, and that they may be
dispossessed if they do not accept some new sort of title changing that
legal tenure !

We are sure that Mr. RHODES will not repeat that illogical and untrue
statement to the Natives who are now in possession of their lands and
know well that their tenure is absolutely admitted and can only be
changed by their own consent and for reasons which will be convincing to
them. That Mr. FROST should have ventured to express to these people the
view that these lands are Crown Lands, is, besides its skewing illogical
confusion of ideas, the more extra­ordinary because he had actually been
the person who had a few years before bartered some of those same lands
from them in exchange for Crown Lands, as every one of them knew when he
was speaking to them; so that every one could see this confusion in his
thoughts. By the way Mr. FROST’S incapacity for clear connected thought
in con­nection with Glen Grey question is notorious.

BENEATH DIGNITY.

EVERY paper [observes the

{Kokstad Advertiser} in South Africa except the IMVO sup­ports the
Government in adopting a firm policy towards the Pondos. It is strange
[the same journal continues] that a Native newspaper the aim of which
should be to advance the welfare of the Native races, and raise its
voice against cruelty and oppression should take up the stand it does.
Even the Cape Mercury which has so often echoed the sentiments of the
IMVO says that no ‘ stupid sentiment ’ should stand in the way.” Now,
the stand we have taken is a per­fectly fair and straight one—at all
events in our own opinion. All we have steadily resisted in this Pondo
business has been the application to the Pondos of the rough-and- ready
doctrine that might is right. Every body knows—indeed the Pondos
know—that, compared with that of the Pondos’ the strength of the Colony
is that of the giant; and as Colonists we take no pleasure in that
strength being employed against Pondos who, presuming that they were
foemen, they are nevertheless no foemen worthy of the Colony’s steel.
We, for one, feel somewhat ashamed that this, the premier state of South
Africa should have so far forgotten itself as to go into the whole of
the paraphernalia and circumstance of war against the contemptible
Pondos who are beneath its dignity. And as those who have the credit of
our Colony at heart; also, as taxpayers we are bound to enter a protest,
however feeble, against the prodigal expenditure now being incurred by
the RHODES Government upon such proceedings. In the language of NQWILISO
at his meeting with Major ELLIOT, we “ do not understand why the
Govern­ment should bring an army as the whole business might be settled
without bringing troops into the country.” It is the distorting and
magnifying of things simple and small—the conversion of molehills into
mountains, to appease the hunger for war, that we dislike in the policy
of the Government in this matter; and as it is not our usual practice to
cloak or dissemble our feelings on public questions we have not been
afraid to proclaim our views even in the face of the fact that “ every
paper in South Africa ” has been against us. We could understand and
even approve of the Colony, with its Ilerculian strength, animated by
the wish to redress wrongs, moving its forces against “ cruelty and
oppression ” of both Natives and Uitlanders in the Transvaal. There it
would have a foeman by whom it would not be discredited; but in the
pompous move against a weak benighted tribe in a dark corner of the map
we smacks the unanointed bully, which we are sorry to see the Colony
playing in this affair.
