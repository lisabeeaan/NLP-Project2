NATIVE OPINION

BRANDY in EVIDENCE.

The Eastern Province is, daring these elections, in great danger of
sending to Parliament two staunch supporters of the Brandy Traffic as
its representa­tives in the Upper House for the next seven years. Mr.
DAVID DE WET, the Bondman, for whose return the great Leader of the Bond
Party in this country is at the present moment moving earth, if not
heaven, is, of course, under an obligation to support the policy of his
party which has long been known to be against any attempt to deal with
spiritous liquors legis­latively in the direction of restrict­ing the
traffic in them either by the imposition of an appreciable excise or
limitation of their sale. Then the Licensed Victuallers Association,
having scoured the country from Dan to Beersheba, have secured a man
after their own heart—Mr. THOMAS BAILEY—who is now known to be the
candidate of the interest that sets store by the Brandy cask. Himself
the posses­sor of several liquor houses in Queenstown—both in town and
district—Mr. BAILEY may bo de­pended upon to worthily and ade­quately
represent those who thrive on this baleful traffic; and the promoters of
the trade may be congratulated on the perspicacity shown in running
their own candi­date with a very fair prospect of getting him in. Now,
it is well known that Mr. HOFMEYR is leaving no stone unturned to secure
seven­teen, out of the twenty-two avail­able seats in the Legislative
Council: and those who have to read signs as they run know full well
that the main and immediate

aim of the Bond Leader in thus filling the Legislative Council with his
nominees is to make it impos­sible for any measure dealing with the
Brandy industry of the Paarl and Stellenbosch districts to pass into law
during the next seven or eight years. But as if this were not
sufficient, the agony is now being piled on by a non-Bond electorate
which is allowing the Whiskey Party to make use of it to send to
Parliament a representative of its own. Are we to believe that the cry
of Frontiersmen against the demoralisation of Native labour

and Native communities of these parts by cheap nasty brandy, was all
cant and humbug ? If not, why is Parliament being deliberately filled
with men who avow them­selves to bo interested in the hate­ful trade;
and why is no stronger outcry being raised by electors against
unblushing attempts to smuggle into the Upper House this special type of
representatives by the votes of men who abhor and detest a traffic that
has been the curse and blight of many a happy home ? Let electors be not
de­ceived. Let them not deceive themselves and be hoodwinked into
supporting brandy-cask principles that they have heartily condemned in
the past.

THE EDUCATION COMMISSION

THE Commission on Education, promised in the prorogation speech, has at
length been appointed. It consists of seven mem­bers, with Sir Jacob
Barry as President, the other members being the Very Rev. Dean Holmes,
of Grahamstown ; Dr. W. B. Berry ; Mr. A. N. Rowan, Deputy-In­spector of
Schools; Rev. A. Morrees, Dutch Reformed Minister of Richmond; Rev. Mr.
Coetzee, Dutch Reformed Minister at Burghersdorp ; and Mr. T. P. Theron,
M.L.A. We are among those who are curious to see what fresh facts the
present Commission will bring for­ward, which the one presided over by
the Chief Justice, ten years ago, omitted to record.

A POLITICAL MEETING

A VERY enthusiastic political meeting was held at
East London a week or two back, to enable the
electors of the Eastern Province resident at East London to have some
knowledge of the candidates for the three seats in the Legislative
Council. All the candidates except Messrs. David de Wet and
L. J. van Heerden, were present. The most interesting incident of the
meeting was in connection with the venting of the opposition of Messrs.
Baker and Malcomess to tho candidature of Messrs. Peacock and Bradfield.
That this opposition is based upon a shameful disregard of gentlemanly
etiquette which insists upon one gentleman accepting what the other may
say for himself as regards a question of facts, and that moreover it
dealt with the private concerns of candidates—makes it difficult to deal
with it in a decent manner in a journal which is anxious to appear
respectable. The wonder is that these would-be politicians appeared to
take no heed of the impatience of the audience with what Capt.
Brabant very properly described as a “family squabble.”

THE speeches of Messrs. Peacock and Bradfield, although founded on a set
of cut and dried resolutions, prepared by the town authorities, and
therefore tended to hamper candidates, were distinguished by a breadth
of view and a thoughtfulness which an enlightened community
welcome. But Mr. Wm. Rogers delivered the oration of the evening. He
accomplished this easily by simply disregarding the string of queries,
and proceeding to give the electors the speech which ho had prepared ;
and this he did effectively; and the fact that he has materially
improved his chances of election at the Port and in Kingwilliamstown is
evidence sufficient to prove what wo say. It was a pleasure to us to
have it demonstrated beyond the shadow of a doubt that the three
gentlemen whose candidature we have favoured were in every way the best
among the many that are offered ; and if the trio are returned they
would do infinite credit to the electors.

BUT the liquor people, THAT TRAFFIC, deeming their craft in danger in
case of Messrs, Bradfield, Peacock, and Rogers being returned, are,
after the fashion of their Ephesian prototype, creating a noisv
demon­stration over Mr. Bailey, and, with an energy worthy of a better
cause, are pushing on his candidature. Mr. Bailey also attempted to make
a speech, with the result that he made such a hash of it as to render
all who lay any claim to intelli­gence to marvel at what could have
ren­dered Mr. Baker, of all others, to espouse his cause. The candidate
does not only speak indifferent English, but, while devoting all his
time to the liquor traffic, it would appear that he neglected the study
of public questions. Mr, Bailey certainly

disappointed his followers that evening; he let the bagged cat out when
he avowed his connection with the hateful trade, and his ideas about the
freedom of the Natives to take their glass, notwithstanding the strong
feeling of both white and black against the sale of liquor to Natives,
With the quiet reflecting elector his candidature finds no favour: it is
with the loud­mouthed, public-house-frequenting-class that he is
extolled as the heaven-born candidate.

THIS candidate did not appear to have a following worth speaking about
at East London, nor did he appear to favourably impress the audience by
speech or manner. He is, however, fairly well supported by farm­ers in
the various districts, but this vote is so split up as to be of very
little value in calculating the chances of a candidate on its strength.
It is no wonder then to find that the general opinion inclines to the
view that his chances are grey indeed;

THE QUEENSTOWN MEETING

THE meeting hold at Queenstown on Friday last was not half as lively as
the East London one. Mr. Rogers and Mr. de Wet were the only candidates
absent; the former owing to swollen rivers, the latter having
engagements elsewhere. Mr. van Heerden opened with a speech in Dutch,
and was followed with an able instructive address from Mr. Bradfield,
and Mr. Gray followed. Mr. Bailey managed to say nothing, but was drawn
into a trap by Dr. Berry on the Excise, when he said he would go in for
an Excise of 2s. 6d. This was properly ridiculed by the audience. Mr.
Peacock made an able address. The Queenstown vote is also so split up as
to make it hard to say who will benefit by it. But Mr. Peacock will come
in for the lion’s share, Mr. Bailey, Mr. Gray, and Mr. Bradfield may
come in for equal portions.

AT the Queenstown meet- MR. BAILEY’S ing on Friday last, Mr.
CANDINATURE. Bailey, of whom we hear so much from his suppor­ters, in
the course ol the disjointed sen­tences he attempted, said the Port of
East London is so good that “ it required no puffing.” Good. But from
this we have also the explanation for the big drum that the cheap Brandy
Party are beating over their favourite candidate. The newspapers are
largely taken up with letters full of fulsome adulation, singing ??? to
this gentleman, although a dis­interested person can scarcely
compre­hend what there is to worship in the man. Clearly here is pulling
indeed ; and well might the publicans do so, for, if the people of these
parts are agreed on noth­ing else they are decidedly against the present
policy, which has flooded the country with cheap and bad brandy ; and no
amount of cajoling in the world will induce the quiet thoughtful
elector, be he white or black to vote for a Brandy monopoly. Let the
heathen rage and puff, for he will in the end have to con­tent himself
with the noise and the mak­ing of misrepresentations.

THE SOUTH EASTERN CONTEST.

ADDRESSED TO THE EDITOR OF “IMVO.”

SIR,—In your account of the contest In the South-Eastern Circle you
naturally fall into the mistake that it is a contest between a member of
the Bond and a non-Bondsman. This is quite a mistake, as both tho
opposing candidates—Messrs. Bellingan and Dolley—are Bondsmen, and the
only difference between them is that, the former is brought forward by
the representatives of all the branches of the Africander Bond in the
South-Eastern Circle, and Mr. Dolley by Port Elizabeth alone. The Port
Elizabeth papers say Mr. Bellingan is put forward by Western Province
wire-pullers, and Mr. Mr. Dolley by Uitenhage, but I assure you the case
is axactly as I have stated it. At a meeting of the representatives of
all the branches of the Africander Bond in the Circle, held at
Uitenhage, Mr. Moolman was chosen as the Bond candidate, and in case of
his not standing Mr. Bellingan was chosen as his substitute. Mr, Dolley
says that he was put forward by Uiten­hage because Father O’Brien and
some other gentleman asked him to stand. Now it is quite clear that
Father O’Brien and some gentlemen do not constitute Uitenhage, and it is
equally clear that Port Elizabeth, having adopted Mr. Dolley as her own,
and having pledged herself to do all in her power to secure his re­turn,
must have had some hand in getting him to stand. Now this deter­mination
on the part of Port Elizabeth to force Mr, Dolley down the throats of
the farmers is what they will not submit to, and many non-Bondsmen are
assisting them in this. They have chosen a farmer to represent them, and
they resent the dictation of Port Elizabeth in insisting on their
choosing a Uitenhage merchant, so much that they are determined to
con­centrate their strength and plump for Mr, Bellingan.

This is exactly what the Independents in the North-Eastern Circle are
doing, and in the same way. They are going to plump for Sir Henry
Stockenstroom in defiance of the Bond, and the Bond here are to plump
for Mr, Bellingan in dfiance of Port Elizabeth. Both may suc­ceed or
both may fail, but at all events both are entitled to try, and have a
right to the good wishes of those who love free­dom and hate dictation.

As to who are the favourites, that will only be seen at the poll, Mr,
Brister cal­culates that Port Elizabeth will give 3,000 votes for each
candidate; this is a large number for the farmers to make up, but if
they stick to eaoh other Mr. Bellingan will not be far behind. In the
town of Uitenhage tho votes will be nearly equal, while at Grahamstown I
think that Mr. Bellingan will get more support than be expected. I do
not think that Grahams­town half likes the way in which Port Elizabeth
has chosen a member for her, and Mr. Bellingan, by his straightfor­ward
honesty and stubborn independence has made a good impression, while Mr.
Dolley, by the shuffling manner in which he answered Dr. Atherstone’s
questions, has certainly made a bad one, and the hostile tone of the
Doctor’s questions shews that the Atherstone influence which is very
strong, will not be employed in Mr, Dolley’s favour,
