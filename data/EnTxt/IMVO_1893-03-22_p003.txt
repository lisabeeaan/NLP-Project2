Territories by a small majority. After a few ques­tions, which were
satisfactorily answered, he was accorded a vote of confidence. It may
here be added that there are audible grumblings in certain portions of
the constituency of Tembuland about Colonel Griffith being a silent
member ; and some of the electors have even sounded Mr. Victor Sampson,
Barrister-at-Law, that he should become a candidate in opposi­tion to
Colonel Griffith. No doubt Mr. Sampson would be the exact opposite of
the Colonel so far as the gift of the gab is concerned; but, as an
untried man, it would still remain to be seen whether the influence he
would exert in the House by speech and a becoming pre­sence would be as
great as Colonel Gri­ffith’s, malgre the silence, has been. These are
matters that have to be weighed by the constituency. We have never
concealed our feeling that Mr. Sampson would bo able to do good service
in Par­liament, but he cannot play exactly the part Colonel Griffith
holds in the As­sembly especially with his unequalled experience and
authority on Native questions. Should the Premier, in re­deeming his
promise of last session to Colonel Griffith, decide upon bringing in a
measure for the increased representa­tion of Tembuland and East
Griqualand, then the Transkei would be fortunate in having two members
of the standing of Colonel Griffith and Mr. Victor Sampson.

EXTRAVAGANT TALK.

ONE of those fighting articles which it has now become

usual for the more acute readers of the Kaffrarian Watchman to regard as
the productions of its “ Unknown Correspondent” in Capetown ap­peals in
its issue of Wednesday last. As might have been expected, it breathes
threatenings and slaughter against the Ministry, because it suspects
them of a certain tenderness towards the Natives of this country, which
shows itself in a disposition not to enforce Parliamentary Statutes with
that rigour which makes both the letter and the spirit felt. That the
aim of the writer in stirring this question up is to pro­voke war is
clear from the remarks which serve as a preface to the obser­vations
under notice. Says this fire eater : — “ Unless sharp and “ decisive
steps are taken for guard- “ ing ourselves against a daily in- “
creasing dinger, the country will ‘‘ become a black man’s country ; ” as
if it were not known even by the children in the street, that Africa was
from time immemorial the black man’s country. Then, as a protection
against this bogey, resort to repressive legislation is recommended. It
is acknowledged that such laws already exist, viz. : the Abakweta, the
Native Loca­tions, the Vagrancy, the Masters and Servants, and other
Acts ; but (and this is the gravamen of the complaint) the Government
does not carry them out. As a matter of fact the Government does carry
out the laws, and they are blamed because in carrying out laws, some of
which are crude and inapplicable to the circumstances of the people,
they do not provoke a war to give some of the “unemployed ” an
op­portunity to eke out a livelihood as of yore. Let it be un­derstood
that in what we

say we have no desire to parti­cularise any Ministry, for during the six
years preceeding the coming to office of the RHODES Government, a
Ministry was installed there, of which the “Unknown Correspond­ent” of
our contemporary was a member, which carried out these laws in a manner
scarcely dis­tinguishable from that adopted by the present Government.
We trust therefore Ministers will pay no attention to the interested
suggestions of which the Watchman has been made the vehicle to
pro­pagate. It is too shallow for the writer to seize upon the folly of
certain so-called “educated natives” —men who have not been
suffi­ciently educated and are therefore a danger, as experience
shows—in running a Tembuland politico-reli­gious movement, and trying
through it to blacken the education of the natives. Give these people a
good education or none at all if they are to become a useful factor in

the land; for educating by half, leaving them in the stage of educa-
tion which breeds conceit, is doing them the worst possible harm. Let
them be advanced to that borderland of education which makes all truly
educated men feel that they may not boast, seeing the vast amount of
which they are ignorant; and you will do them good. But we must protest
against attempts to tax the Native masses with the vagaries of wiseacres
who are the products of a system which favours half education which is
worse than no education at all. For ourselves we think the Government
cannot act too discreetly in enforcing measures, such as those relating
to the Abakweta and Intonjane, and the Locations, touching the very
foundations of the society of the mil­lion or two of the Natives in
South Africa, as it is easy to cause a perilous feeling of despair among
the more ignorant among the people. Such caution is all the more
necessary, because these masses are not even represented in the
Legislature which makes the laws—laws which, made in ignorance of the
real feel­ings of the people, are for the most part unworkable.

THE KAFIR BIBLE.

[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

SIR,—In my last which appeared in Imvo of February 9 I hinted that the
Revisers sometimes interpreted where there business was purely that of
Trans­lators. I object to the use of such words as: Umnqamlezo for
Ikuruse, a Cross— uku Betelela emnqamlezweni for uku Kurusesha, to
Crucify; Indaba ezilungi- leyo, for Ivangeli, the Gospel; Umshuma- yeli
wendaba ezilungileyo, for Umvangeli, an Evangelist.

Every Kafir who has had nothing but the barest elementary lessons in
Christ­ianity knows the meaning of such words as Ikuruse, Ukukurusesha,
Ivangeli, and Umvangeli. In English, French, German and Dutch they
appear as words evidently taken from another language and that language
Greek. If it is supposed that “ Umnqamlezo” is a word which answers to
our word a “ Cross ” I can only say that the word describes the Cross on
which our Saviour was crucified as well as does “Umveleli, Bishop”;
“Umenze- leleli, Priest ” ; “ Umlungiseleli, Deacon.”

Some one has written to me correcting a statement in my last, namely:
that Priest and Deacon according to this translation were synonymous
terms— I am told Umenzeleleli is a Priest! Umlungiseleli, is a Deacon !!
I don’t want to argue—I leave it to your readers and especially those of
the Anglo Catholic or Roman Catholic persuasion to decide between me and
the Revisionists as to the fitness of these words—I have already said
that such words can be made to stand for anything and I won’t go out of
the Bible to prove my assertion. As a matter of fact this word “
Umlungiseleli ” means in this Kafir translation a “ Minister ” or
“Servant” and as such I have no objection to make against it. We read in
Ex. 24:13, and other places of “Joshua tho minister of Moses.” Was
Joshua a Deacon, (Umlungiseleli), or did he hold any ecclesiastical
office? Scarcely. Again we read in Rom. 15: 8, “ Christ hath been made a
minister (umlungiseleli) of the Circumcision.” Will any one say that our
Blessed Lord was in any way a Deacon in the sense in which we read the
following: “Paul and Timotheus tho servants (abakonzi) of Jesus Christ
to all the Saints in Christ Jesus which are at Philippi with the Bishops
and Deacons (abalungiseleli). Let me say again, I want these words
“Bishop, Priest, Deacon” together with other similar ecclesiastical
words to appear in Kafir as wo find them, incorporated or grafted into
other languages.

“ Uzuko,” “ Ubuqaqauli,” “Glory,” I have nothing to say against these
words excepting that ubuqaqauli should be only used when speaking of the
Divine glory or that of the “heavenly bodies,” my idea of ubuqaqauli is
as of something ineffably pure and perfect. The rising sun or a glorious
sunset is the only idea I can form of what this Kafir word, ubu­qaqauli,
implies.

“Sons or children of men” Guyana baka Adam, “ Daughters of men,” “
Into­mbi zabantu,” “Son of man,” “Unyana woluntu,” in Num. 23: 19, but
in the book of the prophet Ezekiel, the Spirit of God addresses the
prophet as “ Nyana ka Adam.” Again in Psalm 140: 1, “The evil man ” is
Isintu esinobubi, but in Job 20 29 ; 27. 13 and Prov. 11. 7, we read not
Isintu but umntu ongendawo. Now what strikes me as most peculiar is why
we should say “ Guyana baka Adam,” “ Sons of men ” and “Intombi
Zabantu,” “ Daughters of men.” To the ordinary reader it is just as
confusing to speak of the Sons of Adam, when you mean Sons or Children
of men as to have written : The Daughters of Adam for the Daughters of
men in Gen. 6. 4. Adam is Hebrew for “a man” or “any human being.”

One more word and I will not encroach upon your space any further this
time. In Gen. 42. 8 we read: “ Joseph knew his brethren but they knew
not him.” The Hebrew word to which I would now ask your readers to
consider is repeated twice in this verse and I want to know why a change
is made in Kafir. It also occurs in the 7th verse. This 8th verse shows
two different verbs in Kafir: “U Yosefu wabazi, etc., ko bona
abamnakaanga yena.” This verb ” nakana” you will also find in Gen. 27.
23, and Job 2. 12 and 4. 16. In Ruth 3. 14 we find ‘ nakanana.’ The only
passage in the New Testament where this word “ nakana ” occurs is in
Mark 8. 24, Revised Version: “ I see men; for I behold them as trees
walking,” “ Ndiya babona abantu ; kuba udiba na­kana behamba ngati
yimiti.” I under­stand by “nakana” either to discern or recognise things
at a distance or after explanation given by another. “ Qondi- sisa” I am
told is the same in this last sense as “nakana.” My contention about
this word is that the change from Ukwazi to Ukunakana is not only
useless butan error in Gen. 48. 8. But the He­brew word has some half
dozen Kafir equi­valents. In English it is translated: “ Discern, know,
perceive, and see.” In Kafir, “ Ukwazi, ahlula, qonda, nakana, fanisa,
buka.” The last three I believe are not right. For Fanisa see Gen. 31.
32, 38. 25; Judges 18. 3. In Psalm 142. 4 ; “ There is no man that
knoweth me,” “Akuko undi-buka-yo.” I should be glad to hear what others
have to say either in English or in Kafir.

  Very truly yours,
