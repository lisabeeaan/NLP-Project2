THE BILL PASSED.

  LTHOUGH the Franchise Bill
  has now passed both the

Houses of Parliament, it is, never-
theless, not an Act. Considerable
interest will still be taken in the
measure whilst it goes through the
subsequent stages which it must
necessarily pass before it becomes
the law of the land. From the
Legislature it is sent on to His
Excellency the Governor. We shall
know presently whether Sir HENRY
LOCH will take upon himself the
grave responsibility of assenting in
the Queen’s name to a Bill radically
dealing with a Constitution that has
Worked wonderfully well for forty
years, and against which the most
ardent advocate of change can
allege no fault. There are various
considerations of a broad and com-
prehensive character which might
weigh with the Governor in finally
coming to a decision on this very
important matter, and cause His
Excellency to shrink from putting
his signature to it. A change in
the Constitution has been asked for
not from a deliberate conviction
of the Colonists as a whole, but by
the forced opinion of an organisa-
tion that employs terrorism on its
unhappy victims to achieve its aims.
This is an aspect of the question
that would doubtless particularly
strike any impartial observer. To
add to this there stands out boldly
the fact that the Bill has been a
thing of shreds and patches devised
by a Ministry that feared greater
evils that appeared very imminent.
Of this the members of the Gov-
ernment themselves have made no
secret. In Parliament, too, one
cannot help remarking that it has
been opposed by an English mi-
nority which could not help itself.
It becomes an important question
then whether, in circumstances such
as . these, His Excellency would in-
terfere with what is clearly the
right of Her Majesty’s Government,
which gave the Constitution in the
first instance, and forestall the
Imperial decision by giving a
reAdy assent to the measure. A
string point was made by Mr. J.
Mr .ORPEN in the course of the
debate in the Lower House that
provision should be made in the
Bill to prevent mad rushes on the
Constitution by chance majorities
in the Legislature. But the 82nd
and 83rd Sections of the Constitu-
tion Ordinance make ample provi-
sion we think against such inroads;
and the Imperial power—should
Sir HENRY LOCH reserve the Bill
for the signification of Her Ma-
jesty’-s pleasure—would, we think,
save us from ourselves by sus-
pending the measure until a strong
expression of opinion in its fa-
vour makes itself felt in the
land. We decline to consider the
resolutions of the Bond, brought
about by designing men by means
of an automatic machine, as the
convictions of the people of this
country; and from legislation
under panic our good Sovereign
should deliver us. Mr. GLADSTONE
in his recent Midlothian campaign
has done much to disarm opposition
to Home Rule by defining the
duties of the Parliament he is work-
ing to establish in Dublin as those
which Parliaments in Colonies like
our own transact in subordination
to the Imperial Parliament. It is
in this sense that we, for one, have
never believed those who said Home
Rule was separation ; but our faith
will be much shaken in Mr. GLAD-
STONE’S policy if, in this affair of
the Cape' Parliament, he does not
watch, with jealous eye that the
subordinate' authority is not per-
mitted lightly to alter the Consti-
tution which brought it into
existence without reference to the
Imperial Parliament. It is as well
that this matter should fall to a
Liberal Secretary of State to pro-
nounce a final judgment on; as it
will be a guide as to the sincerity
of the Liberals in saying the Parlia-
ment at College Green will be truly
and in every sense subordinate to
the great body at St Stephen’s. It
won’t be so if it is allowed to change
the terms of its own Constitution.
If the foundations of the Constitu-
tion must needs be changed, we
submit then the power which gave
should have the opportunity of look-
ing at the thing from every side,
as otherwise the country is in dan-
ger of having any majority for the
time being proceeding to the Con-
stitution if it sees its way to benefit
itself. This, we contend, apart
from the nature of the Bill itself,
should not be.

A RELIGIOUS REVIVAL

  HE work Rev. THOMAS COOK has come from England to do

has claimed attention in every town he has visited. Mr. COOK is a gifted
Evangelist in connection with the Wesleyan Methodist Conference in
England; but from the time he began his evangelistic career, his

  aims have been to bring religion to the door of every man he can reach
  in the most effective and effectual way leaving mere denominationalism
  to take care of itself. Hence as the result of his Mission Services
  there have been sometimes additions of persons not only to the various
  Protestant bodies but even to the Roman Catholic Church, outside his
  own particular body. That Mr. COOK’S work in this Colony should have
  called for notice at the hands of the unconcerned and indifferent to
  religion is no new experience; and so particularly blessed have his
  labours been among our people that we cannot help alluding to the
  fact. RCV. -THOMAS- COOK’S visit was ostensibly for the benefit of the
  European community. So far as we know Annshaw or KAMA’S is the only
  Native station which was in­cluded among his appointments which were
  cut and dried before he came out. In the various towns, however, our
  people have asked for crumbs, and the result so far has always been a
  full meal. The Na­tive people have proved wonderfully impressionable.
  So much has this been the case that everybody who makes religion his
  concern must now regret that his work did not extend more to the
  Natives of this country. At Annshaw, where he has been during the past
  week, re­markable results have been wit­nessed. During the week’s
  mis­sion more than a thousand have pro­fessed the Christian faith from
  Mr. COOK’S ministry; on Sunday last alone 450 deciding for CHRIST.
  What is most gratifying is that the “ reds ” have been largely
  in­fluenced by the Mission. Let us hope that in view of results such
  as these steps may be taken to enable Mr. COOK, on closing bis present
  tour, to revisit this country, more especially with a view to labour
  among Natives, among whom his Ministry seems to be so acceptable.

declarations commands notice. Mr. Rhodes’s speech will be his­torical
because it gave the death-blow to the long-cherished pious sentiment of
a United South Africa. The Premier as­serted in language at once
emphatic and unequivocal that, while everyone of the various States was

POLITICS. Merriman touched upon

matters of no small moment. The Prime Minister stated that his Ministry,
composed as it was of gen­tlemen of marked individuality, was given to
thoroughly probing questions that came before it, and unlike the late
Cabinet it is not a one-man Ministry. Hence to a great extent its
decisions on matters of vital import represent com­promises, such as
only are possible among such talented men as have the control of
affairs. There is nothing to take exception to this. It can only serve
to increase the confidence of the country in those who have the
management of its affairs. Mr. Merriman remarked on the apathy of the
country in political matters. This those of a political turn of mind
have long felt, and it is this apathy that renders it absolutely
necessary that those responsible for the Government should, when
opportunities present themselves, give attention more to what is
practical than to what is absolutely constitutional.

FROM the organ of this LOVEDALE. Institution, the Christian Express, we
gather that Dr. Stewart is expected from Scotland in a week or two. He
will spend probably one or two months at Lovedale and return to
Scotland, where for little short of a year he will occupy temporarily
the Free Church Professorship of Evangelical Theology. Dr. Stewart
resumes his duties at Lovedale about May next year. Other information of
interest concerning the Institution relates to the arrival of Miss M. J.
Dodd, appointed to the princi­pal charge of the Girls’ School in
suc­cession to the late Mrs. Muirhead. Rev. Brownlee Ross, M.A., is also
to begin his missionary labours at Lovedale. He is an old pupil of the
institution which he left to complete his studies in Edin­burgh. Mr.
Brownlee Ross, to whom we wish hearty success, will act for Mr. Geddes
who, with Mrs. Geddes, is taking a well-earned furlough, after fourteen
years of continuous work. Mr. and Mrs. Geddes have the best wishes of
the past pupils of Lovedale scattered throughout South Africa for a
pleasant furlough. The work of the session has been begun with a
considerable number of new entrants, though not as large as at the
beginning of the year.
