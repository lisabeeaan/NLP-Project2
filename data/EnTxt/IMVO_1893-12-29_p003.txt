SHORT PAPERS, CHIEFLY ON SOUTH AFRICAN SUBJECTS. Andrew Smith, M.A.,
Lovedale Press 1893.

We have read this little work with care and much interest, The papers,
which deal with our people and are specially intended for them, have
naturally in­terested us more than others, and it is with such papers
that we propose to deal in subsequent remarks. In passing the others we
may state that we fully recognise their value. They refer to subjects of
great importance, and the ability with which each subject is pre­sented
is well worthy of the writer—and to say this is to affirm that it is
above the common level. The book as a whole is written in a most
interesting style, the tone is always in harmony with the subject of the
paper. It is free, forcible, and sober without being heavy and the
English is pure. Each sentence has something to convey. To say that the
book has been printed at the Lovedale Institution is to say that the
printer has discharged his part of the work with care. The book is
hereby recommended to all who would spend a few hours of enjoy­able and
profitable reading.

But as we have remarked the papers bearing on the Natives will engage
our attention more than those on other sub­jects. These papers have the
very rare merit of combining a good knowledge o the subject treated of
and of being in evident sympathy with the Natjve. This is a rare
combination. We are aware that all who have been a fortnight in the
country claim to be high autho­rities on the Native question, and that
each one professes to be unsurpassed in his desire to do good to the
Natives. Unfortunately for such individuals and perhaps also for us they
fail to impress us with what they profess to be. We feel that their
opinions are crude and harsh—we think that the evidence of self interest
and self glorification is very strong. If, for instance, one will take
the statements which are being made in the papers, on the platforms and
in Par­liament on the labour question and com­pare them with the few but
just remarks in the book under review, we venture to say that he will
experience the same feeling that one has in passing from a scabby flock
of sheep to one which has been under the treatment of Cooper and others.
In this little book we have once after many days come across statements
which leave no doubt in us that the writer knows what he is writing
about, and that our condition to him is a matter of great concern. His
hopeful tone is a great encouragement, and we hope that all our
countrymen who can understand English will get a copy, and we are
con­fident that all who will do so will find much to instruct and to
guide. The papers are intended to lead our people to do and to strive to
be something. Their attention is directed to Government Savings Banks,
Laws of Health, The Land Question, Agriculture, The Future of the Native
races, and other topics of the greatest importance to us. Those of our
people who will read the book—and we hope that all who can will do
so—will find much to learn even in what Mr. Smith blames. We do not
think it necessary to illustrate our remarks with quotations, but we
feel confident that all who will read the book will agree with the very
high estimate we have formed of this work, and that reading it will give
great encouragement. This does not of course imply that we endorse every
statement the writer makes. The points on which however there can
possibly be difference of opinion, are very few and not important and we
