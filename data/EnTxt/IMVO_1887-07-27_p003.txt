THE editor of this paper having gone to Port Elizabeth in connection
with the proposed deputation to England, we claim the consideration of
our friends if there be any shortcoming in this issue.

THE meeting of natives reported in this issue was well attended, and
unanimous in its desires. We wish that those who so eagerly affirm that
Natives are in leading strings had been present. That is if seeing would
be believing with them, but we much fear that it would not.

MR. HOFMEYR’S Bill, it is pretty well known, is to allow all natives who
become registered voters to be free from the laws specially intended for
the large mass of barbarians—at least this is the ostensible object;
though some affirm that it is planned with the ulterior motive of
allowing free trade in drink. We are not going to think so hardly of Mr.
Hofmeyr, and seeing that the Bill dis­tinctly recognises that natives
may be­come in all legal respects equal to Europeans of the same status
we cordially welcome it as a step in the direction of progress.

Pursuant to notice, this society, which consists of representatives from
the various native centres in this large district, held a public meeting
at Mgqakwebe on Thursday evening last, to consider what steps to take in
view of the total disfranchisement of natives under the misnamed
Registration Bill.

Thomas Sambula was voted to the
chair.

The discussion on the disfranchisement measure was opened by the reading
of a letter from Capetown, intimating that the Bill will probably not be
reserved for the Queen’s assent. After the consideration of the probable
operation of the measure as disclosed by the discassions in committee of
the House on the various clauses, especially clause 17, it was felt that
a blow had been
struck at the rights of the natives.

Philip Mali asked who gave the natives these rights originally, and
whether those who were depriving the natives of them had consulted those
who gave them. It was explained that the rights were originally given
the natives bv the British Government. Then, Mali said, according to
Kafir precedents the mandate of Hintsa’s Great Place could not be set
aside by the courts of
Ndlambe or Ngqika. There was always a course of appeal from the decision
of Ndlambe’s court to the court of Hintsa.

Klaas Mlonyeni feared that by appealing to the British Government they
might bring about their complete enslaving by the Government at
Capetown. Some of the speakers held that there was no worse slavery than
disfranchisement, which leaves them perfectly defenceless from harsh
legislation of whatever description, while others maintained that to
appeal conveys no slighting of the local Government. It might if they
went behind their backs, but they were taking an honest and
straightforward position in saying distinctly to the Capetown
authorities that they would abide by the decision of Hintsa (the British
Government). Ultimately the following resolutions were agreed npon on
the motion of Sonjica :—

“ (1) That this meeting is of opinion that a petition requesting the
Governor not to sanction the Registration Bill be sent.”

” (2) That should our efforts by petitioning the Governor be
unsuccessful, this meeting is of opinion that a deputation of about four
natives should be sent to England, to lay our case before her Majesty’s
Government and the English people.”

The following message, to be sent by telegraph to his Excellency the
Governor, was approved by the meeting :—

“The natives of King Williamstown district would most respectfully
request your Excellency not to sanction the Registration Bill now before
Parliament. Petitions will follow.”

It was also decided that in case it became necessary to send a
deputation to England, the deputation should be entrusted with a
petition from the natives of this country to the Queen.

Resolved that a subscription list be started, both among the white
friends of the to all men equal rights, and among the natives to assist
in carrying out these resolutions.

THE LIQUOR AREA.

The liquor areas question was also considered, and in view of the fact
that a Select committee of the House is Bitting on the subject, it was
decided to wire Mr. Vintcent, who is upon it, the conviction of the
natives in the area that the real facts about their effectiveness can
only be obtained from them.

The meeting was large and enthusiastic.

  Bond. This is Responsible Government. And we presume it is an
  illustration of the freer hand that was to be allowed the Colonial
  Government, about which SIR THOMAS UPINGTON told us upon his return
  from the London Conference. It seems a strange anomaly that those who
  are so affected should have the least say
  about the matter. Surely before the closure was put on by Sir HERCULES
  ROBINSON, our people might have been allowed the satisfaction of going
  to the fountain head, where the Constitution of the Cape originated,
  and placing their arguments before an unprejudiced tribunal before any
  finality was given to the obnoxious measure. The difficulty of the
  Native deputation to England —resolved upon by our Port Elizabeth
  friends, and supported by the influential meeting of Thursday – is
  largely increased, though their grievance is undoubtedly made the
  greater by that action. If the proceedings of Parliament as lately
  recorded, and the decision of the Governor are to be taken as
  prece-dents, we cannot see what is to prevent any powerful majority
  from erasing a minority altogether, and so permanently ensure their

  as a safeguard, and in this it appears we were leaning on a broken
  reed. We suppose that the deputation agreed upon will still be sent,

  will be absolutely necessary, for in that period Mr. HOFMEYR’S Bill
