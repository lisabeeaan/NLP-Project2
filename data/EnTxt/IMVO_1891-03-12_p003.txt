A FALSE REGISTER.

THE Kingwilliamstown Register

of Voters, which has been used during the recent elections, has revealed
some gross and curious inaccuracies. Many names of persons who had made
sure of their registration in 1889-90 have dropped out of the printed
list in some
unaccountable way. Whether this is the fault of the local officials or
of the Government Printers, in Cape Town, inquiry will show ; and we
shall spare no pains to see that such inquiry is instituted, if it
should unfortunately appear that a spur is necessary to cause the
Government to do a plain duty in a case of this nature. Not only people
who had made sure of their registration by the Field-Cornets have
dropped out, but many who were rejected by those officers and had to
appear before Mr. CHALMERS on the Registration Court day to prove their
claims, and who succeeded in doing so, have been omitted from the
Register as printed. Much annoyance and inconvenience has been
occasioned by this slipshod fashion of doing public business. We believe

Natives are the chief sufferers in this respect; and whoever has been
instrumental in disfranchising wholesale, qualified persons should have
meted out to him that punish­ment which is the due of every rogue of his
kind. We have clear testimony before us, in respect to one Ward at all
events, to show that the lists as sent in by the Field-Cornets had all
the names of the qualified people in that Field- Cornetcy, and the
responsibility of their dropping out of the printed Voters’ List
devolves either on the Civil Commissioner’s Department or on Messrs. W.
A. RICHARDS and Sons, Capetown ; and the investi­gation, which must be
proceeded with, should determine on which horse that responsibility must
be saddled. For people cannot spend time and energy to enregister
themselves to find that all their trouble has been in vain.

TRANSVAAL

LESSONS of the CONTEST.

THE various contests in the different Provinces of the Colony for scats
in the Legislative Council, have developed an unexpected and sudden
interest in these parts. The Upper House is such a ponderous slow-going
concern that people had become indifferent to its proceedings. But the
struggle for seats in it, which culminated in the polling on Tuesday
week, has been entered into in
these Provinces with as much zest as if the stakes were a seat in the
Lower or active branch of the Legislature. Contests invariably offer
unique opportunities for the ventilation of public questions; and the
elections just closed
have proved no exception to the rule. In the Eastern Province the fight
has turned upon education or no education as an additional and
indispensable qualification for a member; as also on the
brandy cask and sobriety. Those who care little about the future and do
not contemplate the grave effects of laissez faire in these matters have
recorded their votes for their man; and although they
do not by any means comprise the majority of the people of these parts,
they have by the leverage of the cumulative votes, managed to attract
attention ot a certain kind. But they cannot put the hands of the clock
back; and both the cause of education and the sale of spirits will
sooner or later—we believe it will be sooner than later—be dealt with in
the spirit of progress and enlightment. In the important Circles of the
North East, South East and Midland the struggle has
been a keen and interesting one. Those men who love freedom and are for
straightforward management of public affairs—-men who cannot tolerate
the conduct of the Government of the country by tricks—have flung
themselves into the contest for emancipating the country from the
slavery of Bondism. Under the comprehensive device—Independents,
emblazoned on their flag, they have begun a struggle, which we trust
will be protracted until the yoke of Bondage which is sought to be
thrust on the country is finally disposed of. We wish the cause of
Independentism well; and hope it will rapidly secure adherents and
be strong enough to weld together those who do not see any good in the
Bond to the putting in office of a Government that will not rule by the
grace of the Bond.

THE TWO INTERPRETERS FOR THE G.W

SOME time ago wo published a letter from a Barkly West correspondent
calling attention to HIGH COURT, an absurd arrangement in connection
with the High Court of Griqualand West, whereby two Native Interpreters’
appointments had been made. The position had been filled for years by a
very capable Native, Mr. Joseph S. Moss; but, as we gathered from the
remarks of our correspondent, a white man was also appointed as second
Interpreter, as it was deemed not a re­spectable thing for the Court to
have a Native for its Interpreter. The pruning knife of retrenchment has
reached this abuse, and Mr. S. B. Liefieldt, second in­terpreter, in
addition to his interpreting work, is now appointed Protector of
Na­tives in the place of Mr. J. Glen Leary, who is to be Resident
Magistrate of Mount Fletcher. We only regret Mr. Liefeldt has not been
appointed absolu­tely to the Native Protector’s duties.

TRANSVAAL POLICE OUTRAGES.

  WE hope The Star (Johannesburg) will continue to expose police
  brutalities towards native labourers at the Goldfields ; and are glad
  to see prominence given in the Mail

Summary of our contemporary to the following incident: — A serious case,
which will be found fully reported else­where, of alleged ill-treatment
of a native by policemen, is said to have occurred on Sunday last, near
Boksburg. A boy named Scop was arrested by two police­men for being
without a pass. He appears to have been brutally ill-used, and his body
was found in the road the following day. The matter will form the
subject of an enquiry. The pass law places consider­able and unchecked
power in the hands of the police, which can be easily abused; and from
the complaints which Kafirs have recently made, it would appear that it
often has been abused. It has even been said by large employers of
natives, that the consistent ill-treatment in out­side districts has
engendered in the minds of many of the boys a sense of injustice, which
is of itself sufficient to account for the scarcity of labour on these
fields.

NATIVES AND THE CAPE POLICE

SOME time ago in the course of an article a statement was made that in
Commissioner Sissison’s District Natives had been dismissed bag and
baggage. We have great pleasure to state, on the highest authority, that
only those who were guilty of gross misconduct had been so
dismissed—good men being retained. According to our informant there are
in this district seven Native mounted policemen, a number which we must
say is very small indeed, and seventeen foot­men. We understand the
Commissioner requires eight good men for the latter service to fill up.
We hope these will be forthcoming.
