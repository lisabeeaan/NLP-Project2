THE proceedings of

Farmers’ Association have now-a-days a special interest for us.

It is from them that the first inklings of the Government policy, in so
far as it is in the direction of the Native Affairs Department, may be
gathered. The proposals for the resuscitation of Location Inspectors,
abolished a few years back, were communicated to the Keiroad Farmers. To
the Farmers has now been made known the intention to send a Commission
to investigate the tenure of Glen Grey lands by Natives. The fol­lowing
record is taken from the minutes of the Indwe Farmers’ Association.

Letter read from the Undersecretary for Native Affairs, stating that as
soon as Government had completed their ar­rangements respecting the
visit of the Commission on the Glen Grey Native question, the
Association would Le in­formed when and where the Commission would
receive the deputation appointed by the Association.

Now, we are not aware that any I intimation has been made to the

Natives to whom the matter is one of life and death. From the absence of
communications with Natives in such a matter may be construed the
settled policy of Mr. FAURE to kow-tow to the Dutch

suggestions enough for coping with the evil. Our people have never as a
race connived at this species of crime ; and indeed when their own
institutions obtained in this country stock-stealing had been put under.
Things have, however, changed. We are now living in times when black men
are made to feel, and they do feel it, that nothing is expected from
them in the way of suggestions. Being out of touch with the Government,
all they must do is unquestioningly and obediently to carry out what the
Government of the white man bids them to do. The Government knows better
than they do them­selves what is good for them ; and they must not
complain. The people know that Government does not care to consult them,
and will not condescend to take or consider । what they may say,
because, for­sooth, it would lower its authority in the eyes of these
people. In these circumstances it will easily be seen that it would be
the sherest labour of SISYPHUS to work up a public opinion among a
people so hopeless and helpless. It is as much as they can do
despairingly to resist measures of needless oppres­sion : but for any
efforts of self­-improvement, under the present system they are done
for.

Stock stealing is not rife just now ; but much could be effected by
reviving a healthy public opinion among Natives on the subject; and
perhaps the initial steps to this end would be to appoint a Commission
composed of Natives themselves to take evidence among their own
countrymen and present a Report. The people would thus be made to feel
their own responsi­bility in the good government of the country, and
would leave no ' room for Europeans reproaching them with cloaking and
sympathi­sing with stock-stealing or with criminals in general The
question is, Will the Government do this and enable the Natives to
remove the stigma placed on them ? We think not.

GOVERNMENT—not to say the Press— cannot, we think, be too lynx eyed in
watching how justice is dispensed in out­lying Districts like Peddie.
Chiefly peopled by Natives without a Press and a public opinion, it is
not to be wondered at that at times strange and almost un­accountable
tilings are done. One day last week the authorities had summarily
—without warrant and without issuing Summonses—brought before the
Magis­trate a much respected Native—Mr. Mark Mpahla—for allowing his boy
to ride a horse with a wart on the leg, a wart which the doctor had
pronounced caused no inconvenience to the animal. The boy for this was
put in the box and sentenced to three days hard labour or 5s. This, it
would seem, did not satisfy the authorities. Strange to say Mr. Mpahla
for the same offence-although he had not ridden the horse—was afterwards
taken from the body of the Court and put in the prisoners’ box. He also
was lined 10s. or a week's imprisonment. The man who never was before a
Magistrate in his life, feels his degration keenly ; and were not the
principle (where only a Native was concerned) de minimis non curat lex,
this was a tit subject for look­ing into.

AFTER the stand-up light the E. P. Herald has maintained for the Native
Inspectors, it was quite natural it should be angry at the inquiry
Government has decided upon instituting into the matter. Our
contemporary _ declaims against the step as “ the petting of the
Natives,” which ” takes for granted that they know best what kind of
Govern­ment suits them.” We always thought our contemporary possessed
sufficient intelligence to know that Responsible Government always took
for granted that people knew best what kind of Government suited them;
and that therefore Natives have only carried out the letter and spirit
of that form of Government. “ Our Government, ’ we are told, “is
mildness itself compared ' with the kind of treatment to which they have
been accustomed from their chiefs.” Are we to understand by this, then,
that Government must needs be unnecessarily harsh on the Natives and
follow in the footsteps of Native chiefs ) This is a gross perversion of
the Queen’s ⁵ gifts. The E. P. Herald should rejoice at, rather than
resent, the Natives anxiety to make their wants and feelings known ; as
no good can be had from the alternative of muffled talk.

LETTER TO THE EDITOR.

SIR,—I trust the resistance made to the appointment of Location
Inspectors by the Delegates who met at Pirie, will prove successful.
These Inspectors simply torture honest people; their efforts against
stock thieves are perfectly futile. The proposal to appoint them comes
remotely from the Farmers who are exasperated at thieving; and no
wonder. A farmer here had a fine flock of ewes, which he sent to the
out-veldt. Thirty-five of them suddenly vanished, and he was so vexed
not only at the loss, but at the insecurity, and at his helpless­ness to
protect himself, that he sold off the whole flock of eleven hundred.
This state of matters is intolerable. It makes honest industry and
economy impossible.

The cruellest enemies of the Native people are stock thieves. They have
been the chief cause of the Native wars, and their fellow-countrymen
have these criminals to thank for the loss of the beautiful valleys of
the Chumie, the Keiskaina, and the Kat River, and for their being now
cooped up in Locations where the pasture is eaten as bare as a carpet.

Could not the Vigilance Associations do something in the matter of stock
thieving. I am firmly convinced they could, if they had the foresight
and the energy to set about it. There is some­thing wrong in Native
opinion on this question. The bulk of the people would not steal
themselves, but many of them ar₀ suspected of having a shrewd guess who
the thieves are, and yet they keep it quite close, and they don’t
heartily assist the farmers or the police in discovering the thieves.
They have a false sentiment against informing on their own colour. That
is a deplorable error. No quarter should be given to thieves, murderers,
or to the criminal classes generally, who are the worst enemies of their
race. This alleged sheltering of criminals is the standing pretext for
that system of in­justice, which is known as the Boer policy.

I am not quite sure what the Vigilance Associations could effect in this
matter. It is for them to say. They could at least agitate the question,
and create a healthier state of opinion and feeling on the sub­ject. The
mere effort to do so would conciliate European sentiment. It is well
known that the enlightened Natives so far from sympathising with stock-
stealing, would welcome rigour in the punishment of thieves. Veldtman
and I his friends proposed branding even, and although that is
impossible, it shows how they feel in the case.
