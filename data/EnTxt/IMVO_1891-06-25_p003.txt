  Native Opinion,

ONE of those discussions on Native matters in which the country members
are wont to take part came off in the Legislative Assembly on Thursday
last. On such occasions the floodgates of bucolic eloquence appear to be
thrown open, although somehow the speakers manage to say the same thing,
if not in pretty much the same words, at all events, with slight
variations in language, they often convey the same ideas. An opportunity
offered on Thursday last for a debate on a motion by Mr. DAVID DE WAAL
for the closing of Native Schools—the distinction being emphasized—

during the ploughing and reaping seasons. The object is to enable
farmers to secure labour from the children attending these Schools. It
is clear that the proposal has to do with the Schools of the coloured
classes of the West although no effort is made to distinguish between
Natives of the West and those of the Eastern districts of the Colony. A
very sensible suggestion has b^en prominently thrown out in the columns
of the Cape Times pointing out the absolute necessity of
differ­entiating in referring to Natives between the Frontier peoples
and the coloured population of the West; the circumstances of these
classes differing so. This, how­ever, by the way. Mr. DE WAAL argued
that farmers had no labour­ers, all the children being drafted into
schools. lie proceeded to enunciate the old Boer belief that GOD had
ordained that ^(u) the black “should be the servant of the far- “mer,
the ‘baas.’ ” Mr. LE ROEX supported the resolution in the same strain.
Fortunately it was not in this dogmatic spirit that subsequent speakers
addressed the House. Mr. DE VOS, whose speeches arc always characterised
by sound common sense, re­marked on the wants of the various districts
not being the same, and saw difficulties in practically carry­ing out
the motion. It would be hard to gainsay a proposition laid down by Mr.
DE VOS that “ To be “a good farm servant one should “ have learnt to
labour in his youth. “ If children were left at school “till their
fifteenth or sixteenth “ year, they turned out good-for- “ nothings ”
[on farmsj. To the point also are ISIr. BASSON’S obser­vations that “ if
it was considered “ that the coloured people were our “ labourers, they
should be educa- “ ted as labourers besides enjoying “ school
education.” Mr. HOFMEYR demonstrated the impracticability of making a
distinction between white and coloured children, and proceeded to give
what really is the key to the whole question. “ It “ was going too far,”
said the member for Stellenbosch, “ to close “ the Schools for such a
purpose “ when so many children were run- “ ing idle in the streets.” It
is surprising, after such a statement of the case, Mr. THERON should
have still deemed it worth while to refer to very exceptional cases of
Natives playing cricket and lawn tennis as if all Native children were
so occupied. Still the fact remains that for every Native child in
School a thousand are not in the school room ; and the question is not
as to how to harass the small number in school, but how to utilise the
raw material that does not go to school. School-going being a matter
involving four hours at the outside, it is not exactly correct to say
those who frequent schools do no work at all. We are ready to subscribe
to the statement that farmers are the best teachers of work ; there
being wealthy well- to-do Natives, who served their apprenticeship under
farmers, whose lot is being envied by their fellows ; but the real
difficulty is how our people can be brought in contact with the farmers;
but it certainly will not be by such measures as the Srop Bill or using
language such as Mr. DE WAAL’S.

IT is unusual to look to a A FARMER ON meeting of farmers for NATIVE
POLICY, any just or generous feeling towards the black man ; but
perusing a report of a Farmers’ meeting recently held at Bedford, it was
very gratifying to find, among the well- known diatribes against
everything Native, that two gentlemen were not afraid to stand up and
maintain the cause of justice and fairplay so precious to cultured
Englishmen and other en­lightened men. The subject under dis­cussion was
Natives on Juries ; and we are particularly struck with the liberal
views enunciated by Mr. F. King, in which may bo found tho real secret
of a successful Native Policy. In the course of his remarks Mr. King
observed “The Natives were very numerous, and should be made and kept
friendly and not con­verted into enemies by harassing restric­tions. A
Native man of education and good position has a right to demand to sit
on a jury.” Later on Mr. Ainslie ex­pressed a view that “ the law stated
that Natives could serve as jurors. There were intelligent Natives.” It
is to such level-headed gentlemen among Colonists that our people
naturally fall back on trustfully when mutterings of sug­gestions of
getting rid of the Natives till the air.

Question also in so far as it affects tho coloured voters. It is
refreshing to observe that in large and populous European centres like

a privi­lege which it cannot be said they have ever abused. Tho Courant
says:—“ And what is this objection [to the Ballot] ? It is the expressed
fear, that secret voting will return a different set of mon to
Parliament; the ‘ nigger vote’ is the key of the situation. This will
doubtless be made abundantly clear before the close of the session, for
we gather from Hofmeyr’s speech that the Bond party intends to introduce
some measure dealing with the franchise, and wo may be sure that its
tendency will be in the direction of still further reducing the coloured
vote. We are fully alive to the shortcomings of tho natives, and their

party or class. If the arguments that will be adduced hereafter in and
out of Parliament for restricting the coloured franchise were the
genuine expression of honest conviction, the men who have wrecked the
Ballot Bill would have been its strongest supporters, for it might
easily be made the means of eliminating the illiterate voter. But this
is not their end.”
