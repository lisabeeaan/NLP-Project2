Commissioner for the Goldfields. The post is worth £1,000 per annum.
Should the Colonel accept the position then those who find it hard to

“MOB OR PARTY.”

SUCH the other day was “the description applied by the Cape Times to the
forces in the House which should act together as an effective and
enlightened Opposition. No sooner was the observation made than the
on-lookers had an object-lesson of it. The occasion was the Logan vote
of £5,000. The Logan affair has been the trump card of the Opposition,
but they have bungled the matter so fearfully that they have not made
anything out of it— all because those who were supposed to lead the
party—or, to speak more precisely, the mob—had not the pluck and agility
of mind to summon their sympathisers— no matter to which party they
belonged— on this affair to a conclave to discuss the plan of campaign.
Thus one afternoon was witnessed the spectacle of divided and
irreconcilable counsels—Mr. Hay was for putting off the vote until the
Logan papers were discussed ; while Mr. Molteno was for a Bill and
Messrs. Robertson and Jones were for not paying; while on the other hand
Messrs. Hutton and Vincent, were for paying. The ex-Ministers, who were
waiting to see how the cat jumped, finally declared for paying. The
ex­hibition was pitiful, and could easily have been avoided by effective
and proper leadership. A meeting of those who were known to feel
strongly on this affair— and even among Bond members there were
such—could have been convened by a smart “ whip ” and an understanding
arrived at by the friends of the ex ­Ministers. For lack of this all the
dis­cussions in the House during the Session have singularly lacked
purpose.

THE LOGAN COLLAPSE.

THAT we were not alone in expecting that some Resolution would be moved
in connection with the Logan affair is clear from the following in the
Star:—“There is some thing intensely ludicrous in the collapse of the
discussion on the report of the Logan Committee. The whole of South
Africa has been looking forward to a grand and final display of
fireworks over what has been the burning question of the Cape
Parliamentary Session. The train has been laid: a whole series of
preliminary displays have been exhi­bited; and just when the crowning
shower of rockets should have come off, there is absolute silence-not
even an ignoble fizzle. The reason for this, the method in which the
powder was damped before the final display, will form an interesting
subject for speculation and discussion,”

POLITICAL INSTABILITY.

“ELECTQR” contributes the following to the Cape Mercury on Mr. Sampson’s
candidature for the repre­sentatives of Tembuland“ It has been

for long known that Mr. Victor Sampso a has cherished political
aspirations. Alice was wooed at one time—now it is Tembuland’s
affections that are sought. If I mistake not, Mr. Sampson will find his
political canvass and candidature a ques­tion of more difficulty than he
imagines. Certainly more so than the holding of a criminal brief in
Court. Unfortunately, Mr. Sampson has from the outset of his candidature
given evidence of a decided inclination to trim. But Mr. Sampson has yet
to learn that “trimming” is the one thing that Native electors will not
stand in any candidate, and they are far too astute to have dust thrown
in their eyes. This it seems to me Mr. Sampson has been trying to do,
but ineffectually. We have a specimen of this in a letter appearing in
the Imvo from Simon P. Sihlali. He says ‘ Mr. Sampson was asked by Mr.
S. Makiwane whether, if elected, he would support the Ministry or join
the Opposition, he replied that lie would go to Parliament as an
independent candi­date; yet, on the 3rd July, when addres­sing the
European electors at Cala, he said ‘ that it would be more to his own
advantage to join the Opposition, but that he would not do that, as he
was in accord with the Ministry.’ It will be hard for Mr. Sampson to
reconcile these state­ments. No sir, we have had enough of the
jelly-fish order of politician in this Colony. We want no more of them
If the jelly-fish adds ‘ trimming’ to general flabbiness, the
combination is one that should be put down at the polls ; and Mr.
Sampson, if I mistake not, will find that it will be in Tembuland,
unless he can explain matters.”

THE Government have, in our JL estimation, consulted dignity as well as
propriety in withdrawing the Glen Grey Lands’ Titles Reso­lution of
which Mr. FROST gave early notice in the late Session of Parliament.
There never was at any time any doubt that they had a majority behind
them in favour of the Resolution ; but argument upon argument was
brought to bear on them as to the inadvisability of giving Natives
Titles without some sort of restriction as to sale to European
speculators and slim land-grabbers ; or until at last the Prime Minister
was impressed by the evil of the course which some of his colleagues
would have the Cabinet take despite the earnest protestations of, and on
behalf of the Natives. Great credit, it must here be acknowledged, is
due to that old friend of our people Mr. RICHARD SOLOMON, Q C., who now
is a worthy member for Kimberley, for the brilliant oration he delivered
on this subject—a speech which had the effect of shedding much light on
a matter that was not properly understood by the House, then again,
there is good reason to believe that the action of the Natives
themselves in calling public meetings; forwarding Petitions and
Telegrams; and, above all, in sending a Deputation to Capetown,
contributed not a little to the pre­sent result; while the individual
members, friendly disposed to the interests of the aborigines of this
land, are entitled to the best thanks of these people. Yet the raising
of the question of Native Land Tenure in Glen Grey has not been in vain
although Mr. FROST’S Reso­lution has been withdrawn. It has made one or
two matters clear, which have not been clear hitherto, more particularly
in respect to Glen Grey, That the few limited areas now occupied by the
large Native population of this country in five or six divisions of the
Colony should now be kept intact for Natives, is the decided conviction
of the great majority of the people of this country of white complexion.
Now, this has not been quite clear all these years, as the policy of
taking as much land as possible from the Natives has been the duly
recognised principle of dealing with Natives’ lands We have it from Mr.
RHODES as well as from Mr. HOFMEYR that Glen Grey belongs to the Natives
absolutely ; the only question with these two great men now being how
far Natives are to be protected in this and other respects. For
instance, they hold that the protection of the Natives in the matter of
land and of drink goes hand in hand with the privi­lege to vote. We
confess we can­not see where the thing comes in. As a matter of fact
there is nothing in which the Natives have shown themselves more capable
than in picking men of the correct stamp for public affairs This
notwithstand­ing that they ask for protection in regard to lands and
drink. It is hard then to follow the Premier’s logic in this business.
Again, the fact of the withdrawal of the Glen Grey Resolution encourages
the hope that legislation must take place next Session in the direction
of settling the Native land question on the only lines reason and
pro­priety would have it settled. Whilst we place scarcely any reliance
on the capacity of the present col­leagues of the Prime Minister to
grasp such a question in a broad statesmanlike manner, we have much
faith in the ability and earnestness of Mr. RHODES himself to
distinguish himself in it. It only needs a man who is willing to take in
as much light as possible, even from the Natives themselves, in
grappling with this great and interesting problem; and the I

present Premier fortunately is not a man of the class that has only one
idea screwed in the mind and which is incapable of adjustment when
necessary. We are hopeful, therefore, the Bill to be brought in next
Session to provide the final set­tlement of the Native’s Lands will be
broad, large-hearted and sufficiently comprehensive to satisfy the great
Native population of this country as well the sense of justice of the
people who are still true to the prin­ciples of freedom and right the
home of which is Great Britain.

THE Session has closed, and no definite decision has been arrived at by
Parliament on the great question which palled over
everything—the LOGAN Business which precipitated a Ministry to a fall.
That a Ministry should come to grief and the Parliament should proceed
to the transaction of business as if nothing had happened is, to a
discerning political observer, a strange phenomenon. It does not argue
much for a proper comprehension of the principles of Responsible
Government by our public men, and vicariously by the electorate of
this country, that so singular a thing should pass unobserved. It is
interesting to trace a little the course of events in Parliament which
has led to so heartless a
burlesque. On the day the representatives of the people assembled
Ministerial and ex-Ministerial statements were made as to the rupture
which had taken place over the LOGAN affair. But instead of the
ex-Ministers and their friends and sympathizers arranging at some caucus
to propose some definite resolution on which to test the feeling of
Parliament on this grave subject, they neglected assembling themselves
beforehand, and were thus at the mercy of clever schemers
who knew exactly what they were about. The result was that they were
checkmated. Sir JAMBS SIVE-WRIGHT, without even a solitary friend in the
House at the time—
for Mr. DOUGLASS had not then declared himself, as he has since done, on
the side of the slim Knight —with an affectation of injured innocence,
resorted to the recognised Parliamentary subterfuge of
getting rid of an unsavorv subject in the House. He moved for a
reference of the matter to a Select Committee, although no body knew
what it was that was not already before the public in Correspondence and
evidence before the Supreme Court in the LOGAN Case that the Committee
was to bring forth ; and how the Select Committee was to dispose of the
judgment of the Court already referred to. The reference was agreed to;
and we all know the result. The Report,
if it brought anything to light, served to show up discrepancies in Mr.
LOGAN’S evidence—a matter not relevant to the issue. Then, when it
should have been considered, and a resolution of the House
proposed that might very properly have reflected upon the Cabinet
disruption,—Mr. HOFMEYR, who had charge of the matter, took ill. Now,
Mr. HOFMEYR is fast acquiring the reputation of being “ the sick man” of
Cape politics. It does not require any stretch of the memory to calico
mind that he pleaded illness — it was then “ bad eyesight ”—for not
taking
office in 1884. Mr. HAY tried hard to ferret the Report from the depths
of the Business Paper. All in vain. His voice was as of one crying in
the wilderness when he
protested against the burking of the discussion in this way. The Session
was in its last gasp when Mr. HOFMEYR again reappeared, apparently well
to deliver an oration on the perennial subject of “ Poor
Whites” but not strong enough to tackle the LOGAN Report. The outcome of
all these devices has been that the matter has blown over and the
friends of the ex-
Ministers are out manoeuvred. We submit it would not have ended thus in
a fizzle if members were not allowed, in treating themselves to the
variety of enjoyments
afforded by the First Club in the country, to forget their duties to
their constituents by demanding from the Premier a full, true and an
exact account of how it came
about that by an uncommon devotion to the person of the errant member
for Griqualand East he should have sacrificed the three ablest members
of the House. The opportunity has been allowed to pass by, but the
incident reflects no credit on the representative institutions of the
country. We can only hope the country will do something to vindicate its
good name for a proper appreciation of right
and wrong by returning to the next Parliament representatives who will
have a better knowledge of their duties.
