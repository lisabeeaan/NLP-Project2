CURRENT OPINION.

THE PRESS ON THE FRANCHISE.

No. HI.

In its issue of the 15th inst. the East London Dispatch again deals with
the Franchise amendment question, and closes its article with the remark
that “ There promises to be no end to the requirements of the Bond until
its own political position is predominant—and then? Well, then Mr.
Hofmeyr may possibly have recovered the eyesight which tailed him a few
years back.”

We have yet to learn, says the Bedford Enterprise, that the possession
of broad acres renders a man better able to legis­late for the good of
his country, any more than the possession of money bags; though we admit
it is a good plank in the old conservative platform. At present,
however, and subject to revision when more light has been shed on the
subject, we incline to an educational test as the fitter qualification
for the franchise; but here again there would be no finality— indeed
that seems to be the great difficul­ty in this business, and it will
never do to be constantly tinkering.

The opinion of the Potchefstroom Bud­get is thus expressed:—Wescarcely
think that Mr. Hofmeyr can seriously entertain a scheme so manifestly
unjust. Of one thing we are certain, that, between this and next
session, he will have the op­portunity of learning how utterly at
variance it is with the feeling of colonists; how unjust it is in its
encroachments upon the freedom of electoral rights; and how important it
must prove to remove the evil he seeks to remedy. The danger which
threatens Cape electorates every colonist can appreciate, and any just
attempt to assimilate the qualifications o those of the neighbouring
States will meet with their approval; but electoral preferences such as
Mr. Hofmeyr sug­gests will be rejected by the good sense of those with
whom ho will have to reason.

The Natal Mercury thus gives its own opinion “ We are not surprised that
the measure proposed by Mr. Hofmeyr evoked vigorous opposition.
Plurality of votes is a principle more and more condemned by the
sentiment and usage of the age, and when associated, as proposed, with
the principle of increased representation for property, it is bound to
be mercilessly criticised It will be no easy matter for Mr. Rhodes and
his colleagues to give anything more than an abstract assent to this
proposition. The Cape is handicapped by two conflicting conditions:—(1)
That the European population will tolerate no restriction of the
franchise so far as they are concerned ; and (2) that the Imperial
Government will not permit any direct disqualification on the ground of
“colour.” Mr. Hofmeyr’s complete recognition of this difficulty led him
to devise a middle course, in the shape of plurality vote in favour of
property and education. That, however, is as we have said, a principle
altogether at variance with the spirit of the age. Our impression is
that this great and crucial question is one that will have to be dealt
with by the future Parliament of the South African Union, a political
entity that will be strong enough to insist upon its own right to deal
with every question of domestic polity in its own way. What that way may
be it is premature to forecast; but we do not despair that a way may be
found which shall protect the country from the evils of an
indiscriminate and harmful coloured vote, without debarring from the
rights of citizenship natives who may be fairly and safely entrusted
with those privileges. With the experience of North America staring us
in the face, it would be folly to shut our eyes to the possible results
of absolute race equality in the exercise of political rights ; but, on
the other hand, it is not less our duty to act justly by those members
of the native population who may have earned their title to a superior
civil status by conformity to, and acceptance of, the duties and
obligations of civil citizenship. In any case the question is one that
commands the earnest thought of every patriotic politician, and Mr.
Hofmeyr is to be thanked for having directed attention to it.

THE PRESS ON THE SESSION.

The Port Elizabeth Telegraph writes:— There was very little fighting
indeed, and at no time were the Ministry placed in the slightest danger.
The attacks made upon them, indeed, were apparently devised more with
the idea of galvanizing the straggling Opposition forces into a little
life rather than with any hope of bringing about a political crisis. At
the opening of Parliament the Opposition were sadly in need of a
stimulant. Sir Gordon Sprigg, like Achilles of old, sulked in his tent.
He appeared to keep himself apart from his own followers, and never
ventured to harass those in office. What little fighting that was done
partook of the nature of guerilla warfare. A brilliant speech by Sir
Thomas Upington, followed perhaps by a ponderous oration from Colonel
Scherm­brucker, and there the matter ended. Such a state of things was
not healthy, and the public generally heartily wel­comed the ex-Premier
back in the arena when, to use the expressive language of Mr. Merriman,
he Hopped off the hedge. During the latter portion of the session Sir
Gordon displayed his old readiness to engage, but he lacked the vigour
and determination which characterised his younger political days.
Perhaps the obstructive tactics of the Colonel worried him or he left
the impracticability of making any impression on the solid forces of the
Government, or what is even more likely, he found it impossible in his
heart of hearts to fight against a party which was carrying out the very
policy he had previously supported.
