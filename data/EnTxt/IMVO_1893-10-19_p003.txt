THE NATAL MINISTRY.

THE Ministry formed by Sir John Robinson in Natal is an exceptionally
able one. It was pro­claimed last week as follows Sir John Robinson
(Premier and Colonial Secre­tary), G. M. Sutton (Treasurer), Escombe
(Attorney-General), F. R. Moor (Secre­tary for Native Affairs), T. K.
Murray (Public Works).

CANON GIBSON’S PREFERMENT.

KAFFRARIAN Churchmen will be delighted to hear that Rev, Canon Alan G.
S, Gibson, MA, of the Diocese of S. Johns has received the appointment
of Coadjutor Bishop of Capetown. The unselfish and self-sacri­ficing
love of Canon Gibson for the cause of Christ is simply limitless, and he
has accepted the Coadjutor Bishopric no doubt because it will offer him
a wider sphere of advancing his Master’s cause. We wish him all possible
success in the new work to which he is now called.

THE “TICKLISH POINT.”

THERE has, under the title “A Ticklish Point,” been in circulation, in
some of the Colonial newspapers, an excerpt from the proceedings of the
Tylden Farmers’ Association. The matter deserves attention at our hands.
It is as follows :—

The Secretary read a communication from Mr. A. Francis, Convener of
Dele­gates, suggesting that Mr. Tengo-Jabavu be permitted to represent
the Native electors.

Mr. McDonald said he should certainly oppose this. If we are going to
permit this, why not have the Natives in our houses and under the same
blanket as ourselves. He considered it a cowardly way of fighting the
Bond. When the English found that the Dutch had formed the Bond, they
extended the franchise to the Natives ; but still he maintained that the
latter thought more of the Dutch, and that they did far better under the
Dutch rule. He blamed England—Gladstone and Exeter Hall—for all our
ills. He moved, “ That this Association is opposed to Jabavu taking any
part in the pro­ceedings of Conference,”

Mr. W, Forward proposed, and Mr. J, W, Filmer seconded, “ That we accede
to the request contained in Mr. Francis’ letter.” Mr. Forward said that
he was sorry to see such opposition against a reasonable request. There
are 800 Native voters, and they are entitled to be repre­sented, and we
are asked to accept as their representative an educated man, the editor
of a newspaper. He hoped they would not be so narrow-minded as to refuse
this.

Almost every member spoke either for or against, the result being that
Mr. McDonald’s resolution was carried by one vote.

Proposed by Mr. Lewis, seconded by Mr. W, Filmer, “That Mr. McDonald be
one of our delegates to Conference,”

  The former was elected.

Now, in the electoral Division of Queenstown there are a number of
Farmers’ Associations. To these, also, the proposal of Mr. FRANCIS, to
allow a presentative of the Native electors on the Conference to
nominate candidates for the district, has been submitted ; and been by
all agreed to save by theTylden As­sociation. That in the demurring body
the opposition should have carried their point by u majority of one is
significant. Of course, in this matter, as in others, the wishes of the
majority must prevail. So the representation of the Natives on the
Conference has been provided for. It is worthy of attention that such
provision should have been the result of the spontaneous action of the
European Farmers themselves ; no Native having suggested the adoption of
this course, as none could even have dreamt that, even if so fair and
reasonable a thing were suggested by a Native, it c mid pass through the
various Farmer^’ As­sociations. Yet now it is an accomplished fact; and
one which does the farmers of Queenstown, Cathcart, Tarkastad and Glen
Grey infinite credit. It shows more clearly than anything else lh.it
this class of the community is being seized of the greatest fact,
bearing on the foundation of the true pros­perity and happiness of the
people of this country—the cordial co-operation of all, in their various
spheres, to make this a worthy country; and one which would be a credit
to all who live in it. The secret of the success of all whose lot has
been cast in this Ind by Providence lies in each class taking the
greatest possible interest in the prosperity and advancement of the
other; and manifestations of goodwill towards the Natives such as may be
seen in this generous act of the white farmers, are sure to be
appreciated and heartily recipro­cated by our people. In this is to be
found the true solution of the many difficulties of the Native Question;
and it will ever be remembered to the honour of the Farmers in the
Division of Queens­town that they led the way in showing by acts
friendliness to­wards these people.

Whether the Natives will avail themselves of the invitation of the
Fanners, to send a representative to the Conference for nominating two
progressive men to be supported by all, remains to be seen. They

must, of course, meet and consider the matter. We have confidence in the
clear-headedness of our people, however, that they will not spurn so
splendid an opportunity of assisting their country and their district by
combining with their friends to the mutual advantage of all. The mention
of Mr. TENGO- JABAVU’S name as representative of the Natives on the
Conference was, no doubt, premature, seeing the Native voters of
Queenstown had not proceeded to the election of a delegate, and it is
not clear how the name came to be used at the Tylden meeting.
Nevertheless its owner cannot be insensible of the kindness of gentlemen
like Messrs. FORWARD and J. W. FILMER who stood at the breach when
coarse and gratuitous abuse was levied at it by political incendiaries
like Mr. J. MACDONALD.

Chartered Companies of recent

late SALISBURY Ministry, it was
hoped and believed that it would
be all fair sailing; and collision
with Native potentates was regarded as a very remote contingency.
The Government, in these circumstances, does not appear to have
attached to the question of wars,
which might be brought about by
the Companies, the importance
which now compels attention. Nor
does it seem to have been foreseen
that the zeal of the Companies
might even involve England in

remark upon is the

complete transformation of

opinion which the
news from. England, glanced from
newspapers and from Cable intelligence, shows to have taken place

policy of war. We have been
trained to regard the Tory party

MCCARTHY in his history of “ Our
Own Times,” states the word
“ Jingo ” was coined at a Conservative Club. What, however, do we

land to engage in warlike pursuits;
and that even before it had been
made clear beyond the shadow of a
doubt that the Matebeles were bent
on fighting. In the Press it is Liberal
journals like the Daily News which
encourage the war party in this
country on the flimsiest of pretexts
ever heard of. While on the
other hand respectable organs of
Tory opinion like The Times
are firmly for a cautious policy.
The discussions in the House
of Commons also go to prove
that it is the Conservatives who
have not lost their heads over this
Matebele business. Sir JOHN
GORST’S appeals a few weeks back
to Mr. SIDNEY BUXTON in favour

still ringing in our ears; and the
remarks of Sir GEORGE BADEN-
POWELL in the Commons, brought
out by the last mail, fairly express
what seems to be the dominating
feeling in Conservative circles at
home. The honourable gentleman

is the only true description

  that can be given to the bold and
  magnificent utterances of Mr. S. C.

CRONWRIGHT directed to the country in general through the medium of the
Cradock Farmers’ Association, and printed in extenso in the Midland
News. It is one of the few deliverances one meets with in this country
which render people to shake off their lethargy and indifference in
matters pertain­ing to their welfare; and will have, we doubt not, a
great effect in kindling a glowing patriotism in all who are earnestly
seeking the prosperity of the land. Mr. CRON- WRIGHT’S paper on “
Political Ethics, and Political Organisation ” will prove an arsenal
from which many a noble and ennobling idea will be taken by the electors
during the forthcoming political campaign. We hope it may be printed in.
pamphlet form and widely and freely circulated throughout the
constituencies not yet under the blighting influence of the Bond.
Everything dealt with has been treated in a healthy, vigorous and able
way. In clarion tones it summons all electors who have no faith in the
doings of the Bond to stand up like men, and shoulder to shoulder to
purge Parliament of the political parasites who are for temporising with
and resting political power—even as wire­pullers—on the Bond any longer.
On this candidates are to take sides and be fought vigorously. The end
is a glorious one and is bound to be crowned with success. This cry
taken up by the ex-Ministers especially throughout the land will result
in the return of a compact party in the next Parliament that will
present a strong front to the Bond and to their pot-lickers.
Considerable discussion must take place on Mr. CRONWRIGHT’S Paper; but
to-day, for lack of space, we must content ourselves with these
necessarily curtailed observations.

[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

SIR,—In your sub-leader of the 4th you point to the fact that if the
coloured voters only combined, they would be able to effect wonders at
the coming Parliamentary elections, more particular­ly in Western
constituencies. In Stellen­bosch, for instance, (represented by that
arch-Bondman, Mr. Hofmeyr) they com­mand 900 votes as against only 750
purely Dutch ones.

These are the figures given by you, Mr. Editor. But the situation at
Stellen­bosch is even more favourable to sup­posed coloured aspirations
than these figures indicate. For while the whole of the 909 voters given
in the Census returns as coloured are coloured, all of the 751 called
“European or white” in those returns, are not “purely Dutch” or Bond.
Firstly, There are a considerable number of half-whites among them who,
politically, are one in spirit with the coloured section of the
population. Secondly, There are many English shop­keepers, artisans and
farmers, as well as German missionaries among them, who repudiate the
Bond and would be very likely to support the coloured voters in
returning anti-Bondmen. Thirdly, Even among the Dutch farmers of
Stel­lenbosch the Bond is a very weak an emasculated body. At the last
Bond Congress the Stellenbosch branch was unrepresented, and rumour has
it that it has since collapsed altogether. Fourthly, Mr. Hofmeyr is
suspected by several men of his party of lukewarmness in such matters as
the Strop Bill, the proposed National Bank. &c. Fifthly, No special or
spontaneous effort is being made at Stellenbosch in Mr. Hofmeyr’s behalf
and he himself is said to have declined to exert himself to gain the
favour of lukewarm voters. Sixthly, There are already three other local
candidates in the field, all of them influential and exten­sively
connected by family and business ties with the constituency; and meeting
with considerable support among the Dutch farmers.

Should the coloured voters, with any­thing like unanimity, agree to vote
for any particular two of these three candi­dates, such two would be
returned by an overwhelming majority, and the Bond leader would find
himself at the bottom of the poll. No wonder that Mr. Hofmeyr last
session expressed some doubt as to his being in the House after the
ensuing elections.
