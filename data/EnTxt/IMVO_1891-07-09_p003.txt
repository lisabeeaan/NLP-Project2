Native Opinion

  FTEN, since the Session com-
  menced, have Ministers in-

formed Parliament of an intention
next year to introduce a measure
dealing with the Government of the
Natives. As to the direction such
a measure should take, Natives hold
ideas definite enough ; but as their
views do not count in these matters,
one has simply to go on puzzling
his mind as to the nature of the
Government Bill. Be the nature
of the proposal what it will, we
are naturally filled with anxiety,
because no matter the spirit in
which laws affecting Natives are
conceived, they often fail in essential
respects by reason of their being
enacted in ignorance of the real
circumstances of the people. To
this cause is due the obvious failure
of legislation directed at Natives,
evidenced in the enactment and re-
enactment, session after session, of
measures to deal with Locations,
which have no sooner been enacted,
and those who were engaged in
framing them returned to their
homes in the hope that they had
made ample provision for the evils
which had called such laws into exis-
tence, than they find they had
somewhat missed their aim, and
must deal with the same difficulty
de novo. Such being the experience
in respect to such legislation in the
past, all we can look forward to, fail-
ing the adoption of some radical
mode to deal with Native matters,
is that yet another experiment is
to be tried on our people, the result
of which may only serve to perplex
and depress them, even more than
has been the case in the past.
Speaking of the adoption of radical
modes, modes out of the beaten-track,
of dealing with Native affairs, we
should not blink the fact that
hitherto legislation affecting these
people has been directed from the
point of view of the Europeans ;
and laws made or unmade for the
Natives as they appeared suitable
or unsuitable from the stand-point
of the dominant race. Of this we
should be the last to complain, pro-
vided the laws met the case. As
this Locations business shows, they
have so far signally failed.
Hence our present contention that
as much as possible our governors
should now try to look at matters

  through the eyes of the Natives
  themselves; and proceed gradually,

  as a recent writer in the Cape Times
  contended recently, from what the
  Native can understand to that which
  he does not know. Any other
  course is sure to bewilder, con-
  found, and confuse him, rendering
  his last state worse than the first,
  as past legislation has, in a measure,
  done. For this purpose Govern-
  ment might do worse than to adopt
  measures during the recess calcu-
  lated to ask the people themselves
  as to the effect of proposals affect-
  ing them, as also eliciting their
  views on what they might deem
  conducive to their advancement.
  We think we can quite see the way
  to obtain the real feeling of the
  people in these matters, while they
  might naturally shrink from giving
  it frankly, and with the necessary
  candour, if it were asked through
  those to whom they look as their
  rulers.

IT

A SATISFACTORY SETTLEMENT.

Announcing the satisfactory settlement of tho Glen Grey business the
Cape Mercury writes :—

The Glen Grey difficulty is ended. It arose out of a Naboth’s vineyard
feeling, the Dutch having cast longing eyes on a piece of country which
the Native, make better use of than would those who wish to obtain it.
The Census has put things right. There are over thirty-three thou­sand
people living on the ground, and it had been represented that there were
only some twenty thousand souls. There is just lan«l enough for those
settled upon it, and everybody we have seen who has visited the
settlement speaks in high terms of the amount of cultivation, and the
general progress of the Natives there. We are glad that nothing more
will be heard of the question.

We commend the healthy tone of the English Press of the Colony on the
pro­posals that have been thrown out in certain quarters to rid Africa
of its aboriginal inhabitants so as to make it wholly and absolutely a
white man’s land. On this great subject the Bedford Enterprise writes:—

The panacea for our trouble recom­mended by the ^Tohamiesburg Star is
hardly^likely to -provo* acceptable even to the more inveterate haters
of natives. The barbarous suggestion is, in brief, that the natives
should be poisoned off by bad cheap brandy. Colonists have no wish to
commit wholesale murder, even to get rid of a problem which may prove
unsolvable to the present generation. Nor will the remedy suggested by
the Bloemfontein Express prove more accept­able to any but the mere
rabid negro- phobist. That no natives should be allowed to own land, is
such an unjust pro­position that it cannot be seriously dis­cussed

Let other measures which may be con­sistent with our profession as
Christians be tried ; but not let us adopt any in­human or unjust
suggestion, of which we must be ashamed, and which might possibly
precipitate the very evil it is intended to avert.
