HAVING called special attention to what we considered a lamentable
failure of justice at the Magistrate’s Court at Alice in connection with
the Registration, we reproduce the following observations directed to
the same Magistracy made by the Crown Prosecutor and the Judge at the
recent Circuit Court held at Fort Beaufort. It is as well that these
things should be brought to the knowledge of Government who must be held
res­ponsible in case the administration of justice falls into disrepute.
We depend upon the Fort Beaufort Advocate for the following:—“ Mlenzana
was indicted for stealing two sheep, the property of Mr. G. Attwell, at
Battlesden, in the Victoria East district on the 3rd February.—Mr.
Advocate Tamplin defended.—Soon after opening the case Mr. Solomon
informed his lordship that there was no official present from Alice :
that the record was in a most disgraceful condition, and that he could
make but little of it. Two witnesses were then called but did not answer
to their names.—Mr. Solomon remarked that the witness who could identify
the sheep was not in court, and he did not even know that he had been
subpoened. There being some evidence, the case went to the jury, but
this evidence was rebutted by Mr. Tamplin who called witnesses to prove
that the sheep were the prisoner’s property.—His lordship, in summing
up, also pointed out that the Chief Constable in the subpoena on one of
the witnesses returned that the man had cleared with his own horse and
somebody else’s saddle and bridle. A very curious return for a Chief
Constable to make. The prisoner was acquited.”

MR. STANFORD’S reading of the 10th Section of the Constitution is the
same as Major Elliot’s. He admits that he would be impressed by the
contention that, the Natives had been naturalised by the Acts of
Annexation that made them British subjects, if there was not the
reservation in the 10th Section about the Batavian subjects. This
reservation, as we took occasion to point out when commenting on Major
Elliot’s judgment, is of no conse­quence whatsoever as regards the
Natives. It has served its purpose in so far as it has defined, under
our Constitution, the rights of subjects of the Batavian Govern­ment,
who were taken over by the British Government when the Colony changed
hands under the Treaty of Paris. Were this proviso not inserted, it is
plain that those who were natural-born subjects of the Batavian
Government, who, forthat reason, owe allegiance to it still, would need
to take out letters of naturalisation to be entitled to their present
privileges under the British Government, and this would have been a
manifest hardship. One has only to undertake something more than a”
superficial reading of the 10th Section,—read, in fact, works on
Constitutional law, and decided cases in point,—to remove all doubts as
to the effectual naturalisation of the Natives for all practical
purposes.

SEEING that the Magistrates have arrived at their decisions, on the
question of the Natives being aliens, with such evident hesitation, is
it not against the spirit of our law to deprive people of rights? Would
it not have been more in accordance with English jurisprudence to have
allowed the Natives the benefit of the doubt?

THE Umzimkulu native vote, estimated at 1,400 has been swept away as so
many cobwebs by Mr. Zeitsman’s Committee, Mr. Zeitsman himself assisted
by Mr. Jones, acting as Attorney. The tedium of going through the list
of the 1,157 names objected to was obviated by making six test cases
typical of the nature of the objections.” (1) Thomas Mkize, born in
Natal after the annexation of that colony objected to on the score of
not being a natural-born subject. The decision of the Chief Magistrate,
Mr. W. E. Stanford, was that claims of this character should be
accepted. (2) James Nyangiwe, born in Tembuland before annexation,
objected to for not being a natural-born subject. Mr. Morris argued that
he had been naturalised by the an­nexation act while residing at
Umzimkulu as subject of Adam Kok. He quoted Ficher’s common Law p. 1047.
Mr. Zeitsman argued on the wording of the 10th Section of the
Constitution, and maintained that the Act of Annexation makes no
reference to naturalisation of natives. Mr. Stanford’s judgment was, “
It has been argued that the Act of An­nexation is sufficient to
naturalize, as by the operation of constitutional law”; when a country
is annexed the people resident in it become thereby naturalized. Against
this there is the fact that it was found necessary in the Constitution
Or­dinance to provide for the Batavian subjects. That seems to me to
prove that annexation was not sufficient in their case, and therefore
cannot be in this. James Nyangiwe’s name for these reasons must be
struck off the list of voters.” (3) Mtshololo, born in Natal before
Annexation, was rejected not being a subject within meaning of the 10th
Section. (4) Msingapantsi, born in East Griqualand before the
annexation, ditto.

(5) Umbhuqelwa, a tenant on Mr. Donald Strachan's farm, objected to on
    tribal and communal tenure. Mr. Stanford de­cided that Mr.
    Strachan’s property could not come under the designation, tribal and
    communal, and admitted all of claimants type, if otherwise
    qualified.

(6) Nondabula, one of a tribe whose chief holds title to a farm in trust
    for his clan, objected to on tribal and communal tenure, in Mr.
    Strachan’s opinion it is not held on tribal tenure as these people
    bought the farm. To simplify matters, it was recommended to
    Government that title should be in the name of one of those who
    purchased the farm. It was clear to the Chief Magistrate, that in
    this last case, tribal tenure, within the meaning of the 17th
    Clause, was proved. Nondabula’s claim was therefore not accepted. In
    all these cases Mr. Morris, as representing the people noted an
    appeal to the Supreme Court. When the hearing of the appeals will
    come off, we can’t say. The Natives have no reason to dread them ;
    they have on the contrary every reason to welcome the decision of
    the higher courts, for as things are, they are the victims of
    inter­pretations of the statute and constitu­tional law by
    Magistrates whose decisions, have been as varied as there are
    various divisions in which objections have been heard. Since the
    above was written we have learnt that the appeal has been withdrawn
    so as to facilitate the complet­ing of the register, and enable the
    election to go on forthwith as Parliament sits in a few weeks time.
