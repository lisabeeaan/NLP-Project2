VALUE OF LAND.

  T will be in the recollection of
  the readers of this journal

that about the beginning of the
present year land values in the
Kingwilliamstown Division offered
a subject for much bitter comment
in the local Press. The cause was
the decision of the Civil Commis-
sioner of the district in connection
with the Registration cases heard
before him, which placed a ridicu-
lously low value on the landed pro-
perty of the Natives. It so happens
that the Civil Commissioner of
Kingwilliamstown for the time
being is also Registrar of Deeds
for Kaffraria, and has to present a
report to the Government every
year giving an account of the
transactions of the Deeds office.
Now, as Civil Commissioner, Mr.
HOLLAND pronounced a decision on
the claims of the Natives which in
effect reduced the value of the land
of Natives’ holdings from £22
10s. set on it by the Divisional
Council a few years buck, to £6 or
£7 apiece. We have never been
able to understand how so abnormal
a fall of property in Kaffraria oc-
curred, and consequently did
nothing to conceal our dissatisfac
tion at what was done on that
occasion. Great, therefore, has
been our delight on receiving from
the Government the Deed’s Reports
for the year, and we read with
much avidity that of Mr. HOLLAND,
as Registrar of Deeds of the local
establishment, in which we find
the opinion recorded that ''on the
“ whole the value of landed pro-
“ perty in Kaffraria has not alone
“ improved, but there is also a
“ greater demand for the same.”
The italics are our own, resorted to
with a view to fix attention to what
we maintain to be the confirmation
of our opinion expressed in connec-
tion with the value of our people’s
lands two or three months ago; and
to this day we have not been able
to understand the improvement of
the values of property from the
high value of £22 to £6 ; except it
be on the principle of what is
known as “ Irish promotion ”

N-E. CONTEST.

THE contest for the seat in the Legislative Council vacated by the death
of Mr. MICHAU in connection with the representation of the North Eastern
Circle, promises to be a lively and an in­teresting one. The Circle is
made up of the electoral divisions of Somerset East, Fort Beaufort, Cra-
dock, Colesberg, and Albert; and at the general election last year Sir
HENRY STOCKENSTROOM, Mr. D. VAN- DEN HEEVER, and Mr MICHAU were returned
after a hotly contes­ted election, in which the Bond were struggling to
secure the three seats allotted to the area in question. Their nominees
then were Messrs VAN DEN HEEVER, the lamented member Mr. W. J. MICHAU,
and Mr. F. F. WIENAND ; while a school of electors, which was called
into existence by the exigencies of the moment under the name of
In­dependent, in contradistinction to the Bond, put forward Sir HENRY
STOCKENSTROOM, and returned him at the head of the poll, by the aid of
the cumulative vote, to Mr. WIE­NAND’S detriment. We had hoped the
Independents would, seeing they had Sir HENRY, have been content to let
Mr. WIENAND in without a contest. They appear, however, to be eager to
carry on the struggle to the bitter end; and Bondmen in (he
North-Eastern Circle are to be allowed no quarter. By arrange­ment with
competitors who were ready to come forward, Mr. PHILIP WATERMEYER, who
formerly sat for Colesberg in the Assembly for many years, and was
turned adrift on the advent of the Bond, has been put forward by the
Independents, and is now stumping the constitu­ency. Mr. WATERMEYER’S
pre­vious Parliamentary record is that of a fairly able and enlightened
member, who could always be de­pended upon to advance the cause of the
party to which he was at­tached in the House. In his day he was closely
allied to the SPRIGG party ; and consequently we were no admirers of his
political methods It must not be forgotten that the creed of some of the
erst­

while followers of Sir GORDON SPRIGG has undergone such a change that it
would be cruel to tax them now with the actions of those bygone times ;
and with the exception of a recent speech de­claiming against the RHODES
Gov­ernment in connection with the Wesselton mine, hardly anything has
escaped Mr. WATERMEYER’S lips from which one could ascer­tain the drift
of his opin­ions lately. But Ons Land seems to know all about Mr.
WATERMEYER, and tells the world that during his retirement at Hanover he
has been oscillating between Independence and Bondism in the hope of
effecting a return to Parliamentary life. If this be correct it will
damage Mr. WATERMEYER’S candidature with many an honest Independent bent
upon coping with the Bond; and his prospects of success, which at best
were anything but good, will be exceedingly small. We trust Mr.
WATERMEYER may be able without delay to clear himself from the
imputation of conduct so flagitious as is imputed to him by Ons Land,
otherwise he goes into the contest doomed to failure, as many who would
under the circumstances have voted for him in the faith that lie was a
genuine Independent will under the circumstances abstain from voting.
Nevertheless he should be supported, if for no other reason, at all
events, because he could be depended upon to vote on the right side
should a contest for the mas­tery be precipitated by the Bond.

200 horse­men. He was received by the boys out­side the gate. who sang a
glee as a wel­come. At 11 o'clock the Bishop of S. John’s took the chair
at the public meet- ing. He asked for £200 to clear the debt off the
building and for milch cows for the boys. Ho was supported by the
valuable presence of the respected Chief Magistrate, Major Elliott,
C.M.G. In a speech of great weight the Major pointed out the value of
education, stating his belief that the intellectual power of the Native
was equal to that of any other in the race of life, and calling for
support to the missionaries who came amongst them seeking nothing but
their welfare, and often at a personal loss. In the givings after, it
was clear that Major Elliot had done very much to influence his hearers
and much was given as a direct consequence of his presence and speech.
Mrs. Elliot also graced the scene. Canon Waters and Rev. John Xabi were
the chief speakers, and they spoke in excellent form and with good
effect. Finally, promises to the amount ot £75 were taken. 2 Oxen had
been killed for the visitors of whom there were 350, and a very pleasant
day passed away without a hitch. A missionary meeting at which old
Theological students spoke was held in the evening and warmed the hearts
of those present.

THE following is what the DITTO. Port Elizabeth Telegraph says on the
same subject: “ We regret to find that the Government are again stirring
up the Glen Grey Native Question. Disguise it as they may the movement
proceeds from a number of farmers on the frontier, who are actuated by
the desire of possessing the lands upon which certain of the Natives
were located, or located themselves at the close of the last Kafir war.
Whether they ought to have been allowed there at the time is not now a
question worth dis­cussing. There they have been for a number of years
in peaceful possession, and certainly have more right to the land than
any other section of colonists. It would be not only impolitic but cruel
now to remove these Natives. The Government has appointed a Commission,
consisting of Messrs. Holland, Frost, and Van Rensbarg, to enquire into
and report upon the matter, a id whatever their re­commendations may be
we trust that the Parliament will see justice done to these people. The
best way would be to grant individual titles to the Natives.”

THE Cape Times has PECCAVIMUS. come down on us rather mercilessly. It
says.— “The support of Imvo, representing the political Natives is given
to the Govern­ment franchise proposals for reasons which would have been
better left un­stated. It there should be a party sufficiently
courageous to maintain that no reason has been shown for meddling with
the franchise just now, Imvo will be with that party. But “failing
this,” says our Native contemporary, “ we have no option but to fall
back upon the com­promise which, maintaining the status quo, proceeds to
legislate for posterity of which we know nothing and care less.” This
would indeed be a shocking princi­ple of legislation. We know nothing of
posterity, it is true, but patriotic men Care as much for their
successors as for their contemporaries. What would our Eng­lish nation
have been if the founders of schools and colleges had been of Imvo's
mind? William of Wykeham, our Henry whose holy shade grateful science
adores, Wolsey, Colet—how would such men have shuddered at the thought
of caring nothing for the generations to follow them! After this
confession of indif­ference to the permanent effect of the coming
legislation, the Government should abandon the saving clause in favour
of those now on the register, and apple the purge boldly, unreservedly
and at once. We shall see then how the Imvo folk like being treated as
they are willing that their sons shall be treated.
