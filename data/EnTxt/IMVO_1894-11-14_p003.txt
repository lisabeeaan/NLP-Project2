Topics of the Day.

Taking Votes Away.

From reports that have come to hand from Glen Grey it would seem that
Mr. Jenner’s work in that unhappy district has not been so much the
demarca­tion of the locations as the remov­ing Native voters’ names from
the existing lists. So Mr. Rhodes appears as one who begrudges the
Natives of the shadowy privilege of representation; but he does not see
that he is strength­ening their case for appealing to the Mother
Country. As the voting power Natives had was of no very great value to
them, Mr. Rhodes’ vindictiveness seems beside the question ; and, to say
the least, small and puerile.

It is with exceeding regret that we learn of Bishop Knight-Bruce’s
retire­ment from the glorious missionary field he had engaged in in
Mashonaland after a noble but short career. Being a hard worker in the
Lord’s vineyard he could not but break his health. Both dioceses over
which he presided since he came to Africa were vast and, strictly
speaking, unbounded. The tone and missionary spirit by which he was
characterized showed that he used his best endeavours to discharge the
sacred duties of his high office as Bishop. He was a true missionary
Bishop, and a philosopher. To the Basutos he was a father.

Interesting to the Tembus.

An important and remarkable meet­ing took place at the Chief
Dalindyebo’s “great place” in Tembuland on Wed­nesday last, 7th
November, to which the people were called by the paramount Tembu Chief,
as also the neighbouring Wesleyan Methodist minister—Rev Wm. S. Davis.
It transpired that the Chief desired to make the announcement that he
had fully and finally resolved to dis­sociate himself with the
religio-political movement which has for some years past attracted
attention in Tembuland ; and that he desired to cast in his lot with the
Methodist body in which he was born. His defection previously was due to
want of experience as a youth. He wished the Missionary to conduct
services at his place, and that a teacher be placed with him. The
declaration clears the Tembu chief from the suspi­cions that have long
attached to him, even in political circles, from his con­nection with
the Tile movement, and we sincerely pray that he may himself and his
people receive the highest good from being “under the wing” of the
Missionaries.

  Fun.

  There are two publications in this country which go by the same name—
  the Express of Bloemfontein, and the Christian Express of Lovedale.
  Their politics on certain subjects of moment are as different as the
  poles asunder. Hence the following comment from our friend the East
  London Standard:—“ We should feel obliged if our contemporary, the
  Imvo, when discussing anything taken from the Christian Express,
  pub­lished at Lovedale, would kindly give that paper’s designation in
  full. For we had got nearly to the end of a leading article in a
  former paper the other day. largely consisting of extracts from the
  Express, all the while filled with aston­ishment at what seemed to
  exhibit the dawn of a regenerate mind on the part of our Bloemfontein
  contemporary of that name. But when we came to a strong plea for ‘ the
  forces that make for righteousness ’ we knew there must be some
  mistake somewhere, and, search­ing the columns of Imvo more narrowly,
  found that it was the Lovedale paper that was quoted from ! The
  disappoint­ment was great.

  Faithful Wounds.

  It has been said “ Faithful are the wounds of a friend,” and so appear
  to us to be the observations of the South African Review (Capetown) on
  that part of Mr. Sauer’s speech, which bears upon the wretched George
  election. It will be seen that the S. A. Review is quite of the
  opinion expressed in these columns —an opinion shared also by the
  Eastern Province Herald—that when Mr. Searle, for want of backbone,
  retired from the contest, those of the late Mr. Vintcent’s way of
  thinking should forthwith have put forward a candidate,
  notwithstand­ing the fact that it was somewhat late in the day, and
  persisted in the fight for right principle to the bitter end. The
  Reviewer, holding exactly the same opinion, not unfairly blames Mr.
  Sauer for inaction in this matter. He “ utterly fails,” he says, “ to
  see that it is tile first duty of an acceptable Opposition to show its
  hostility by action. He refer­red to the George election, and we may
  take that as an instance of the complete failure of the Opposition to
  vindicate its right to be considered as worthy of notice. Here is a
  case of the abuse of political privileges which stinks in the nostrils
  of every honest-minded man in the Colony. What did the Opposition do
  to frustrate it? Better the most ig­nominious defeat to overthrow the
  shameful tactics of wealth and pander­ing than to stand silently by
  and let the pernicious practices go on without a protest. It
  ill-becomes Mr. Sauer to say a word about it. The candidate whom he
  had put up was the first to withdraw and leave the field to the sweet
  bribes of the Rhodes candidate. Had the Op­position been worth a straw
  it would have fought this battle, and by defeat have thrown up the
  tactics of the Minis­terial party into such relief as would have
  raised a more general cry of dis­gust. It is only on a friendly
  platform that Mr. Sauer can parade his platitudes and swell himself
  out with the assertion of good intentions. When it comes to action he
  is the first defaulter. This he himself has to admit.

  We do not want an Opposition of words, but of facts, and one too which
  neglects no opportunity. By tacitly consenting to the unopposed Rhodes
  candidate at the George election Mr. Sauer has shed the last atom of
  respect he could have demanded from the country, and his appearance in
  public is nothing but an occasion for fatuous and pretentious
  maunderings.” The foregoing remarks will be admitted by every
  right-thinking man to sum up the duties of an active vigilant party ;
  and it is sincerely to be hoped that the Parliamentary Leaders, to
  whom they arc addressed, will lay them to heart.

AN OPPOSITION BROADSIDE.

  THE speech which Mr. SAUER
  delivered at Capetown on

  Monday week was an able, sensible,
  and incisive criticism of the political
  situation; and the fact that it has
  goaded the organs in the press of
  the opponents of the honourable
  gentleman and those who think

  with him, to substitute gibes and impotent tit quoques for calm honest
  argument, testifies both to its effec­tiveness and the neat manner the
  Leader of the Opposition dealt out

  his blows. This, however, is what one might have expected from Mr.
  SAUER any day, and the pity is that, with such precise weapons of
  attack he so rarely sallies forth to engage the enemy during the
  recess. It is to be hoped that his appearance at the Capetown meeting
  last week will not prove to be the last; and that there are good
  grounds to in­dulge the hope that the country may hear from him
  oftener on matters of public import may be gathered from a remark
  which he made in the course of his brilliant speech, and which we
  trust was not casual, that the criticism of the recent Session’s
  doings could only be done by instalments, and that he and some of his
  colleagues would

  give these instalments in due course. To this no objection can be
  urged, not even by op­ponents, as it tends to the educa­tion of the
  public in affairs. One could only wish that Mr. SAUER’S address was
  the first of a series in connection with something after what is now
  known as the ^((t) Mid­lothian campaign,” which served so much to open
  the eyes of the Bri­tish public in 1880 to the enormities of the Jingo
  policy, leading to the disastrous overthrow of the BEA­CONSFIELD
  Administration. If the leaders of Parliamentary parties are seized
  with the fact of the absolute importance of the necessity of
  edu­cating the community in the re­quirements of the public
  institutions of the country, then there would be hope as to the
  future. Such educa­tion should, however, be persistent, well-directed,
  and earnest withal ; and then, but not until then, would people be
  spared witnessing revolt­ing transactions, on which Mr. SAUER very
  properly commented with well deserved severity, such as the
  exploitation of the pub­lic estate by the Ministry in the purchase of
  support witnessed at George. As to the subject matter of the address
  of the member for Aliwal North it may be said that he made an
  admirable selection, taking up those matters on which he could attack
  his opponents most vulner­ably. We believe that on the sub­

ject of the great Northern Bubble —that is, so far as it is of
commer­cial value to this Colony -- Mr. SAUER said enough to open the
eyes of those who seemed most anxious to be duped ; stating that while
wish­ing well to the civilization of that part of the country, he
nevertheless looked for the salvation of the Colony from the development
of its latent possibilities. He has touched a responsive chord in the
hearts of Colonists by the observation that the Colony should not merely
be an ‘‘appendage” of the North, which the RHODIAN policy is carefully
cal­culated to make it. Questions such as the Customs Union, and the
Railway Tariff negociations with the Transvaal, were treated with
characteristic statesmanlike grasp; and on the whole the address, which
was studiously free from bitter invective, but nicely spiced with sly
quips at opponents who deserved them, must be ranked among the most
statesmanlike deli­verances in this country; and the cry is for more.

NATIVE LIBERALITY.

  THURSDAY last was a red-
  letter day in Fingoland, the

occasion being the opening of the
pretentious Ayliff Memorial Church
erected on the Butterworth Mission.
Rev. W. J. HACKER is much to
be complimented on raising the
structure for purposes of worship
which is said to be the grandest in
the Transkei; and we hope he may
reap some satisfaction from the fact
that the building will be associated
with his name for the time it lasts.
The debt on the building is re-
ported to have been £1,400. It is
to the credit of the Native people
of the Transkei, who met on Thurs-
day that they that day contributed
of their substance the whole of that
amount. That they are people of
humble means, all know; and for a
peasant class to meet in a day, and
contribute what these people have
done in Fingoland is action de-
serving of the highest com-
mendation. The principle con-
tributions were in kind :—Most of
the men giving from one to two
head of cattle,’ including money.
The European Missionaries also
contributed their quota by no
means a small one. In this way
this Temple was opened free of
debt. May a rich blessing rest on
those who have been so large
hearted as to achieve so noble a
result.

THE RIGHT OF VETO.

(Ons Land.)

There seems to be an earnest resolve to promote an agitation among the
Natives against the Glen Grey Act. A petition, says IMVO in an article
on the subject, has been forwarded to Her Majesty the Queen drawing her
attention to the fact that the Natives are being deprived of their legal
rights. The plan is further to send a deputation of Natives to the next
Parliament with the object of get­ting the law relating to the
alienation of the ground modified. If the deputation should fail in this
the advisability of sending the deputation to London will be considered,
who, if needed, will go through the whole of the United King­dom in
order to awaken sympathy among the British people with the Natives.
Whether this is only intended as a threat, or whether IMVO proposes this
pro­gramme in earnest, will be seen later on.

The Christian Express meanwhile ap­pears to agree with the Kafir paper
and says that the first step for the Natives now is to get the Queen to
refuse her assent to the Act. “She may well do it as the protector and
Paramount Chief of the Natives, and they should all unite in both
cabling and petitioning to that end.”

Induced by what the Graaff-Remetter said lately on the subject we made
known our surprise that any Colonial newspaper should approve or justify
such a policy on the part of the Natives. We had, however, misunderstood
our contempo­rary, and were pleased to learn the decla­ration that no
endeavour to secure Im­perial intermixing in our political matters shall
obtain his support. If there is one matter upon which public opinion is
strongly formed and at present unani­mous, it is this: that the time has
passed for good for the Imperial rule to trouble about our household
cases. A few journals —like the Cape Mercury, IMVO, and the Christian
Express—might still make themselves heard in favour thereof; and men
like Hutton, Hay, and solitary ultra negro friends, might see no harm in
it; still they are not aware of the harm they do to their case when they
encourage and ap­prove such a policy as that which the Natives mean to
follow. Even the Cape Times, a journal which cannot be re­garded as'
positively partial against the Natives, has now come to a positive
con­viction that an agitation such as that sought to be spread among the
Natives will not do, but will on the contrary only damage the interests
of the Natives.

The agitation which IMVO promotes rests, according to that journal, on
the following argument. Treaties entered upon by Her Majesty with
Natives de­clare that the Natives “ shall keep their ground for ever,
that it is inalienable except by Her Majesty, and in case of hostilities
and causing war.” The Kafir journal argues now that the Glen Grey Act
elienates the ground, whereas it is inalienable according to the
treaties. We entirely deny the validity of this argu­ment. The Treaties
do not say that the ground is to remain unalienable, but only warrants
that the Natives shall never deprived of it except in the case of
insur­rection or war. This law does not deprive the Natives of the
ground, but gives the right to the Natives themselves, if they choose to
do so, with the consent of the Government, to let go the ground. This is
quite a different thing.

Now as to the right of veto upon which the Natives threaten to appeal,
it will be remembered that we have on former oc­casions already pleaded
for a change in the Constitution on this point. According to the
Constitution the Crown has the right for two years to exercise its veto
upon any law passed by Parliament and approved by the Governor. This
question was also discussed in the Bond- Congress at Queenstown, and a
decision was come to that the term of two years should be shortened. The
advisableness of such a step is now shown by the pro­mised agitation on
the part of the Natives. That they should agitate and betake themselves
to our parliament, and there seek to get what they can, nobody can say
anything against. The way is open to everyone. However, that the
constitution should leave the door open for two long years for the
Crown, in order to exercise its right of veto, only serves to keep up
the agitation-fire during all that time, calling to life and waking up
among the people discontentedness, if not insurrec­tion or revolt. There
is no reason why this right should remain valid longer than say six
months. The Native agitation would do well to call attention to this
question again, and we shall be delighted if this time they should come
a little further than the bare discussion of the matter.
