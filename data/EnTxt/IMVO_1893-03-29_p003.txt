[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

It is to the highest interests of the Natives that there should be at
once a general agreement and a united request for protected individual
title to tribal lands. Had individual titles been applied for five years
ago there would have been no “ Private Locations Act of 1892.” As long
as Locations remain tribal property no one can effectually answer the
demand of the “Anti Native” “let them go to their own locations and
leave the farms to the white people.” But not only for the protection
but also for the advance­ment of our people should individual titles be
secured. Their issue will mark the beginning of gradual improvement of
the condition of the Native agricultural labourers. The more prosperous,
having the right of selling their location lots for fair value to other
Natives, will do so and rent or buy larger farms; while the less
prosperous class will take their places in the locations with the same
prospect of advancement, for having a home and as much ground as can be
cultivated by the women and children for their own sup­port, the men
will be free to go forth and find employment and so augment the family
income until by frugality and industry they also are able to place
them­selves in a better position than that of location people.

The history of the coast farms especial­ly proves that they are most
successfully worked by Native labour. Here, then, is the Native’s fair
field. Lot European, capitalists employ their capital in stock farming
and in developing the country to their own particular advantage as well
as to the public good. But let Natives, whose labour can prove as
valuable to the country as capital, work steadily on the holdings, which
their labour alone can make of real value, and then will the Native
races of South Africa attain to true civilisation, independence, and
honour. Hitherto Native farm labourers have worked for the improvement
of white men’s farms while their Locations have deteriorated. Let them
get titles to their lots in the Locations and they will soon, learn how
to improve their own property as well as the property of others.

[FROM THE “ E. P. HERALD.”]

It is a matter of surprise to many that Imvo should be so disconcerted
because Mr. Hofmeyr is consolidating his Parlia­mentary forces. Surely
the class whom our contemporary especially represents have no cause to
fear power falling into the hands of a generous and philan­thropic
political! who brought in the Native Disabilities Removal Bill and
carried it through Parliament. Paeans of praise and triumph were
phonographed from East to West, and at the time we wondered if after all
Mr. Hofmey would take Imvo under his influential protection and become
the champion of aboriginal rights. The Native Disabilities Removal Bill
was gilded a pill to render palatable the Parliamentary Registration
Bill brought in by Sir Gordon Sprigg at the behest of the Bond to knock
off some hundreds (perhaps thousands) of native names from the voters
list, and Messrs. Innes, Sauer and Merriman con­sistently voted against
this measure because it disfranchised a class of persons who had enjoyed
the privilege of voting ever since' representative institutions were
conferred on the Colony. The fascination of Mr. Hofmeyr’s Native
Disabilities Removal Bill lasted until the Franchise Bill became law for
Imvo, and other native authorities simply gave themselves away on this
measure which was so sweeping in its effects that the Parliamentary
Registration Bill was perfectly innocuous in comparison. We quite
approved of the new Franchise Bill, but it was impossible for a
spectator not to be thoroughly amused at the general frost which came
about when the Bill was introduced ; Messrs. Innes, Sauer and Merriman
who were righteously indig­nant over the Parliamentary Registration Bill
were the fathers of this sweeping measure, while Sir Gordon Sprigg, who
had set the ball rolling with a bill on similar lines, now felt his
sense of pro­priety shocked. Neither Mr. Sauer nor Mr. Merriman liked
the business, but personal interest triumphed over private conviction.
Mr. Innes made a bold con­fession that expediency influenced him in
submitting to what he could not help and making the best bargain he
could. This may be all very well, but it is not Res­ponsible Government,
for if the three leading politicians named had firmly adhered to their
convictions and taken the course which no doubt their con­sciences
dictated, it might have created such a division that we should possibly
have heard no more of the Franchise Bill for some years to come. We
think such a result would have been matter for regret, but we should
have preferred it to the spectacle of our leading politicians being
placed in such an undignified dilemma and throwing aside their
con­victions where principle alone should have ruled their actions.
This, of course, is ancient history, but the recapitulation serves to
show how thoroughly Imvo and its friends have fallen in with Mr.
Hof­meyr’s views and have become his allies in carrying out measures
which they have professed themselves adverse to. We do not think that
Mr. Hofmeyr and his party will attempt to enforce any legislation which
will be more objection­able than one would have supposed the franchise
would be judging from the expressed views of its opponents, and having
swallowed so much, they may rest content that the consolidation and
increase of Mr. Hofmeyr’s Parliamentary forces will in no way tend to
the injury of the Native races. Have they not Mr. Hofmeyr’s Natives
Disabilities Removal Act as a standing memento of his regard and
affection ?
