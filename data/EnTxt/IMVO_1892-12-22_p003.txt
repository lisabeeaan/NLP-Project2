Native Opinion

  URING the recent Kimberley contest for the seat in the

Assembly vacated by Mr. J. H. LANGE, the coloured people, who became
somewhat prominent in connection with the Franchise appeal to England,
again distinguished themselves by their eccen­tricity in espousing the
extremely doubtful candidature of Mr. J. S. COWIE. This gentleman had a
meeting with these people in the Imperial Hall, George Street, where
among 250 more than half the audience were coloured, and in­cluded many
leading men amongst the Indians and Mahomedan com­munity as well as Cape
men. After an address from the candidate they met to honour, and who was
straightforward enough to declare himself a Bondman, these coloured men
unanimously carried a vote of confidence in Mr. COWIE, Thus the coloured
people virtually rejected Mr. R. SOLOMON, their truest friend. These are
the people some hare­brained enthusiasts were going about a short time
back saying the aboriginal Native of this country should join, and
associate with, for political purposes. This journal was among those who
doubted that any good could come from such a combination. But even in
our wildest dreams it did not occur to us that these coloured people
would so soon demonstrate their utter in­competence to grasp a political
situation by taking up a discredited Bondman like Mr. COWIE, not
possessing a scintila of fitness for Parliamentary work, and reject a
gentleman of Mr. SOLOMON S South African reputation for fairness to the
coloured classes of the land. Such fatuity is scarcely conceivable ; and
it is a class like the coloured people alone who could possibly be
guilty of it The incident, how­ever, affords a striking object-lesson to
those in the Colony who were so green as to believe that there could be
any lasting union between the classes who gave what reality was to be
found in Mr. COWIE’S candi­dature, and the Natives who would not have
failed to distinguish be­tween such a man and a friend of Mr. SOLOMON’S
standing. It must be sorrowfully confessed, however, that if these are
the lines along which it is to proceed, the Union of these coloured
people is bound to do more harm to themselves than good. Conduct such as
this can only lead to one logical conclusion that they were better
without the franchise than have it to cut off their own noses, and with
it to spite the country by sending to its Parlia­ment political
mountebanks like Mr. COWIE who would make it a veritable bear-garden.

IT is much to be regretted BAD NEW’S, that the Free State Gov­ernment
appears to be receding from a creditable position in respect of the
Native Policy it has for some time occupied in respect of the sale of
drink to Natives; as the following from the P. E. Telegraph will show :—
“The Free State liquor law has raised a new industry in the Free State.
A publi­can is bound to serve a black man who produces an order signed
by his master. There has now sprung up a class of imaginary masters, who
make a trade of selling orders for liquor. Even these ingenious fellows
have not a monopoly, for there are also certain persons com­monly called
bummers who stand in the vicinity of the canteens in order to buy drink
for niggers, charging 6d or Is for their trouble, and thereby making a
good living without working for it.”

TUESDAY last was an SHAFTESBURY eventful day for this HOME famous Native
Institution, PEELTON. which owes its existence to Miss Sturrock. She
closes a very useful and valuable and honourable career of twenty-nine
years association with the place. Sho made her valedictory address — her
last there. Therefore, Miss Sturrock invited friends and others to be
present on the occasion, to inspect the Home and all that is to be seen
there, and where they can compare notes to draw the contrast between
1862 and 1892 in relation to that locality, what it has been since, and
what work it has done towards civilization and religion amongst the
Natives. Later Miss Sturrock will publish an account ot what has been
done on the station and in the schools and Home. When sho began her work
it was necessary on admitting a girl to subject her to a considerable
amount of tubbing and scrubbing and combing before a lent dress (no
other garment then was worn) could be put on her or she could touch a
book in the school. Now no greater insult can be offered to a girl than
to ask her whether she would like to go back to those scapy days. Those
days have, happily, passed away. Girls no longer need this pre­paratory
process. The time of lending clothes, no fees, no books is changed into
a demand larger than can be met for more accommodation for the education
and training of the girls of our Natives. Miss Sturrock’s aim has been
to educate the hand, the head, and the heart—and the parents appreciate
her work. All who can, now pay for a year’s clothes, books, etc., in
advance. It is the exception indeed when help is wanted. The Home girls
have done a good mission work. Out of their mission work-meeting
pro­ceeds they have paid £20 towards a teacher for the Interior. Besides
assisting con­stantly in the Sunday school, girls went out in couples
for fifteen years (up to ’88) and distributed over 15,086 tracts in
English and Kafir over the Station, and dealing mostly with the “ reds”
all over the place, not neglecting the Christian residents. During
nearly thirty years over £985 has been spent on free girls trained in
this and the old Home. This sum does not include any part of the day
schools or other work. The Shaftesbury Home, collected for by Miss
Sturrock, cost over £2,037. Not Peelton only has reaped the benefits of
the money and personal labours expended bv Miss Sturrock and her neice.
A life’s blood in energy has been spent for the rising generation of
Native girls and women— that these two good women may leave the world
better than they found it. They have toiled in the sight of all. The
doors of the establishment under their control have bean always open for
their work to be inspected; and none leave a better record of service,
as they have given it in the cause of Him they claim for Master.

Under the heading “The Gladstone of South Africa ” The Eastbourne
Gazette of October 26th, 1892, gives an interesting account of the
funeral of Mr. Saul Solomon in that town, from which we take over the
following :—

With a quiet simplicity which was more affecting than any of the pomp
and. pageantry of woe, the mortal remains of Mr. Saul Solomon, a
distinguished Colonial statesman, were yesterday after­noon interred at
the cemetery at Ocklynge. The coffin was brought to Eastbourne by train,
and the cortege, which started from the railway station shortly after
two o’clock, included an open glass hearse and a number of mourning
coaches drawn by white and grey horses. The first portion of the service
at the cemetery took place in the chapel, and was conducted most
impressively by the Rev. George Thompson, who delivered a brief address.
He said : The body which we carry to its last resting place^^day is the
body of a

great soul. The man who tenanted it was never very strong physically,
but ho was always strong mentally and morally. Ho was a man of deep
personal piety, and benevolent. He was a distinguished man of business,
a politician, a statesman, and a philanthropist. And he gave his
splendid services in the interests of the African race. He laboured for
its free­dom, education, development, and progress. He was not a
missionary, but le was the missionary’s friend and helper and protector.
And he longed for the full and complete redemption of Africa. There are
those in this country who believe that Africa is the coming race. Be
that as it may, all the leading deno­minations of Christians have
declared themselves for missionary enterprise, and, during the last five
years, they have antered upon a great forward movement in the
evangelization of the world. And they have all, more or less, centered
their chief thought towards and in Africa, And we cannot help but
believe that the nation which has had so many lives spent in its
service, including such lives as Livingstone, Moffat, and Sani Solomon,
will one day take a great lead among the nations of the world. One of
the gieatest and last wishes of him whose funeral we celebrate to-day
was “ God Bless Africa.” He was kind and tender and thoughtful and
considerate to a degree to all who were about him. He passed away in the
bosom of his family. And those who saw him die, affirmed that his
death­chamber was radiant with glory. He was not a poet, but his life
was a poem; and he has left a memory in the hearts of his family, and in
the hearts of the thousands of Africa, which will be more durable than
any mausoleum of granite or of marble. Let us emulate his virtues.

At the gjaveside Mr. Thompson con­cluded the service, and prayer was
offered by Mr. John Knox Bokwe, an African gentleman who is connected
with the Lovedale Institution, where some six hundred youthsare being
educated. When the coffin had been lowered, Mr. Bokwe shook hands with
the widow and other mourners, thus representing Africa and England
joining hands over the grave of deceased. Despite the inclement weather
the obsequies were attended by several friends from a distance.

The late Mr. Saul Solomon was born at St. Helena, and died at Windsor,
Kil- creggan, Dumbartonshire, on October 16th in his 76th year. In early
life he went to the Cape, and became a prominent figure in the political
world. He had the con- ract for the Government printing, and also the
control of a leading newspaper the Cape Argus, and was an effective
debater in the Cape Parliament. The premiership was offered to him, but
he declined to form a cabinet as he could not obtain the co-operation of
the then Attorney General, the Hon. William Porter. He advocated the
voluntary principle in religion, and carried it against the powerful
opposition of the Dutch Reformed Church. He strongly opposed the
Contagious Diseased (Women’s) Act, and brought about its repeal. He was
often called “ The Gladstone of the Cape” because he generally advocated
in Opposi­tion causes that were at the outset un­popular. Somewhat late
in life he married Miss Thomson (a Scotch lady). Ten years ago his
failing health caused his withdrawal from public life. He leaves a vidow
and three children.

CURRENT OPINION.

The Cape Mercury writes as follows on the barbarian question :—The East,
too, lias its barbarians fast relapsing from primitive respectability
into a condition of abject misery. Those who live on the frontier, and
keep their eyes open to the events going on around them, will know that
we allude to the Natives whose prosperity and happiness is beingblastcd
by drink. That is about the only thing which runs free in this country.
Now if Mr. Hofmeyr and his friends would consent to an Excise then funds
would be available to assist the white bar­barians, about whose
condition so much anxiety is evinced. At present the canteen fraternity
arc pocketing the difference between 3s and 9s on every gallon of
brandy, and each hogshead sold represents a given average quantity of
poverty and crime. When a being has been reduced to one condition the
other is within easy reach. We want this altered and we ask the Herald
and the Dutch Reformed Ministers, and Mr. Hofmeyr, and everybody else to
lend a hand in raising our black people, so that the same movement may
result in assistance to the poverty stricken whites. Is it only on the
progress and prosperity of the white race that the welfare of this
country depends. Certainly not. The Native is a consumer to an enor­mous
extent, and every sixpence put into brandy means ten times that amount
less to spend in other and better commodities. When a class of people
have been reduced to a condition of frequent drunkenness, they labour
less and purchase less, so that the entire community loses immensely
more than the liquor seller pockets. For our black barbarians we appeal
to the men who would open our purses to aid their white barbarians.

It is probable we shall be told, as we have been over and over again,
that if a tax is levied on drink in such a man­ner that it will not
conic upon the pro­ducer then well and good. The only possible answer is
that it never did come out of the pocket of the producer. But there is
no difficulty whatever about the imposition of an Excise which will be
collectable near the pocket of the consumer, if that be all that the
Bond wants. Wo have sketched a plan once, before, and its repetition
here will do no harm. First the grower or distiller should not be
allowed to sell by retail, or to any retail dealer. The wholesale
merchant might buy when and where he liked, but the moment the liquor
went from his store into the, possession of a retailer, then the invoice
should bear a revenue stamp equal to the, Excise imposed per gallon. The
issue of such invoice to the seller should be compulsory, and the whole
trade should be subject to the most rigorous inspec­tion, with stiff
penalty clauses for any infringement of the regulations. Upon a third
conviction there should be no alternative but the breakwater. Such a
scheme would ensure the tax being paid immediately upon the liquor going
into consumption. As the white barbarian question is a blot on the
civilization of the Colony, and of necessity appeals most acutely to our
fellow-Colonists of Dutch extraction, wc trust that they will sec their
way clear to adopt our views, as the outcome would be a larger revenue
available both for the prosecu­tion of humanitarian aims and public
works. This too at no cost to the brandy producer, while it would remove
the stigma from the Cape of taxing coffee 12s. Gd. per 100 lbs., while
allow­ing the intoxicating and more debasing article to go free.
