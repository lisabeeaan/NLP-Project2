to have been made by Mr. C. J. LEVEY, the respected Magistrate of Gala.
II is position, experience, and the very active interest he has for many
years taken in the progress of our people entitle his opinions to
respect. Had the statement been made by a man whose views do not merit
the same consideration we would not have felt it to be our duty to point
out what is misleading in it. His words as sent on the wings of the
Press are as follows :— I consider the present system in the Mission
Schools in the Transkeian Territories is an utter failure. We are
building up a class of Natives who may become a menace to the Colony.
The dignity of labour is lost sight of altogether in these Schools and
the result is that children return to their homes more useless than they
were when
they left.

We submit that the above state­ment is entirely misleading. And we
regret that a man in Mr. LEVEY’S position should have made it, and that
at a time when the attitude of many towards the Natives is anything but
friendly. As those who are intimately con­nected with Mission Schools
both in the Transkei and in the older portions of the Colony, we state
that children who attend these Schools do not leave their homes. It is,
therefore, incorrect to say, “ they return to their homes more “ useless
than when they left.” Mission Schools, as such, are put up as near the
centre of a location as the circumstances permit, and the site is always
chosen where the children who come to school will be able to return to
their homes every day. It is only those who go to Native Institutions
who can be spoken of as leaving or returning to their homes. Of these
Institu­tions the man who would assert that the dignity of labour is
lost sight of would not be worth argu­ing with, especially after such a
statement as has been given by Dr. STEWART of Lovedale in his “
Love­dale, Past and Present.”

But again, we think it important to explain to those who do not know the
actual state of things that during the time children attend Mission
Schools before and after school hours they work at their own homes just
as any other Native children do. Indeed during ploughing and shearing or
reaping time the real difficulty is to get children to come to school.
It is incorrect to speak of these children, as if during their school
period, they had no work to do, and that they were brought up with the
idea that to read and write were the only necessities of life. In this
connection Mission Schools are only a part of the daily life in a Native
Location, and although Mission Schools have now existed among our people
for several years we submit that the class Mr. LEVEY speaks of has no
existence. At Mission Stations or Native Loca­tions there is a number of
children who through the carelessness of their parents never attend
school, and we can testify from abundant experience that these children
do not work more than those who attend schools. Our contention is that
Mr. LEVEY’S statement is loose and misleading, and that no such a class
as he refers to has been or could be the result of the present system
which plants a school where the teacher and the parent will work hand in
hand. Of course our remarks do not deny that there may be room for
improvement; but the system as such has answered fairly well, and the
charge made against it in the statement under consideration is
undeserved.

THE much-talked-of Trek has ended exactly as we expected it would —in
dust and smoke. It was too late in the day, after Mr. RHODES had
exploited the regions to the North of the Limpopo, which at one time
were supposed to be

impregnable, for any body of men to start a movement such as the one
about which we have heard so much, with any hope of success. To begin,
not taking into account the almost endless military resources of the
Chartered Company, there was the voice of South Africa against it. The
Trekkers could not expect to be countenanced by the Transvaal,which knew
that if nothing has ever deeply ruffled the temper of the British Lion,
any expedition of this sort started under her auspices would open up
subjects involving her own independence, which she prizes so much, and
not without reason. Where, then, were the Trekkers to get support? At
signal we believe Mr. RHODES could have set on them the formidable
manslaying machine ” which LOBENGULE, like CETYWAYO, has reared ; and
the Trekkers would have been smashed. None knew this better than the
Trekkers themselves. The gyrations they have been indulging in have
therefore always appeared to us as harmless as they were unreal; in
short, as a sheer make-believe, or, to use the
emphatic Taal, bangmakery pure and simple. Our political leaders are to
be congratulated that they have sustained from this hideous dream
nothing more serious than a shock to the system. This much we have
always felt, that even if the Trekkers did go into the British sphere it
would be as peaceful and dutiful subjects of the Company,
and not as its enemies.

THE LIQUOR BILL.

THE principle of the Govern-
ment Bill, for the regulation and restriction of the Liquor evil, has
been well received by the country. The Bill requires amending in two
respects to render it a complete measure of its kind ; and we trust
Ministers will see their way to adopt the amendments, without
which their Act will be a dead letter so far as practical purposes are
concerned. One amendment is that for the purposes of the measure Hut-Tax
Payers in Native Centres should be given the status of voters in
connection with the Local Option clauses. It will be generally conceded
that it is the outcry of the Natives that has made legislation
in this direction imperative. They were for total prohibition, if not
for black and white, at all events for themselves. This is granted them
by Government in the shape of Local Option. But Government must have
overlooked the fact that by far the greatest majority of the Natives are
not Registered Voters ; and that, therefore, the concession of Local
Option in the form proposed will
not benefit the Natives one white. It is only in the manner we suggest
that Local Option can be made to promise some prospective benefit for
our people. The Legislature might well grant them this, as otherwise, to
give our people the Bill as it stands, and nothing but the Bill, would
be giving them a stone in the place of the bread for which they asked.

Yet another very sensible amendment has been thrown out by Mr. VICTOR
SAMPSON, Barrister-at-Law, a gentleman who has long evinced a deep and
enlightened interest in public affairs. We hope the day is not far
distant when Mr. SAMPSON may grace‘the Legislature with his presence, as
we are assured it would
not have a more deligent and intelligent member, who would in every way
be a credit to it. Mr. SAMPSON is more concerned with the principle
underlying the sale by the bottle; and makes out a case bottle ; against
it which seems to us as irresistible. The Government would be wise to
embody it in their Bill.
“ There is no greater curse to the
country,” writes Mr. SAMPSON, than the licence to sell by the bottle. I
speak from observation, and I could produce the most conclusive evidence
to prove that the greatest amount of crime is due to the half-bottle
(pint) which is bought at the canteen or bottle store, and consumed, far
away from police or other supervision, in the hut or veldt. The
principle, therefore, of the Government measure, in so far as it permits
this evil to continue, is wrong. We should strive for the regulation of
drinking in public. By regulation I mean that no canteen-keeper or other
licensed victualler should be allowed to sell to any native or person
frequenting the canteen, liquor to be carried away. It should be
consumed at the counter, and three or four convictions for drunkenness
upon the premises should render the licence liable to forfeiture. In
this way we should enlist the licence holder on the side of temperate
drinking, without depriving the subject of his right to a glass of
brandy if he

AMONG BILLS TO BE SUB-

milted to the forth

coming session of the

Cape Legislature is

one to amend the law relating to the Estates of Deceased Persons, Minors
and others. The measure is a vary important one indeed especially in so
far as it affects the more advanced class of our countrymen. These
people, to show that they had taken up civilised life, went in
for buying land or otherwise securing titles to their holdings. At
death, not knowing that the State has to deal with estates, left all to
their heirs under the impression that everything would be dealt will
under Native custom. The result has been misunderstanding leading to
confusion ; and we believe to day more than half the estates of deceased
Natives are not held under proper legal title. The Attorney General
would do the people a service, while be is about amending the law on
this subject, to make necessary provision for fixing matters in the case
of Native estates.
