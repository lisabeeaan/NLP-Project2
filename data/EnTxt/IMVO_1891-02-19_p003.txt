We have been under the im­pression that there was no possibili­ty of a
distinctive Liquor candidate being put forward for these dis­tricts with
any chance whatsoever of being returned ; and it certainly is an
astounding revelation to us to find to-day that two may be sent to the
Legislative Council for seven years, leaving only one to Liquor
reformers. The plain meaning of this is that we are not to hope for the
long looked-for amendments dealing with this hateful traffic until some
time during the 20th Century ! For if a purely English constituency like
the Eastern Pro­vince can be so manipulated as deliberately to assist in
securing a majority of representatives of the Brandy interest, what can
be expected of other parts of the Colony that know nothing of British
traditions and British principles? We confess this is very
dishearten­ing. Only the other day Aliwal North had a meeting under the
Mayor’s auspices to urge upon Government legislating on the Brandy sale;
Grahamstown, Port Elizabeth, and even Bloemfontein did the same. All
muttered, Amen. What will be thought of people who did this and returned
a majority of Liquor candidates?

Yet it is not too late to mend The English vote in the con stituency,
which is considerable, is helplessly weak to-day because of the
multiplicity of the candidates ; and while the friends of the reform of
the Liquor Laws are shut up in little opposing camps, the two Liquor
candidates will stalk into the Upper Chamber and there block the path of
progress for years. If one at least of the two candidates favoured by
the party that swears by the Brandy Cask is to be kept out, the
opponents of Cape Smoke must decide at once to close up their ranks, and
concentrate all their strength on two candidates who are fairly well
supported now and who stand the best chance of disturbing the
arrangements of the Anti-Excise League. Until the central fact of this
argument dawned upon us we were ready, nay, anxious, to advocate the
return of three gentlemen who would work together to conduct the
busi­ness of the country on safe pro­gressive lines, and would act
fairly towards all sections of the popula­tion. But this was when there
was no fear of two strong candidates taking the field who would deal
with measures affecting Cape Smoke in the interests of a ring interested
in the production and the sale of this article; and nothing but the
public cause which we are striving to serve makes us think it would be
the height of insanity to labour and toil to engineer three gentlemen in
while the Brandy interest were smartly slipping in their two
repre­sentatives.

THE Commission on Edu- THE cation lately appointed is EDUCATION being
closely canvassed COMMISSION, in the Press. One writer in the Cape Times
is try­ing to make party capital out of it. There must be very little
material out of which to frame an attack on Ministers when even the
personnel of a Commission is pressed into service by opponents of a
Ministry. The appointment of Judge Barry seems to be the least
justifiable, and certainly if a Judge was to be ap­pointed as President
there was no lack of gentlemen on the Judicial Bench, even more closely
identified with education than the Judge President of the E.D. Court.
Mr. Justice Smith, for example, as Vice-Chancellor of the Cape
University, might have been relied upon to watch the interests of the
Higher Education of the country on the Commission ; failing that learned
Judge there are Judges Laurence and Solomon whose interest in educa­tion
is well known. Besides, Sir Jacob Barry, had only a short while ago
pre­sided on the Native Laws and Customs Commission, and might well have
given way to another judge on this occasion. Nor can people understand
why two Dutch Ministers are appointed ; and well might Roman Catholics
grumble that one of them was not jettisoned for their Dr. Kolbe. The
Weslevans are putting in a claim too, but on what it is founded is not
clear to us as they have not earned the reputation of educationists as a
body. Dr. Berry might be depended upon to see to the interests of Native
education, and we fall in with the P. E. Telegraph view that “ the name
of Dr. Berry of Queens­town will command universal respect.
Intellectually he is probably the superior of any member of the
Commission, and in general culture and variety of reading no other
member can approach him. His interest in education is keen, and his
support of our undenominational system sincere.”

" ELOQUENT STATISTICS.”

  DEAR SIR,—Referring to a paragraph
  in IMVO, (February 5, 1891), the writer
  seems to think an average of one in ten
  (10 per cent.) very small as representing
  the result of evangelical work within the
  Colony. According to the figures quoted,
  taking the total of the six districts named,
  instead of ten per cent, it is only five per
  cent, or one in twenty!!! Leaving out
  Kingwilliamstown there is then nine per
  cent.

  Assuming the above statistics to be
  correct, and leaving out Kingwilliams-
  town, there is cause to be thankful. We
  too frequently speak of Native Christians
  as though they had been bred and
  brought up surrounded by every means
  of promoting civilization and Christianity. We entirely ignore or seem
  to
  ignore the fact that fifty years ago a
  civilized native in the Eastern Province
  was as rare as black diamonds. How
  many Natives were here in this “civilized Christian Colony,” only
  twenty five
  years ago, capable of holding such positions as Lawyers, Clergymen,
  Doctors,
  Teachers, Clerks and Interpreters? If
  any one could supply statistics under
  this head for the years 1865 and 1890
  respectively, it would make a percentage
  of ten per cent, more appreciated than it
  would appear to be by some. No one
  but the Native Christian or (in some
  cases) his missionary can realise the
  difficulties, the trials, the temptations
  which beset a convert living or trying to
  live a consistent life either in a “ civilized
  Christian Colony” or in Kafirland.
  Among the former community he's only
  a “nigger” or a “School Kafir,” which
  is about fifty per cent, worse. His
  Christianity is a myth to most colonists.
  A Kafirland, if his conversion be genuine, he is ridiculed, or looked
  upon as a
  deluded individual by his own people.
  Humanly speaking, whether in the
  Colony or in Kafirland, he has nothing
  but his own moral determination to hold
  him up. In his new life he has no
  Heredity to influence him, and all his
  environments only tend to pull down
  that Christianity, that spiritual life which
  his better nature under the influence of
  Divine grace tells him he should adopt.
  With such a low percentage among his
  country men, his companions, it must be,
  and it is a hard struggle for the Native
  to continue faithful to the end. In conclusion I would ask those
  interested in
  Native progress not to take ‘ percentages ’
  or ‘averages’ as tests of the growth of
  that kingdom which “cometh not by
  observation.”

  Very truly yours,
