Let us look, now, at the other side of the picture. Suppose the Imperial
Gov­ernment, or the Colonial Government, were to take over Pondoland
peaceably with the consent of the Pondos ; were to appoint Magistrates
for administering justice; were to give the people indivi­dual titles to
their lands, and protect them in possession by a Native Land Court; were
to give them the blessings of Education, general and industrial; were to
open up the country by a railway, and utilise St. John’s River harbour
for exports—there would be created a peace­ful and prosperous community,
and the example thus set would breed loyalty to the Government among all
the Native races.

There is nothing new in this. What is Fiji now, where this was done; and
the Pondos are not like the Fijians, a race of cannibals. The New
Hebrides have again and again since 1862, implored the British and the
Australian Governments for annexation, and it would have been given but
for the French who wish to follow out the Pondoland scheme sketch­ed out
above, and to occupy the islands with a hundred thousand French
crimin­als, who are to go scot free provided only they never return to
France. So says the Rev. J. G. Paton. It seems the Colonial landgrabbers
have no monopoly in the art of robbing their fellowmen.

I have thus set forth the two alterna­tives in the mode of dealing with
Pondo­land. If the one is adopted, it will turn Pondoland. Basutoland,
and the other Native Districts into another Ireland; but I hope the
English in the Colony will determinedly carry out the other, and make
British government as honour­ed a thing here as it is in other parts of
the world.—CENSOR.

DRINK IN SOUTH AFRICA.—The advent of white man, and the civilisation
which (more or less) follows in his train, is (says the Weekly Graphic),
generally speaking, anything but an unmixed blessing to the native races
that come within the sphere of his influence. Wherever he goes, the
trader, eager to make as much money in as little time as Sossible,
carries with him the vilest of rink—rum, whisky, or what not—for the
worse the compound the better the profits. Nowhere, perhaps, are the
evils which result from the unrestricted sale of this horrible stuff
more marked than among the native races of South Africa, splendid
specimens of savages though they be. Magistrates, missionaries and
everybody else interested in their welfare, have urged again and again
that the sale of spirits should be regulated in some way, the
authorities at home have been addressed upon the subject, and, recently,
the better disposed of the natives of Swaziland have themselves
protested. Up to the present, however all this has had no effect, and
the South African native is still at liberty to spend every coin he has
earned, or to barter away anything of value he may possess, in order
that he may drink himself into a state of brutal insensibility or
fiendish quarrelsomeness. Such a sight as is afforded by our
illustration is common enough around the canteens. The blacks dressed in
cast-off European habiliments lie about, litterally among the pigs; or
fight among themselves, using the empty bottles or their knobkerries as
weapons, and the tender and phlegmatic Dutchmen as a rule, look on
unmoved. Drink is answerable for by far the greater portion of the crime
of South Africa.
