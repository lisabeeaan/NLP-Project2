THE FRANCHISE.

AT a meeting held at the Paarl
last week to receive the re-

port of the Deputation sent to spy
out Mashonaland the Hon. J. H.
HOFMEYR, M.L A., took a promin-
ent part. In the course of a long
speech, mainly devoted to explain-
ing his own attitude in regard to
the subject of the Afrikander Trek
about which so much was written
a few months back, he introduced
the subject of the Franchise in
terms the importance and signifi-
cance of which cannot be gainsayed.
“ The great question of the future,”
observed Mr. HOFMEYR, “ was the
“ electoral privileges of the fran-
‘‘ chise—whether we shall in future
“ be governed by blacks or by our-
“ selves. The latter (added the
“ member for Stellenbosch) was
“ what Mr. RHODES was striving
“ for, and he deserved the fullest
^(u) co-operation of the Afrikanders ;
“ his heart was with them.” Fur-
ther on, in the same connection,
he hinted at an immediate dissolu-
tion of Parliament as likely to
“ come sooner than most of you
“ present expect; ” and urged
union among the Afrikanders, with-
out which the triumph of the Bond
party, which he anticipates, will be
converted into a defeat, “ when
“Afrikanders allow themselves to
“ be split up into parties on this
“ matter of the franchise, or of the
“ trek.''

Such are the declarations of the
political leader who has the reputa-
tion of knowing exactly what he
wants; and of steadily and patiently
setting himself to secure it. Mr.
HOFMEYR is, however, but the
leader of a section so small
by itself at present as to be unable
to form a Ministry, although
powerful enough as a balancing
party in a House where those not
of its way of thinking are unhap-
pily not agreed among themselves
as to the best way to compass the
success of their own principles.
The Bond Leader is quite within
his province, then, to lay himself
out to strengthen his party with a
view to turn it into a majority that
will enable it to govern hereafter on
Bond lines untrammelled by any
fears of being ousted if ho took a
course not altogether relished by
English sentiment. As Natives
we can scarcely expect consideration
for what we cherish as our rights
and privileges as British subjects
from an Afrikander regime; and
it is hardly to be wondered
at if we recoil with fear from
any anticipated victory of the
principles of the Bond at the polls.
This being the case then, that we
should be ready to cast in our lot
in support of any organisation or
party that is labouring to thwart
the sinister designs of the Bond
leader is perfectly natural Some
time ago the devices of the Bond
to gain ascendancy in the land were
blurted out by Mr. T. P. THERON
who should know ; for he is a leader
in the organisation as powerful as
Mr. HOFMEYR. He said, among
other things, they would secure
victory by throwing dust into the
eyes of their opponents. Now, we
have always said that the indecent
haste to effect an alteration in so
big a matter as the Franchise by
representing that in the next Par-
liament the blacks were going to
wrench the Government from the
whites by sheer brute force of
numbers, was but a broadside of
“dust” and smoke from the
Afrikander torpedo to blind “ its
friend the enemy; ” and he is a
fool who would say the enemy is
the Native. For although ever
since the colonisation by Great
Britain of this land the Natives
have naturally shown a larger pro-
portion in numbers than the Colo-
nists, the idea of elbowing whites
out of Parliament has never seized
them, not even to the extent of
sending a single member of their
race. It is “ dust ” then to
vehemently urge the change in the
Franchise now to ward off an inva-
sion of the precincts of Parliament
by blacks. We hope to show later
on that it is only the reduction by
such expedients of the strength of
the English and liberal-minded
Dutch members in the House that
is sought—nothing more nor less—
since there are no Black Members of
Parliament to be disposed of by the
franchise amendment now urged.
The Native has no power now to
defend himself in this Franchise
business, as he is virtually out of it;
and the real battle must be fought
by members of British extraction and
liberal and fair minded Dutchmen
As we can’t do more we can wish
them all success, for we have no-
thing to hope from the ascendancy of
the principles that animate the Bond.

STRONG exception is being THE AFRICAN taken in the Colonial Press
MAIDEN. to the remarks made in Mr. W. T. Stead’s world- famed magazine,
the Review of Reviews, as the outcome of an interview with a coloured
maiden, a member of the South African Choir now in England, who is said
to be acquainted with five different languages. She represented that
Natives are not treated fairly by Colonists; that free education is not
given to her people ; and lastly that nothing is being done to save
Natives from drink. The view taken by the maiden depends on the
standpoint from which she looks at these subjects ; but whatever the
point of view, we feel bound to say the statements of the damsel are far
too sweeping even for the facts of the situation ; there being no lack
of interest among Colonists in the ad­vancement of our people to warrant
the blackening of their name at home. So far all the grievances real or
fancied of our people in respect of treatment are of a domestic nature;
and there has not been any disposition on the part of Europeans in this
country to refuse listening to what our people have to urge in their own
behalf; and acting up to the necessities of any case. Our desires may
not be met in the way, and just when we wish; but this is but the usual
course of things. It is enough if our lips are not sealed ; and we are
free to urge our wants. The maiden might as well have said what she felt
here as in England, where by saying it she would appear as a dirty bird
fouling her own nest.

NATIVE EDUCATION.

LETTER TO THE EDITOR.

SIR,—The progress made by education amongst the Natives of South Africa
since the days of Sir George Grey is very slow. The Elementary
Education, imparted to the Natives, has been of equal pace with only
slight variations which are not worth while to refer to. Some eight or
ten years ago the Native students at Healdtown were taught some
carpentry which is still of some use to the teachers and others who left
the Institution at that time; for after school hours they go into their
little workshops and do some work. But what a great difference if you
compare them with those who went there after carpentry was abolished!
They can­not even repair broken school furniture. The students at
Healdtown are compelled (so to speak) to be all teachers; whereas the
lads and their guardians are to have the option as to the trade or
profession they prefer. At present there are too many Native Training
Institutions (Wesleyan) such as Healdtown, Clarke- bury, Bensonvale, the
Ayliff Industrial Institute (Peddie), and Lesseyton, &c. And there are
still others to be establish­ed. All these are aided by the Confer­ence
; consequently large sums of money are wasted.

What is now required is a Native In­dustrial, Normal and Agricultural
In­stitute similar to the Hampton Institute, Va (U. S. A.), where all
trades such as carpentry, wagon making, printing, book­binding,
tailoring and agriculture, Ac., are taught and also the training of
girls for domestic employments. This could easily be done by Conference
if it were to make Healdtown the Institute as it is more central, or
some other suitable place. The objection which will be raised, if any,
will be that such a place would not be within the reach of all. To that
I would merely say, it would ; for the simple reason that Colonial
Railways are nearly all connected with each other, and students travel
at reduced fares; and lastly, but not least, that the Natives do now see
the value of education ; therefore they would send their sons and
daughters to such an Institute although they still rely on their
European friends for counsel and administration. Look at Lovedale
(though I do not say it is far ahead as there are only one or two
Natives who are matriculated), most of the above mentioned trades are
taught, and students flock there from all quarters of South Africa, from
Natal, Basutoland and Bechuanaland, &c. Are our Wesleyan Ministers
satisfied that our Native lads must go and seek training amongst the
other denominations? The hearts of the Native youths are eager for
higher education, and industrial and agricul­tural training. If
agriculture is taught at our Native Institute, then the Native youths
will be able to go and labour for farmers, and the Cape will be richer
than at present; and this will also be part o the solution of the Native
Labour Ques­tion. Hoping that this will cause some of our energetic
ministers to take the matter up at the approaching District Meetings.
Thanking you for insertion, I am, etc.,
