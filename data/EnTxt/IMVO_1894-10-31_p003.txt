Topics of the Day.

We note that the Cape Argus says that Mr. Dick's speech in praise of the
Glen Grey Act is a counterbalancing force against IMVO. It forgets that
IMVO merely expresses the views prevailing among the Natives, while Mr.
Dick, as a European, gives vent simply to the prejudices of a section of
his race.

Agreement-

We note that “ Batavius ” in Ons Land makes the prediction we made in
our last about the George election, that, notwith­standing apparent
differences of opinion on the merits of candidates at Mossel Bay, yet
the real fight is in George itself, where Mr. Thompson as a Wobbler will
have no chance against Mr. Searle the Opposition candidate. By a
reference to the records of the General Election we observe that the
Opposition candidates Messrs. Robertson and Vintcent scored
res­pectively 1787, 1708, while their Wobbler opponent, Mr. Barrington,
received 781 votes. Thus Mr. Thompson the Wobbler candidate has much
leeway to make up.

It is to be hoped that the constituency that has had so able a
representative as the late Mr, Vintcent will not consent to be bought
with money bags by the candi­date who flourishes them ; for thus it will
bring on itself lasting disgrace. We observe that Mr. Thompson’s
candidature is being urged on with much noise, pomp and circumstance;
but in a con­stituency like George the victory is not invariably with
those who are capable of making the most noise, and we still hug the
hope that the De Beers dummy candidate will be sent away empty when the
result of the Ballot becomes known. That at George Mr. Thompson at his
own public meeting has been accepted by the narrow majority of
thirty-nine; while the most influential, because sensible and best known
man in the District—Arch­deacon Fogg, who is, besides, one of the ablest
political leaders in the South-West —has strongly espoused the cause of
Mr. Searle, shows that the booming of Mr. Thompson is not going to carry
the con­stituency.

For the information of the Watchman it is necessary to explain that the
Editor of this paper appeared at the R.M. Court in a friendly way for a
poor friend who could not afford legal expenses. The victim was being
prosecuted for carrying on the trade of a general dealer without a
licence. True, the accused lost the case although no attempt was made by
the prosecution to sustain the charge. Super­intendent Smith as
prosecutor led evidence to prove that bread had been bought at the
accused’s coffee house ; and what we can’t understand is that this
should be deemed sufficient evidence to support a charge of selling
without a general dealer’s licence seeing the law is clear on the point,
describing a general

Dealer as “ any person who carries on the trade or business of selling,
or offering or exposing for sale, barter or exchange any goods, wares or
merchandise, not being the growth, produce, or manu­facture of South
Africa.” No evidence was attempted to prove that the bread was not
manufactured in Kingwilliamstown, with Colonial wheat without which the
charge should have been dismissed; and the Watchman seizes this as an
occasion to crow over the Editor for having lost his case! It certainly
was not the fault of the Editor that the party lost.

The Cause of the Labour Difficulty.

On this head the East London Standard writes:—The conclusion come to by
the members of the Labour Commission, which traversed the country
recently, was that where Native servants were pro­perly paid and cared
for there was no deficiency in the supply of labour. That was what many
of us had declared long before the Commissioner sat, but it was worth
while spending a few thousands to have it officially so recorded. To
give their servants a miserable dole of a few shillings a month, and
where possible to do them out of even that upon some cooked-up charge or
other, as many farmers of a certain class do, and then to make a fuss
and call for legislation be­cause they find labour difficult to obtain,
its—well, it is what it is! A specimen of his kind of treatment came
before the Circuit Court at Queenstown the other day. A farmer named
Goosen prosecuted a Native boy in his employ for theft of six boxes of
matches and some tea and coffee.' It transpired that the Native ser­vant
had been hired at 6s per month, and had been there nine months without
re­ceiving a penny—Goosen intending, so he said, to pay him at the end
of the year! The prisoner in his defence said that the case was trumped
up against him because Goosen wanted to get out of pay­ing him any
wages, now eight months overdue. We are very glad to say that the jury
took this view of this case, and acquited the prisoner; the judge also
commenting very severely upon Goosen’s conduct. These be the men to
whom, alas, Glen Grey Natives are only too often to be compelled to go
out and contract themselves, and for whom we are asked to institute
something like compulsory labour!

  LETTER TO THE PAPERS.

  STR,—Few people in England under-
  stand the significance of the Glen Grey
  Act, which was rushed through the Cape
  Parliament a few weeks ago, and to
  which, with equal haste, the Royal Assent
  was given on 15th August.

  A measure thus sanctioned by the
  Crown, after its acceptance by a self-
  governing colony, is difficult to deal with,
  but it can be protested against, and the
  protest may have some effect. I hope,
  therefore, that you will accord space for
  the following copy of a modest appeal
  that has been addressed to Lord Ripon.
  The Glen Grey Act applies only, in the
  first instance, to the Natives in one com-
  paratively small portion of the Eastern
  Province of Cape Colony; but it can be
  extended, by proclamation, to all the
  others.

  In his speeches, while forcing it through
  the Cape Legislature, Mr. Cecil Rhodes
  showed more than his usual frankness
  and cynicism. He wants, as he says, to

crush out the Natives, who are “ a dis­tinct source of trouble and loss
to the country ”—i.e., to its white masters; and this, he thinks, may be
done by parcel­ling out their land into allotments of which a favoured
few are to be “ regis­tered holders,” paying a “ perpetual quit­rent,”
with the power of transferring their rights to any land grabbers who can
make terms with them, and by establish­ing a system of “ forced labour ”
among all the others. This tyrannical project will surely not be
approved by right- minded Englishmen, even though, in the event of
trouble arising, it may not be difficult for the white rulers of Cape
Colony to over-awe the Natives, who are four or five times as numerous.

I am, sir,

  Your obedient servant, H, R. Fox BOURNE.

A LETTER TO THE SECRETARY OF STATE FOR THE COLONIES.

MY LORD,—I have the honour, by direc­tion of the Committee of the
Aborigines Protection Society, to address your Lordship with reference
to the Glen Grey Act which has recently been passed by the Legislature
of Cape Colony, and which received the Royal Assent on 15th August.

Our Committee is aware that there are weighty reasons against
interference by Her Majesty’s Government with a legis­lative project
which has been approved by the Colonial Parliament, and to which the
Royal Assent has been given; but, as many of the provisions of the Glen
Grey Act appear to it to be open to grave objection, and, as the
enforcement of those provisions is in some measure left by the Act to
the discretion of the Governor oi the Colony, our committee earnestly
and respectfully appeals to your Lordship in favour of such exercise of
the authority of the Crown as will minimise the injury to Native
interests which it threatens. The provisions of the Act which in our
Com­mittee’s opinion, are especially open to objection may be briefly
indicated as follows :—

(1) The proposal to divide all the arable land not already Anally
    disposed of in the Glen Grey District into small allotments, each of
    which shall or may be assigned to “ a registered holder ” paying a “
    perpe­tual quit-rent ” for it, and thus to intro­duce a system of
    individual ownesrhip, appears to be an innovation likely to be much
    less advantageous to any but a few of those who are able and willing
    to avail themselves of it, and also to the commu­nity as a whole,
    than the communal arrangement, based on long-established and wide
    spread Native institutions, which has hitherto prevailed.

(2) Although in the first instance the proposed allotments are to be
    granted only to approved Natives, provision is made in Section and
    Schedule B of the Act for their alienation and transfer, without any
    stipulation that the registered holder shall not be at liberty to
    dispose of his title to a purchaser who is not a Native of the
    District or of his own race. There is great risk therefore unless
    pre­cautions are taken to prevent such a result that a considerable
    portion of the land will ultimately fall into the hands of strangers
    and that the Natives, for whom, probably, there will not at present
    be a sufficient number of allotments, will in course of time be to a
    large extent dis­placed by European and other outsiders,

(3) The imposition of a heavy “ labor tax,” the nonpayment of which
    involves very severe penalties upon all male Natives who are not
    holders of allotments and who fail to obtain service or employ­ment
    beyond the borders of the district appears to be a hard and
    unwarrantable interference with the liberty of the sub- j^bf, not to
    be excused on the plea put forward by the advocates of the Act in
    the Cape Legislature that the black inhabit­ants of the Glen Grey
    District are too numerous, and that “a gentle stimulant to induce
    the barbarian to work” is ex­pedient in the interests of the white
    popu­lation of the Colony.

(4) Commendable as may be the inten­tion of organising a form of local
    self- government in Glen Grey it appears that in the proposed
    council, the Natives will have no opportunity of managing their own
    affairs, seeing that six of the twelve councillors over whom the
    Resident Magistrate is to preside will be elected by or in the name
    of the Governor and that the other six are only to be such persons
    as the authorities “ may deem fit.”

On these and other grounds, our Com­mittee ventures to hope that Her
Majesty’s Government will take such action as it may consider expedient
and practicable to limit and control the opera­tion of the Act with a
view to the protec­tion of the interests of the Natives, net only in the
Glen Grey District but also in those “other districts or portions of
districts in which the inhabitants are mainly aboriginal Natives” to
which it is provided that the Act “in whole or in part with such
modifications as may be necessary,” can be extended by Procla­mation. As
a very large number of the Natives in Cape Colony and its de­pendencies
are thus directly or indirectly affected, our Committee respectfully
urges that the Governor should be in­structed to use all the discretion
allowed to him both as regards the enforcement of the Act within the
Glen Grey District, and as regards its application to other parts of the
Colony.

  I have the honour to be My Lord,

  Your Lordship’s most obedient servant H. R. Fox BOURNE,

Secretary, The Most Honourable,

  The Marquis of Ripon, K.G., Secretary of State, Colonial Office.
