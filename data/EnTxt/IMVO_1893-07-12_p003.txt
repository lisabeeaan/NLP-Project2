THE Session is now about PARLIAMENT, a month-old. Apart from the
interesting onslaughts on Sir James Sivewright’s obliquity by his
ex-colleagues, its other characteristic so far has been the power of the
three ex­Ministers, who seem to have had every­thing pretty much their
own way. It is quite evident that Messrs. Sauer, Merri­man and Inues,
have raised themselves immensely, both in the estimation of the House
and of the country by their action in giving up office for principle,
and the immediate effect of it is that the House looks to them, not to
the discredited Ministry,‘for any declaration of saving principles.
Several times have the measures of the Government been modi­fied or
rejected at the suggestion by the new Opposition, to such an extent is
the country becoming impressed with this that the following from the
Port Eliza­beth Telegraph may fairly be taken as ex­pressing the general
feeling of all distant observers of what is doing in the House : —Since
the Ministry has no policy to submit to the country, and, further, since
it is apparent that the men in office will be willing to give expression
in Parlia­ment to the wishes of the majority of the- representatives
returned at the eusuing contest, let the leaders of the so-called
Progressive party sound the battle call. We do not know who must be
regarded as the leader of the Opposition at the present moment.
Probably, were the truth known, the three politicians who sacrificed
office in defence of Ministerial purity, have decided to act in concert.
Be that as it may, they have strengthened their position by reason of
their repudi­ation of the Logau contract, and a well thought-out policy
of reform drawn up by them would be sure to receive the close attention
of electors. If there was a time when it was possible to form a strong
Progressive Party, it is at the pre­sent juncture of affairs/ Messrs.
Merri­man, Innes and Sauer are already at the head of a powerful
combination. Their followers are, it is true, in a minority as regards
numbers; but, guaged by the standard of merit, ability, and earnestness
of purpose, they are first amongst Colonial politicians. That party
requires to be welded into solidity and to appeal to the country as an
organised band of reformers. Let the leaiersgive them a policy to fight
for and we are convinced that they would fight at the ensuing election
with success.

CAPACITY FOR EDUCATION.

  HE Express of Bloemfontein
  attempts a reply to our

strictures on Mr. SCHREINER’S
theory that Natives should not be
educated until a thousand years
have elapsed, seeing that the Euro-
peans have taken that time to be
what they are to-day. Of course
we scouted the idea as absurd,
maintaining that our people have
been fortunate enough to live in a
time when they have only to utilise
advantages already secured by the
thousand years of European plod-
ding ; and it would be incredible
folly on their part to refuse to avail
themselves of them on the pretext
put forward by the Cape Attorney-
General. We illustrated our idea
by a causeway which was so many
years in building, saying that those
who subsequently travel along it
have not to take as many years as it
took the builders to construct it.
Our clever contemporary at Bloem-
fontein thinks it has disposed of the
matter by hurling at us the follow-
ing sentences:—

That is surely skimming the surface
of the whole question. How is it that
when a Native gets on a well-constructed
causeway, he generally manages to break
his legs and what not, in using that com-
fortable and safe thoroughfare? Why
does education spoil a good nigger?
Let IMVO tell us, and let us assure NATIVE
OPINION in the meantime, that painting
a black man white does not make a white
man of him, but only ruins his natural
complexion, and by interfering with the
action of his porous epidermis leads to
greater inward mischief. We shall aim
at the same thing, that is to preserve a
good nigger, and to improve a bad one;
but 50 years’ experience has taught us
that technical education has to precede
all book-learning, and technical educa-
tion means for him the learning of those
arts and handicrafts which form the
occupation of an inferior labouring class.
To define it: Chop wood, carry water,
dig, plough, drive and herd cattle, and
do all of it—if possible intelligently and
faithfully.

To our mind it is the Express that
skims the surface of the question.
Let us analyse its arguments for a
little. We are asked, to begin
with, “ How it happens that when
“ a Native gets on a well-con-

“ manages to break his legs and
“ what not in using that comfort-
“ able thoroughfare ? Why does
“ education spoil a good nigger ?

the position whether a Native
should be educated or not at all.
At any rate a journal published in

it is paltering with the subject to
