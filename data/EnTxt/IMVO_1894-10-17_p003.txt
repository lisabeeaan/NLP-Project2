Topics of the Day.

Good News.

The telegraphic news to hand shows the good sense of the Mossel Bay
electors, and gives assurances that Mr. Vintcent’s shade will have great
potency in the election there. Should it be so it will be right for the
constituency and the coun­try too. The way Mr. Frank Thompson has been
heralded by that fortune hunter Mr. Davis Alien who as a railway
pro­moter presumes to speak for George, in the scandalous open-letter to
the Leader of the Opposition has calised disgust among right thinking
men. Mr. Davis Allen has sought to corrupt politics by throwing to the
four winds of heaven right principles in regard to political actions in
general and for filthy lucre has cast his lot with Mr. Thompson’s
candidature after having promised to support Mr. Sichel. The electors
have, to their credit, seen that they were being exploited in the
interests of unprin­cipled politicians, and done the right thing under
the circumstances. They have listened to Mr. Davis Allen and Mr.
Thompson, “heckled” them, and un­animously resolved not to accept the
can­didature of that gentleman. This will tell throughout the
constituency, and the wisest course lor Mr. Thompson is to

accept the inevitable and retire; for we are much mistaken if at George
itself, famed for its intelligent electors he does not receive similar
treatment.

Er. Sichel’s Candidature-

Mr. G. Sichel is said to be receiving good support in George; and the
electors will do themselves credit in the eyes of the country generally
if they return a man so able, and imbued with straight and correct views
withal. The Cape Times last to hand states that his candidature is
likely to have the advantage of the advo­cacy of the Opposition, of
which the late conscientious representative was a mem­ber, on the spot;
and the politics of the gentlemen comprising that Party would appear to
have always claimed the admi­ration of the intelligent electorate of
George,

The Way we are Governed

The whole country has, as it were, stood aghast with astonishment, at
the shame less and shamefull manner its present rulers have offered
lucrative posts in its service to leading political opponents. It has
been reported that the Rhodes Minis­try has again offered the position
of General Manager of railways to Mr. Merriman, who, to his infinite
credit has declined the offer with disdain. Then the newly created post
of Assistant Attorney- General, said to be lucrative has been offered to
Mr. Richard Solomon, who also refused the bribe. Such conduct on the
part of the Ministry goes a long way in un­dermining public confidence
in the administration of affairs, but the hope of the country lies in
the existence of up­right men like Mr. Merriman and Mr. Solomon who will
steadfastly refuse to demean themselves and the country.

TRANSKEI LOCAL GOVERNMENT

THERE was no time nor space last week for comments on the Transkei Local
Government Proclamation. Shallow and super­ficial people have described
the Proclamation as the introduction of the Glen Grey Act in Fingoland ;
but it is nothing of the sort. As was incidentally remarked last week in
these columns, the Proclamation does not even quote the Glen Grey Act
once. It begins simply with “ Whereas it is expedient that provision
should be made for the “ establishment of Councils for the ^((i)
administration of local affairs within certain districts in the “
Territory of the Transkei, and for “ other matters affecting the
inhabitants of these districts; Now, “ therefore,” etc , etc , etc This
paper has always been in favour of anything in the direction of trusting
people with the management of their local affairs ; and we were among
the first to hail the Prime Minister’s suggestion to make an experiment
in the matter in Fingoland. That opinion has not undergone any change,
and we again welcome with pleasure the local government prin­ciple of
the Proclamation before us, although reserving to ourselves the liberty
to dissent from certain de­tails of the instrument. The scheme of the
Government seems to us far too elaborate and complicated to be
satisfactorily curried out by a people in the stage of enlightenment at
present attained by the generality of the Natives, and we shall be very
much surprised if its working does not entail upon the Magistrates a
responsibility which, with their or­dinary duties, they will find it
irk­some and difficult adequately to dis­charge ; for, comparatively
speaking, they may look for no assistance from those who are likely to
be their col­leagues in regard to the carrying out of nine-tenths of the
multifari­ous and important duties entrusted to the Councils over which
they are to preside. It requires men used to taking a broad view of
questions touching the public good ; and not trained (as Magistrates may
be said to have been trained) to thinking in leading strings—carrying
out in­structions, scarcely assuming re­sponsibilities on themselves —
to carry out the considerable general powers conceded in the
Proclama­tion. Our fear, in these circum­stances, is that the machinery
de­vised by the Government for the districts of Butterworth, Idutywa,
Ngqamakwe, and the Tsomo may break down from its own weight. It would
have been more satisfactory if, as Captain VELDTMAN re marked at Major
ELLIOT’S meeting with the Fingoes, they had been assigned one or two
specific things to deal with; the rest being gra­dually added as they
showed competence in dealing with these. But the Government have arrived
at another decision. All then we can do now’ is to express the hope that
when the results which they hope to compass by this means are not
achieved they will not blame the people because of inability to gulp
down their mammoth scheme. The Councils are nominee ones. To this we do
not oppose strong demurrers, although the people should also have been
entrusted with and thus interested in the work of their two Councils -
the one District and the other General — by being allowed to elect a
certain proportion of representatives on these Boards. The election
machi nery need not have been a showy one. At their home pitsos the
people might have been allowed to send their own representatives to the
Boards; for with the Headmen or Magistrates the Boards are too much the
hands of paid servants

of the Government and can scarcely be regarded as representative of the
people. They thus run the risk of becoming isolated and out of touch
with the community. It is a thousand pities this principle of local
Government was not given a trial by itself without being intro­duced in
connection with a Land measure of a highly contentious character. But
while we accept the principle in regard to the Transkei, we still shall
protest as earnestly as we can against the vicious land provisions of
the Glen Grey Act.

Current Opinion.

THE GLEN GREY ACT AGITATION.

[The Northern Post (Aliwal North).]

By its publication in the “ Gazette ” the Glen Grey Act is now appealing
to us as a real thing. It was difficult a few weeks ago to think of it
in any other light than that of an academic exercise. It smelt of oil.
It was the sort of revolutionary measure that most of us are in the
habit of drawing up for the reformation of our neighbours or for the
establishment of society on a new basis. The Glen Grey- Act is admirable
in all its details. Unfor - tunately, it is an attempt to gather fruit
before the tree has reached the fruit-bear­ing stage. It is an
experiment with the Natives. And experimenting with a million of Natives
against their wish may be magnificent sport, and exciting to a degree,
but so is larking in and around a powder-magazine with a cigar in ore’s
mouth. The end thereof may be peace, but it is more likely to be pieces.
It is ominous for the future tranquility of the land that IMVO is
exerting itself against . the Act “totis viribus.” Now, IMVO is not, has
never been, a disloyal paper. Its influence is distinctly on the side of
the governing class, but it has every justification for its present
action. The Glen Grey Act is not a Government measure. It is not the
expression of the deliberate and carefully-thought opinion of
Parliament, It has not been called for by the Press as a voicing the
desire of a people. It is an audacious leap in the dark, neither more
nor less. 11 it were mere question cf whether Mr. Rhodes lands safely on
terra firma or breaks his neck, it would concern the Colony but little,
but unfortunately it is just the Colony which must jump along with him.

There is at present a luminous halo around Mr. Rhodes. By a combination
of luck, good financing clever share operations and conquest, he has
become a much talked of man. But lie may be that and be as far from
being a statesman as the Duke of Wellington, groat soldier as ho was,
showed himself to be. Now, only a man possessed of the instincts of a
statesman can interfere in Native affairs. If our object is to cause
suspicion and annoyance in the Native mind so that we may have thr
opportunity of employing against him the repeating rille and the Maxim
gun, we can safely give Mr. Rhodes carte blanche. But if we want to win
them to our views, to loyalty to a spontaneous development into a higher
social life, we had better clearly recognize the fact that Mr. Rhodes
should be one of the last men in the Colony to whom we should entrust
the task. Against the Act itself, as an Act, there is no objection. Its
aim is the benefit of the Native if he could be got to see it. As the
Scotch once said a propos of a ceitdn marriage ’twas not the match they
objected to but the manner of the wooing.

[Fort Beaufort Advocate],

If Mr. Rhodes flattered himself that his Glen Grey Bill could be passed
and be­come law without discussion, he made a sad mistake. If he thought
the Natives were such consummate asses as to allow themselves to be
converted into semi slaves, to please our Premier and his bond
coadjutors and supporters, then Mr. Rhodes showed himself remarkably
ignorant of human nature. The Bond have the Premier under their thumb
and they will keep him under: he may struggle to free himself as much as
he pleases, but the more he struggles the firmer will he become
entangled.

Mr. Rhodes courted the Bond to advance his personal schemes of ambition
and advantage, and they were quite willing to assist him only on
condition that one good turn deserves another.

The only redeeming feature in the Bill is there is to be no compensation
to canteen keepers. Compensation, indeed to a parcel of bloodsuckers who
do all that lies in their power to ruin the country—mere vermin who are
a perfect curse. Well-conducted hotels are neces­sary for accommodation
of the travelling public; but what accommodation do canteens afford?
None, absolutely none. Yet, but for the action of the Opposition' these
pes's would have received large sumsofinouey from the pockets of the
taxpayers.

The colossus of Rhodes was sold to a Jew who loaded up 900 camels with
the brass it contained. How many loads of brass does our colossus and
his helpers contain, that they shamelessly stick to office after having
been defeated upon so potent a measure?

Can anything be conceived morr des­picable than men, calling themselves
politicians, holding on to office with the tenacity of limpets for the
sake of emolu­ments, after having suffered such a deci­ded defeat?

What a piece of hypocrisy on the part of the Cape Times to advise the
Glen Grey Natives to accept the Act as children ac­cept a sugar plum. Do
not agitate, you will only make matters worse. Be’good children, and Mr.
Rhodes and the Bond will be ever so kind to you. The Bond loves you
dearly, you know, and would only appropriate your lands to save labour
of cultivating them.

But the Natives are not to be gulled by such flimsy pretences. They
know- better; and Queen Victoria’s word is pledged that their rights are
to be respec­ted. To Her Majesty they will appeal to a certainty, and
the Bond will be defeated in its nefarious project of getting
posses­sion of the Glen Grey district. If the Glen Grey Natives were
ousted from their possessions, how long would it be before all other
Native communities would be plundered-legally plundered of course— by
Acts of Parliament obtained by Bond influence.

That the Natives of South Africa are angels of goodness, no one knowing
them will for a moment contend ; but they are entitled to justice
equally with all other races under the folds of the Union Jack
