[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

SIR,—In the debate on the third read­ing of the Glen Grey Bill, Mr.
Rhodes is reported to have said that the South African Methodist
approved of the Bill. Such an expression of approval may lead some to
think that the South African Methodist is the official organ of the
Methodist Church, and conveys the sentiments of the Methodist people on
this Bill. I wish to correct any such impressions. The South African
Method­ist is a purely private venture, and con­veys no opinions except
such as the Editor is responsible for. At one time the paper was edited
by a Wesleyan Minis­ter whom Conference requested to dis­charge those
functions. This arrange­ment terminated some two years ago, and I am
certain that the politics of the South African Methodist—however
faith­ful they may echo those of the Journal, and of a party in and
about Grahamstown—do not represent the opinions of the great bulk of the
people called Methodists. The South African Methodist Church has no
official organ. Many think it ought to have one. But pending the
establishment of a paper by the Methodist Church, it is as well that
your numerous readers should know that Mr. Rhodes in citing the South
Af­rican Methodist as approving of his Bill really only quoted the
individual editor of that paper as agreeing with him.

NATIVE VILLAGE MANAGEMENT.

We wish to make a study of this ques­tion, and with that object in view
have referred to the report of the Native La vs and Customs Commission,
1882 to see what the Commissioners have to say upon this matter. In
paragraph 127 page 47, they say, under the heading of “Mu­nicipal
Government The Commis­sion cannot say that its enquiries have led to the
observation of anything like a general desire, or indeed any sort of
aptitude amongst the Native tribes for the management of Local
Government, or Municipal affairs, as understood by us. All their
national traditions are in favour of government of the village or kraal
by the headman in the same way as the tribe is governed by the chief;
and when these authorities have been removed, there has been decidedly
manifested a readiness to submit to the personal administration of the
Magistrate, or the personal influence of the Missionary, as the case
maybe; and often with the best results. No specific legislation in the
direction of municipal self-government is, therefore, recommended now
(1882). It is however suggested that the requirements of villa­ges. and
mission communities, where the Native element prevails, can at present
be supplied by the jurisdiction of the Magistrate in the one case, and
the mis­sion station officers in the other. The Village Management Acts
of the Colony are adapted for any communities, show­ing a healthy desire
for popular govern­ment in municipal affairs, and the general municipal
and district laws of the Colony will meet any cases possibly occurring
of greater advance, and where the people have acquired the superior
qualifications afforded by education and civilization.”

“ THE HAPPY MEAN.”

There are two great dangers, both of which have to be avoided. The one
is stagnation ; the other is drowning. If the Natives are so far left to
themselves, the danger is that of stagnation, or even degeneration. If
the full glare of civili­zation is laid upon them, the danger is
drowning. They do not understand : they are overwhelmed. So that the
question for us to consider is whether it is possible to strike between
the two ; whether it is possible to find the “ happy mean,” and apply it
for the advancement of the people, and so awaken the spirit of
self-help, if not of self-reliance.

That it is possible to do so, we desire to show. It has been sug­gested
in a criticism of our land scheme, that we write on what we know nothing
about, in dealing with these questions. Far be it from us to deny that
such is the case, and further still to estimate that our attempt in this
direction deals with the questions from a point of view beyond
improvement by anyone else. Indeed, we are quite con­scious that in the
list of those competent to deal with the solution of the African Native
Problem, in all its intricacies, we take only a humble place.
Neverthe­less, we cannot help offering expression of these views in
these papers, for what they may be worth, as contributions to the
present discussion of the Native Question, for the consideration of
abler minds. In doing this, we have en­deavoured to the best of our
ability to lay down definite and practical proposals rather than
theoretical ideas. Should they serve the purpose of setting think- ing ;
or of stepping stones for the work­ing of the problem by abler minds of
European or any other descent, who are as anxious as we are for the real
and permanent advancement of our country­men, the writer will have been
amply repaid. He knows full well that there are not a few Europeans
whose minds are thus exercised, who are glad to a clear expression of
Native view on these questions as an index whereby they may be compared
with other views, and place them in a position of formulating a just and
fair policy. As instances of what is here meant, your readers might be
direct­ed to two admirable lectures delivered, respectively at Cape
Town, by Mr, Wm. Hay, M.L.A., on “Frontier Natives,” and at Burghersdorp
by Mr. E. Jukes Knight on “ The Native Question.” CONSTITUTION OF THE
VILLAGE MANAGE­MENT.

In order to make ourselves clear, let us see how a Board of Management
would be constituted, and what would be its powers, and duties. We have
examined very carefully the “Village Management Act of 1881 ” ; and
though it is, no doubt, a very suitable Act for the purpose for which it
is designed, it requires to be modified somewhat for the purpose we have
in view.

There are two principles of action that have to be applied in Native
reconstruc­tion. The one is (1) To work up gradual­ly from the Native
standpoint, so as to carry the Native mind, and will, along the steps of
progress. We have acted upon this principle in our suggestion of a land
settlement. The other is (2) Adapt­ation,—to adapt European ideas to
Native ideas. In granting Village Management to Natives, we require to
act upon this principle of adaptation. To apply the Village Management
Act, 1881, as it stands, to Native villages would be to overwhelm the
people. They would fail to grasp the requirements of it, and they would
therefore remain inert. So, we have to adapt its provisions to a simpler
system of village management to one that they can more readily
understand, and grasp. The great point is to establish such a system as
part and parcel with the settlement of the Land Question. That is to
say, let the whole system of village management be based, primarily,
upon the interest of the Natives in the land. Let them realize that they
become citizens principally in respect of their occupation of land, and
that they must undertake gradually the duties and responsibilities of
such citizens in respect of their “ land­ed interest.”

1.  Electors and Election.—We would, therefore, suggest that the
    electors of the village managers should be owners, or possessors of
    the different lots in the village, and the election should take
    place before the Resident Magistrate. The Board of Management should
    consist of, say, fifteen of their own number, five of whom would
    retire in rotation every year. The last five on the list would
    retire first, the next five the second year, and the first five
    would retire the third year, and so on in rotation. The five who
    retire would not be in­eligible for re election. The voting would be
    viva voce to the R.M., and, perhaps, at first it might be open
    voting as an education, and to gain the confidence of the people. A
    smaller number, say seven, of the Board would form a quorum to carry
    on the business, in the event of the non-attendance of the whole
    Board.

2.  Chairman,—The method of nominat­ing a chairman, or president would
    be this. The 15 would nominate three of their own number for the
    post, and leave it to the R.M. to decide which of these three should
    be chairman, or president.

The whole fifteen would have the status of “headmen,” and the duties
that are at present laid upon the headman, or chief, would be
transferred to all and each of them. And in all their acts within the
scope of their prescribed duties, they would have the protection of the
Resident Magistrate,

3.  Meeting and Conferences. — There would be three kinds of meetings
    amongst themselves:

(a) Meetings of the Board of Management might be held once a month on a
    recognized date ; to be called by the Chairman ; and failing him, by
    any live members.

(b) Public Meetings or Assemblies of the Electors of the Village might
    be held twice a year to be called by the Chairman of the Board upon
    certain fixed dates; and failing the chairman, by five members of
    the Board, or the Magistrate, who on any special occasion can always
    call a public assembly,

(c) Meetings or Conferences with the Magistrate. These joint
    conferences, when any occasion for such arose, would be called by
    the Resident Magistrate ; but upon any occasion when the Board
    wished, or were required by the law to consult the Magistrate, the
    Boards would notify the fact to him, and he would call the meeting
    or assembly.

All acts requiring to be done, would be enforced by the Resident
Magistrate as representing the Government, provided these acts had gone
through the initial stages, and were agreed upon, at their respective
meetings.

4. The lowers and Duties of the Board would, primarily, lie connected
with the village lots, and commonage, but in addi­tion to these, there
are many other duties that could be fulfilled by them. In our next
paper, concluding this series, we purpose, therefore, to sketch out
under four heads, the various duties and powers which they might quite
well be called upon to perform ; it not all at first, then, gradually,
as they evinced the aptitude.
