THE Responsible Government fever continues to run high in Natal. The
elections about to take place are being fought on the question whether
that Colony is to be governed on the present lines, or that Responsible
Government should be introduced. Those who, like the Cape Colonists,
have had the form of Government which some of the Natal politicians
regard as the panacea for all ills, cannot resist a smile at times when
advocates of Responsible Government say that the sister Colony will be
better served as regards the conduct of public business under
Responsible Government than under the present system. Among the other
hallucinations of the fever that has seized Natalians may be mentioned
the fear that Natal is to be absorbed in the Cape Colony if Responsible
Govern­ment is not demanded at once, also that it, cannot enter properly
into negotiations with the other Colonies and States if without
Responsible Government It bodes ill for the introduction of the new form
of Government when those who fight for it do not scruple when it suits
their purposes to disfranchise the coloured classes as they have
attempted lately, and nearly succeeded but for the Supreme Court. The
advocates of Res­ponsible Government by resorting to such expedients
only make outsiders think they are wishing to establish an oligarchy
under the guise of Responsible Government, and it would be unwise to
entrust an oligarchy with the full freedom and absolute control of the
Natives.

WE have been placed by the Secretary of the Newspaper Press Union in
pos­session of the conditions imposed, by the Government, on journalists
in connec­tion with the free conveyance of “ Ex­changes” by post. Our
sense of gratifica­tion at this concession was expressed last I week ;
and while we have no intention of withdrawing anything we said, we
con­fess to a feeling of disappointment that the concession should have
been accompanied by conditions which we cannot in regard as a leetle
excessive, strongly redolent of the fumes of burning red-tape and
sealing--vax. We had been under the opinion that Mr. Sauer, whose
depart­ment has to do with the matter, would have been a little more
practical, and, as a good Radical, not so strait-laced as to insist on
petty and subtle distinctions, for example, as that newspaper wrappers,
which will be passed free by the Post Office, being “addressed to the
‘Editor of,’ in his capacity as such, and bear on the cover the words
Exchange copy» with the signature of the Editor or Pub­lisher
transmitting the newspaper, at­tached.” Why insist on “ exchange copy ”
being written ? The very fact that a copy is sent from one newspaper to
another is conclusive evidence that it is an “Exchange.” Then what is
the signature of the Editors to attest? Had anyone versed in journalism
been in the Colonial Secretary’s office he would have known that
Editorial work leaves no time or in­clination for signing newspaper
wrap­pers. There are 75 newspapers published, in the Colony. Is the
Editor of the Cape Times to write his name to the wrappers of all the
papers sent as “ Exchanges ” to I all these papers every day. We seem to
hear the maledictions that will be heaped on the devoted head of the
official who sends his gifts forth accompanied by such conditions when
it comes to the attach­ing of one’s signature to a pile of 75 labels. It
will be said that it does not consort with good manners to look a gift
horse in the mouth. Perhaps it is so; but being so much identified with
the giving party we may be permitted to advise that- the gift be as free
and unfettered by impossible conditions as not to render it valueless or
worse than valueless.

Countries like Natal that cannot possess their souls in patience, impressed by the laziness of the Kafir, have even risked being placed under a ban occasionally by introducing Asiatics with all their vices and epidemics. But no sooner does the Native come forward as labourer than the only inducement—good wages—is cut short in the manner proposed. He is driven to labour on starvation allowances. The result is he will return to his kraal and the companies will be compelled to import labour from abroad. For the sake of the Natives themselves the resolution of the Gold Companies is to be regretted. There can be no question that the country generally derives good from bands of Natives coming forward to enter the labour market. It increases its purchasing power, leading to a general rise in revenue obtained from customs duties. Thus the money paid to Native labourers is spent in the country, and indirectly the Companies get the money back, since the rising in the customs leads to the reduction of taxation now falling directly on the Companies. Discouraging Natives from work, contracts the purchasing power of the community, with the result that Governments keep their claws on rich companies, when revenue from customs seems to decrease. Looked at from this point of view, then, the proposal to reduce wages for Kafir labour, which can only end in discouraging Natives from coming forward to work is as short sighted as feeding a hungry dog on its tail. This is much to be deplored as the development of industries such as the Diamond and Gold mining are the only opportunities on a gigantic scale which offer themselves for teaching the black inhabitants of this country the pleasures and dignity of labour for its own sake. The opportunity is lost when schemes are contrived carefully calculated to cause these people not to come forward at all with the labour which the country needs so much. For these reasons we hope the proposal of the Gold Companies will receive no countenance from the press of the country.

The preparations in the Legislative Council election have during the past week being entered upon in earnest in the Border Circle, the Bond, as usual, leading the way. A meeting of the Provincial Bestuur was held in Queenstown last week to decide upon the candidate the Bond should support for this Circle. The result was, that although Messrs. Snyman (Komgha), and P. van Heerden (Tarka) had, at a public meeting previously, announced themselves as candidates, they were passed by. Mr. David de Wet of Aliwal North being nominated as Bond candidate. Mr. de Wet it may be mentioned, sat for Aliwal North, in the last Parliament as Mr. Sauer’s colleague. As a member of the lower House he showed himself a moderate and sensible Bondman. It remains to be seen, however what sort of an appearance a Bondman can make in the Border Circle.
