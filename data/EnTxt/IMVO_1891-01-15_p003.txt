Sir,—Something disgusting, or amusing
to those who choose to says so, has been
done in connection with the Educational
Department of the Edendale Mission. A
native young man (Rev. J. J. Jabavu),
brother to Mr. J. Tengo-Jabavu, the
Editor of a kafir newspaper, NATIVE
OPINION, has had, a week ago, a cold
treatment from the supervisors of the
said Mission.

The Rev. J. J. Jabavu was appointed
by the last Wesleyan South African
Conference to be an assistant teacher at
the Edondale Training Institution. He
had to como from the Old Colony, and
could not. have taken his appointment
before next August, when schools re-open.
It is only a week ago since he came. We
were surprised to hear that when Mr.
Jabavu came, there was no house pro-
vided for him. He had to stay, and is
still staying, I presume, with one of the
head men of the station. Goodluck, ho
received him ! Notwithstanding the long
time since he was appointed, no steps
have been taken to build, hire, or prepare
a house for him. It was not till the day
he arrived at Pietermaritzburg by train,
that the boys of the institution were sent
to sweep and clear one of the old-style
built cottages of that Mission Station for
him. The cottage has two rooms, two
windows, and one door, and such cottage
was to be occupied by a man who is to
set an example of cleanliness and general
improvement, to teach and to preach—a
man who is put to a responsible and
respectable position—a man who has
received good education which qualifies
him for the position he is to take—a man
brought up among civilised communities.
It was quite right that he declined to take
that cottage for a dwelling. There is a
house connected with the buildings of
the Institutions which was occupied by
the headmaster of the Institute. It is

now empty, as he has removed into
another. It is difficult to find out the

reason why Mr. Jabavu cannot be given
that house to live in. One may be in-
clined to think that it is the colour which
renders Mr. Jabavu unlit for the house.

If that be so, what next? In vain we
speak of the coalescence or the nearer
approach between the blacks and whites.
If onr missionaries do not see it meet to
be hand-in hand with their Christian or

civilised natives, the colonists are quite
justified to treat them with contempt,
and even consider them as low savages.
It is an admitted fact that the Gospel has
been preached with success to the native
population ; but has the practical gospel
—the gospel of home-life fellowship been ?
I doubt to affirm. Missionaries call

Christian natives their children; but the
fact of their being children is not proved
practically. They call native ministers
brothers; but the state of brotherhood is

not plain, except among clergymen of the
Church of England.—I am, sir, yours, &c.,
