Topics of the Bay.

It is noticeable that no papers in Kafir have been set for 1st and 2nd
year candidates at the Pupil Teachers’ Examination conducted during the
past week or two, although in the notes at the foot of page 63,
Education Manual it is said that this would be done. Naturally the

East London Logic-

On the subject of the Glen Grey Act, as dealt with by us, the East
London Standard had the following the other day: —In the columns of the
Argus M”. Surveyor Murray corrects the ascertiou of IMVO regarding Mr.
Jenner’s com­mission in connection with the delimita­tion of Glen
Grey—that it was a mere “ marking time,” seeing that Glen Grey was
mapped out into blocks many years ago. Mr. Murray points out that
whereas these old blocks were forty-three in number, with no natural
boundaries, the result of Mr. Jenner’s work has been to reduce them to
eighteen, with well-de­fined natural boundaries. It is unfor­tunately
characteristic of our Native con­temporary never to admit an error, and
he thus contents himself with a simple reiteration of his previous
assertion, though that has been clearly shown to be quite erroneous. Mr.
Murray further says that having travelled all through the said blocks
with Mr. Jenner, he can speak with some little authority on the subject,
and can safely say that the agitation against the Act has in no wise
proceeded from the location itself, but has all been got up outside.
Which is what has struck us all along.—Simply because the Standard
elects to believe Mr. Murray’s ipse dixit does not necessarily follow
that our statement is incorrect. Our contemporary is misinformed as to
the assertion that it is a characteristic of this journal never to admit
an error. May it not, perhaps, be because we are so careful that the
Standard has never seen admissions of errors committed?

The Glen Grey Act-

The statements of European corres­pondents to country newspapers about
the satisfaction of the Natives with the Glen Grey Act should invariably
betaken with a pinch of salt. How they get at the true feelings of the
Natives, seeing that they do not mix freely with them, is a Chinese
puzzle to us. This as preface to the following item communi­cated to the
Frontier Guardian (Dordrecht):—“The Resident Magistrate of Lady Frere
(Mr. Sweeney) held a meet­ing at Buffeldoorns on the 15th inttant, when
over 250 Natives were present, and after Mr. Sweeney explained the
provisions of the Glen Grey Bill to them, they proceeded to nominate
three members for the Location Board as provided for by the Act. After a
little consultation they unanimously ap­pointed Headman Dolph Komanie,
Booy Fannie, and another Native. They appear to be well pleased with the
Bill, especially at having the management of their own location affairs.
The Natives appear to be well satisfied with their new Resident
Magistrate (Mr. C. J. Sweeney), and the Government, in appointing that
gentle­man in Lady Frere, has not only made a judicious selection as far
as the Natives are concerned, but has also satisfied the wishes of the
European inhabitants of Glen Grey. I expect to see Buffeldoorns become a
village in time, as it is centrally situated, and ere long to hear of a
Periodical Court being held there. On the following day a, meeting of
Natives was held at the Location of Zwartwater and Vaalbank, where
Council­lors were also appointed, and general satisfaction seems to have
been ex­pressed by the Natives with the Bill.”— What we. know is that
the people have no intention to disobey the Government, although they
moan to agitate against the law until the needful amendments an? made.
This is mistaken for satisfaction by simple folk.

  LAND AND PEOPLE.

[LETTER TO THE EDITOR].

  SIR,—If the Glen Grey Bill which has
  just been passed leads the Natives all
  over the country to strenuously guard
  their land rights, it will prove a blessing
  in disguise. It required such a drastic
  measure to open our eyes, and it is only
  to ourselves that we must look for any
  assistance. Government of any kind is
  generally a delusion, and the present
  government of civilised states in this
  18th Century — Ballot Acts notwith-
  standing—is wholly and solely a gov-
  ernment of the classes, by the classes,
  for the classes. The classes in the Colony
  stand for the white population, the
  masses might be taken to represent the
  coloured population. Much fun has
  been made by several of our Colonial
  papers of our dozen educated Natives,
  but they cannot get away from the fact
  that these same dozen men are intel-
  lectually superior to nine-tenths of the
  white population. Now it necessarily
  follows that if we have in these few
  years been able to give the Colony men
  able to compete with well educated
  whites in both trade and the professions,
  a system of Education rightly intro-
  duced would, in a very few years, make
  the coloured population, who would
  come under this Education, mentally
  equal, as at present they are physically
  superior, to the white and dominant
  race. Now all governments are com-
  posed of the ruling classes, which in our
  day mean the moneyed men (although
  there are many excellent men in our, as
  in most governments), and the natural
  result of getting power and money is the
  desire for more. The ever-increasing
  danger of modern civilisation is the
  growing power of capital and the terri-
  ble awful cheapness of labour and men.
  The rich are becoming richer, the poor
  are becoming poorer. It is of little use
  perhaps going into social problems at
  the present stage of the Native Ques-
  tion, but it is of the greatest necessity
  to know that no help can be expected
  from the present, or any other Govern-
  ment. Mr. Rhodes is not all that could
  be desired, and the power which he
  wields through his money is enormous,
  but were he not in power, some other
  equally as bad, from our point of view,
  would. The Irish tenant farmers, have
  been quarrelled over, in the House of
  Commons, for over a century, by Re-
  formers of every description, yet their
  present state is worse than ever. Thirty
  years ago there were 8,000,000 persons
  in Ireland, to-day there are 4,000,(XX).
  The manipulation of tribal rights is not
  confined to South Africa. All over the
  world the aggrandisement of the ruling
  classes at the expense of the crofter or
  small occupier, is going on. In Scot-
  land crofter rights were similar in many
  respects to the tribal tenure, the Dukes,
  who really were headmen, .exacted an
  annual payment, but lately they have
  disregarded the crofter’s rights alto-
  gether, (many of whom had been in
  possession for a dozen generations), and
  either evicted the crofters to make way
  for Deer forests, or sold the ground to
  some wealthy nabob for the same pur-
  pose. In this way many counties have
  been almost depopulated. This is a
  very suicidal policy for any nation to
  allow. The rural districts of Great
  Britain are a standing example of the
  rottenness of our boasted civilization.
  By the action of the English landowners
  the rural population is gradually di-
  mishing and the urban population in-
  creasing by steps and bounds.

  Native Education-

  We are continually being asked to
  deal with certain articles appearing in
  some of our contemporaries abusive of
  the Natives. But we are spared this duty
  in regard to one that was published in
  the Wynberg Times by the following
  sub-article, which appeared some weeks
  back in the columns of our Wynberg
  contemporary In our issue of 1st
  September last an article appeared
  enunciating the principle of Native
  education, being conducted on lines
  distinct from that, of whites, on the
  ground of the inherent and essential
  differences physical and otherwise
  between the two races. The subject is
  perhaps not suited for ordinary news-
  papers writing, being of an abstruse
  character involving serious matters,
  which can only be properly considered
  in well digested and carefully written
  essays, which would be worthy of a
  place more permanent than that of a
  newspaper column. This fact has been
  brought, home to us by the receipt of a
  letter from a respected correspondent
  in the Eastern Province, who living
  amongst the Natives and taking a deep
  interest in their welfare, is undoubtedly
  fully competent to criticise the views
  we had set forth, and he is good enough
  to express to us his dissent from the
  propositions laid down in the article in
  question. While fully maintaining the
  main issue that the race differences
  alluded to, render it essential that each
  should be subject to different treatment
  in the matter of education, we are
  willing to confess that the article
  mentioned was unduly severe in detail,
  and apt to be misconstrued. No one
  could be more rejoiced than we are at
  the good opinion expressed by our
  correspondent, of the result of Christian
  teaching among the Natives of the
  Border, and if they are capable of a
  higher standard of civilization than we
  had thought, it is a matter for profound
  congratulation. However imperfect,
  the article was intended to express the
  view that in the interests of Natives
  themselves, their education should be
  on different lines from that of whites,
  but the writer of the article had no wish
  to be offensive, and is willing to admit
  that the wording lays it open to a
  construction far different to that which
  had been intended. We have formerly
  in allusion to the Editor of IMVO,
  afforded a full need of appreciation of
  the sterling and the good qualities
  inherent in the Kafir race; and we
  accept the reproof of our valued corres-
  pondent in the same spirit of fair
  minded and straightforward criticism as
  it was given.

Only those who are familiar with the terrible life which the masses live
in our large towns, can fully appreciate these figures at their worth.
The rawest Native in the country, with his hut and mealie patch, leads a
better and more natural life, than three-fourths of that urban
population. Unless stringent measures are taken to guard against it,
traders and farmers will, in time, do the same with Native lands, that
the English landowner has done for the small occupier in England. With
their great flock of sheep, they yet covet the one little lamb and will
verneuk (I can’t get a better word) the Native out of his little right.
Farm servants there will be in plenty and the mines will be kept well
supplied but the independent occupiers of the soil will be few and far
between. Any re­form of existing conditions must come from the Native
himself individually and collectively, and with a little know­ledge of
how things are, and a firm resolve to help one another, great things may
yet be expected from the Bantu Race. I strongly deprecate working in the
mines. Not only is life and limb held cheaper than plant, but very wrong
notions and very bad habits are picked up. Let “ every rood of ground
maintain its man,” and with a knowledge of socialism as taught in
England at the present day by some of the best men England has. The
edu­cated Natives may be able to do incal­culable good to the great mass
of their uneducated brethern.

What is wanted in education for the Native is, (1st) A knowledge of
agricul­ture ; (2nd) a knowledge of the errors of civilisation, more
especially the evils connected with capital and labour; and (3rd) a real
knowledge of the ethics of Jesus the Nazarene, whose teaching as applied
to every day life—and apart from the mass of extraneous supersti­tion
which an ignorant people heaped upon his life—is the grandest social
system ever taught.

I trust to see a meeting of Headmen, irrespective of tribe, convened at
an early date,—say at King Williams Town, to disscuss the Glen Grey
Bill, and see what united action should be taken to render it as
ineffectual as possible, and keep the land as free as the air, two
things which God Almighty surely in­tended to be free to all mankind.

Wishing you God speed in your fight against this latest encroachment of
Native rights.

Current Opinion.

THE GLEN GREY ACT.

The (Cape Times'),

The silence of the majority during the forced passage of the Glen Grey
Bill was due to a prudential agreement. Though a politician bidding for
future support in the satisfaction of a great ambition might boldly avow
his dissent from the Premier’s objection to any alienation of Location
lands from the Native people, it would have been highly inconvenient
that the rank and file of the party should confess a delighted
expectation of such erain from the operation of the measure. But meeting
their constituents face to face these cautious legislators feel no
re­straint on their freedom of speech. They are discharged from the
conspiracy of silence. And noting what has been said by Mr. le Roux, and
even by Mr. Theron, with regard t<> the working of the Glen Grey Act the
Christian Express observes : —We do not expect that the Royal Sanc­tion
will be withheld from this Bill, though we should rejoice if it were.
There is not much moral earnestness in the Horne Ministry just now, any
more than in our own, and we do not. expect any stand to be made for
principle. The remedy for the evil which we believe has been done, and
done with great astute­ness and callousness, lies in the direction of
patient submission for a time to the injustice, and to steady but not
angry constitutional agitation. The forces that make for righteousness,
that is for pro­gress, are steadily triumphing, though they are slow,
because of opposition, and the regime of principle, even in politics,
will return to bless the land. Meanwhile we hope the Natives will be
able to endure hardness as good soldiers and we believe they will. If
men are earnest in the cry for political and administrative reform they
will steadily keep in view the amendment of the Glen Grey Act in the
important par­ticulars which were carried against argu­ment by brute
force in the recent session of Parliament. As a manifesto and instrument
of Native Policy the Act should he made to declare the absolute right of
the Native people to the lands conveyed to them in community of
occupation. The so-called quitrent title is in fact but an individual
appropriation under a communal system, the franchise which would attach
to a true, quitrent tenure being expressly barred. The Act should be
made to carry on the face of it the recognition of the tenure as a
racial one strictly limited in any future transfer between individuals
to the people whose rights it ostensibly protects as well as defines. A
year’s trial will indicate where other amendments are needed not only to
bring the Act into conformity with principles of justice, but to render
it practicable. The Christian Express wisely counsels a course of loyal
submission combined with an agitation on constitutional lines. It should
not be possible when next the matter comes under review in Parliament to
appeal to the silence of the people concerned in evidence of their
contented acquiescence in the legislation. Only the petition for redress
should be confined to those points in which a principle is either
suppressed or violated. The Act is a good one in its general scope ; it
needs amendment only where the details are in conflict with its object
as set forth by the Premier him­self.
