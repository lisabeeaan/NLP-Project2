PROSPERITY AND WAGES.

  ONE of the things that strike one forcibly at Port Elizabeth

is the growth of the place, to be seen in the enlargement and
beautifica­tion of business houses; but strange to say this has been
signalised by a corresponding fall in the wages of Native labourers.
Wages at “the Bay ” have not, for some time past, been what they were.
By degrees the rate is receeding from what has got to be known as a
“living wage”: and we confess we do not think that the country has any
reason to congratulate itself on this condition of things. No one would
begrudge merchants all the prosperity they can attain to ; but it seems
to us that it is more on the prosperity of the wage-earning class who
form the real community that the success and prosperity of a country
rests ; and we find little to rejoice in when we see our merchants
breaking up their barns to build larger and more commodious stores,
while the bulk of the community appears to be none the better for it
all. We must enter an earnest protest against the cheese-paring policy
of our merchant princes in forever taking advantage of the Native
labouring classes to reduce wages in order to swell yet more the bloated
incomes of capitalist employers. The disposition to squeeze the labourer
to appease the guinea-pig has shown itself in nothing more vividly than
in recent speeches by Mr. RHODES who, of all people, we should have
thought had the least reasons to growl at the Native labourer. That
gentleman has lost no opportunity of late to state his belief that this
country would be infinitely better if its labouring classes earned 2d.
per day, while the bulk of what they now received went into the pockets
of the capitalist who seldom settles in this country. That this would
tend to impoverish and cripple trade is clear; and the wonder is that
people should have gone so far in worshipping money-bags from which they
derive nothing, as to counte­nance a doctrine that would in its effect
be the curse and blight of the land. Let the prosperity of the merchant
go pari passu with that of the labourer and the artisan and then, but
not until then, we shall see comparative contentment prevail.

Current Opinion.

If the Imperial Government does not exert itself to deliver our people
in the Cape Colony from the injustice which the operation of the Glen
Grey Act will bring on them, it will certainly not be because no steps
were taken to point out to them the gross injustice of that Act.
Notwith­standing advice to the contrary, the Natives have petitioned—and
rightly pe­titioned—the Home Government to pro­tect their rights, and in
this they have been joined by the Aborigines’ Protection Society whose
sense of justice will not allow it to sit still and witness such a high
handed piece of injustice on the part of Mr. Rhodes, We can but hope
that the Home Government will do what is expected of it, and advise the
Queen to disallow an act which an overbearing Prime Minister has forced
a weak Ministry to accept against their better judgment. But should it
be found that the Home Government is without that moral earnestness
which is so necessary in a matter of this kind, our people will have to
submit patiently to the injustice until, by means of a steady,
persevering, constitutional agitation, they can induce their Government
to remedy the evil,
