THERE seems to be a general impression that the Cape Government mean to
take up the matter of the relations of the Colony with the Pondos, and
deal, with them; and, as a consequence, we hear of war and rumours of
war. It has been well observed that it takes two to make a quarrel; and
what wo know of the past of the gentlemen who comprise the present
Cabinet, no consideration, save those of expense, would prevent them
from going into war with the Natives. But, however reckless tho present
Government may be in matters of this nature, it behoves the people of
this country to inquire carefully whether the Natives, on their side,
arc desirous of war. We make bold to say that the Vendas have no desire
for war. They arc too feeble altogether to with to engage in a war with
this Colony ; and this fact alone should ho suffi­cient to prevent any
chivalrous

Government from doing a thing so scandalous as to embark upon war­like
operations against a people so feeble as the Pondos. We write thus,
premising that there is cause to go to war with the Pondos, of which, at
present, we have not heard even a whisper. Such being the case, then, we
can scarcely believe that his Excellency the High Commissioner would
give his sanction (Pondoland existing under sacred Treaties with Her
Majesty’s Government) to the commencement of hostilities without good
and intelligible grounds. As has been observed, these at present are
lacking, and, notwithstanding all we hear about a war with the Pondos,
it is hardly credible that Sir HENRY LOCH, in the name of the great
Empire he represents, can permit the infliction of so gross an injustice
as the slaughtering of the Pondos without rhyme or reason. We shall no
doubt be told that the Pondos are slaughtering each other under their
own chiefs; but two blacks do not make one white. And the slaughter
going on is not the thing that should be brought forward to justify
another and a greater slaughter by means of Maxim Guns. Nobody can be
more desirous of putting down anarchy than ourselves; but let it be
done, and it can be done without blood­shed—by civil, not by military
ways of settling difficulties; and we earnestly protest against the
heart­less and cruel policy which, accord­ing to all accounts, the
RHODES- SPRIGG Government seems bent upon. It is such proceedings which
undermine the confidence of a sub­ject and weak race in their rulers,
and makes it despair. The very weakness of the Natives should save them
from being tyrannized by a strong power.

THE Cape Register of Saturday I week has the following ap­preciative
paragraph of Miss STUR­ROCK and Miss E. STURROCK lately of Peelton:—“
This week’s mail “ steamer took from our shores two “ ladies who will be
much missed “ in the mission field, Miss STUR- “ ROCK and Miss E.
STURROCK of “ Shaftesbury Home. The Home “ is closed, and the work for
the present at an end. Every one “ connected with the London Mis- “
sionary Society and the Education “ Department should go into “ ‘
retreat ’ for a month.” Only Natives, wo believe, can fully and truly
estimate the work these ladies have done for Africa—and a grand work it
is. We sincerely regretted, as wo shall ever regret, the unto­ward fate
which betook their glorious, because successful, work of ameliorating
the lot of the daughters of our land ; for never have Mis­sionary
agencies succeeded in their work as the Misses STURROCK have done in
making a permanent im­pression on Native home life. There exists no more
powerful a lever in raising a people than the improvement of its women;
and it was the good fortune of Miss STURROCK and her niece at Peelton.
to accomplish this in an emminent degree, as the lives and homes of many
a girl trained by them testify. What makes our regret because of their
departure more poignant is the knowledge that there was still as much
useful work in them as ever there was when Miss STUR­ROCK came out early
in the Sixties. And now all we can way is, that they are followed into
retirement by the warm hearts of a grateful community who fully
appreciate their work and worth; and it is to be hoped they will cherish
these feelings of our people, treasuring them up, in the coming times,
as a priceless gift which a people just reached by tho light can give.
