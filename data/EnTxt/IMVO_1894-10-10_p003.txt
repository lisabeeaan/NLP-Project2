Topics of the Day.

Fingoland Local Government.

There is no opportunity this week to comment on the Government
Proclama­tion, said to be based on the Glen Grey Act, but which seems to
be a totally different affair from that instrument. The Proclamation
does not even mention the Glen Grey Act, thus showing the Government
have themselves already seen that it is a very faulty enactment; and in
our opinion the Transkei law is certainly a more liberal measure,

The Liquor Question-

Space forbids us from canvassing the important speeches of Mr.
Rose-lunes, Q.U., Bishops Key, and Gibson and of Mr. W. Hay at the
meeting of the Church Temperance Society at Capetown last week on the
momentous subject of the Natives and Liquor as raised by the Glen Grey
Act. Mr. Rose-Innes, needless to say, unreservedly condemned the clauses
of that law, bearing on this matter, and so did the other speakers,
while all joined in welcoming the restrictions so wisely proclaimed by
the Government the other day affecting Native territories.

We join Mr. Hollands’ friends in con­gratulating him on his promotion to
the Registrarship of Deeds in Capetown, a position vacated by Mr.
Hercules Tenant who assumes Mr. J. C. B. Serrurier’s post as Registrar
of the Supreme Court. It has not transpired who will succeed Mr. Holland
as C. C. of Kingstown ; but if promotion went by merit, Government could
not do better and give genuine satisfaction to people in these parts of
all classes than give the position to Mr. R. J. Dick the Special
Magistrate, A more worthy chief of the largest Native district
Government cannot find anywhere.

The Swazi Deputation to England.

The Swazi nation has lost no time in showing in a practical way that
they are in earnest over the right settlement of their land, by
despatching without delay the Deputation which Her Majesty’s Government
haye signified their consent to meet. The Deputation arrived at East
London by the “ Grantully Castle” on Sunday last, and proceeded by rail
to Capetown on the same evening. It con­sists of five indunas, viz.,
Nongqanga, Zibokwana, Mhlonitshwa, Mabovu, and Mnkonkoni; and of an
educated Native Mr. Cleopas Kunene. They are accompanied by Mr. G. H.
Hullett legal adviser ; Mr. James Stuart as interpreter. We wish them
Godspeed in their work.

The Consecration of Canon Gibson-

It will be a supremely interesting item of news to the many friends of
Rev. Canon A. G, S. Gibson in the Transkei to learn that his
consecration as Coadjutor Bishop of Capetown came off with much eclat on
Saturday. 29th September, in S. George’s Cathedral, in the presence of a
large and representative congregation. The Bishop of Natal (Dr. Hamilton
Baynes), one of the ablest and most spirited prelates, preached a
soul-stirring sermon from John I, 51—“ Ye shall see the heaven opened,
and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of man.” It is
noteworthy that the Bishop of Natal and the new Bishop were at College
together, and had shared in company the joys and disappointments of
University life,

From the Lovedale News (a bright little leaflet of Institution
intelligence), just to hand, we gather that Dr. Stewart is stead­ily,
but slowly, improving in health, and is expected from Scotland by the
end of the Session or the end of the year. Con­siderable additions to
the teaching staff have been made, no less than three ap­pointments
having been confirmed. Mr. W. G. Bennie has left the Institution to
assume more responsible duties as Assist­ant Inspector of Schools in
Tembuland and neighbouring divisions. In the changes in the staff we
note that in the making of the appointments Natives are conspicuous by
their absence — unless Messrs. Sinclair (from Zonnebloem) and Weakley
(from Colesberg), whom we do not know, are Natives; although we were
assured recently by our respected friend, the Christian Express, that
the Institution does show faith in its own work by appointing Natives to
positions on the staff.

Hear, hear! Hear, hear!!

Commenting on Mr. Theron’s im­portant asseveration at Richmond, that by
the Glen Grey Act the tenure of their land by the Natives was as “
inse­cure now as it had been secure before,” the Eastern Province Herald
remarks, with truth and force: “ Mr. Theron is, of course, entitled to
the opinion that this fact is an advantage to the country, but, if he
holds it, why did he not support the Bill on these grounds when it was
before the House. Mr. Theron must see that the avowal of such an
intention after the passing of a measure must sap public confidence in
members of Parliament. During the discussions in the House Mr. Rhodes
said many times that the Bill was not intended to weaken the Native
titles to their land, whereas Mr. Theron now avows that his support was
given because the Native title was weakened, and Mr. Theron was in
constant touch with Mr. Rhodes.” Just so.

Natal Native Education-

It is not without interest to know what they are doing in Native
education in the sister Colony of Natal. That things are still on a
small scale there is not sur­prising, as we believe it is not so long
ago that Government has been induced to assist in the education of the
Natives on practicable terms. They have one Ins­pector of Native
Education (Mr. R. Plant); and he has just issued his report from which
we gleun the following facts: — “Grants in aid have been made to 93
Native schools during the year. Of these one has ceased to work owing to
the dis­turbance on our southern border, and one through not being able
to secure the minimum of 15 required fora grant. The number of schools
at present receiving aid is 91, an increase of 18 on last year’s number.
Applications for grants in aid from 16 new schools are in hand, and have
yet to be dealt with. The system of the amount of aid given has been
altered from that of an annual amount (fixed) to a per capita grant on
the average quarter­ly attendance; and consequently the amounts given to
the different schools are considerably altered from those of last year.
The total amount expended as grants in aid during the year has been
£1,440 6s. 61,, as against £3,396 7s. 8J. for last year; and, taking the
average daily attendance of 3,913, gives grant in aid per child of about
£1 2s. 8d. The usual annual returns furnished by the grantees show the
total number of school children on the books at the end of the year to
be 5,061, with an average daily attendance of 3,913, as against 3,829
and 2,802 respectively for last year. Of the number attending school
this year 2,125 are boys and 2,939 girls. 3,491 children have been under
examination this year, and with a few exceptions have evidenced
attention to school duties. Taken as a whole, the school work has been
quite satisfactory, and the industrial part of it very much improved.”

We have always admiration for straight­forward honest writing by the
press of a country; it goes far in the long run to influence a community
intellectually fed on it for good. It is therefore with plea­sure that
we occasionally come across the welcome style of expressing just
opinions in that excellent newspaper The Midland News; and what our
contemporary has said on the late Mr. Saul Solomon will not only commend
itself to the admirers of that great Cape statesman but will command
respect even from those who may be unable to agree with The Midland
News. We reproduce with great satisfaction the observations of the organ
of the Midlands on the immortal states­man:—“The unveiling of the bust
of Saul Solomon in the vestibule of the Houses of Parliament, and its
attendant circumstances have been so commented upon already that it may
seem super­fluous for us to add an additional word; yet wo would not let
the occasion pass without our sincere tribute of respect and admiration
to the dead statesman, The fact that none of the Ministry took any
prominent part in the ceremony, or even uttered one word upon such a
memorable occasion, is a significant one, and one that has called forth
almost unanimous cen­sure from the Press, not often independ­ent enough.
It may be true that they dared not face even the marble present­ment of
that great man, that they were ashamed before the bust of the man whose
sheet anchor was Truth—but we doubt it. We do not think they have grace
enough to be ashamed. It is true that members of such a Ministry, from
the depths of their degradation, can have but little in common with the
generous and lofty soul of him who has joined the Immort­als; they who
have prostituted public life, and made the very name of politics stink
in the nostrils of honest and straight forward men, cannot be
reason­ably expected to eulegise the man. who, if he were alive, would
have attacked them with all his divine force. Still we fancy the real
reason for their conspicuous absence lay deeper than this, and was more
discreditable to them: they dared not speak, from fear of their masters
I 2V Bond governed ministry eulogise Saul Solomon !! That benighted
organisation would not endure it for a moment. Saul Solomon was the one
man who would have nailed his unsullied colours to the mast in the sight
of all men, and fought such a fight against Bond principles as only
those fight who have the strength, the ability, and clearness of mental
vision to stand firm amidst a perishing world. The Servile Servants of
the Bond, licking the sole of every veldschoen, dared not praise even
the dead statesman ; the noble severity and integrity of the soul of
that giant looking out from the presentment of the dead features, would,
if they had any sense o shame, have paralysed their tongues and the Bond
would have been angered with its myrmidons.”

Current Opinion.

The following we cull from the account of the Richmond Era of a
political meet­ing when Mr. Theron, M.L.A., Mr. Hofmeyr’s great
lieutenant, and Chair­man of Committees of the House of Assembly
addressed his constituents:—

Mr. Chambers then asked him if there was any hope of an alteration in
the mas­ters and servants act, to which Mr. Theron said that it had
already been altered by the Glen Grey Bill. lie went over the Glen Bill
in detail as a great effort to settle the Native question especially
emphasising the statement that the land tenure of the Glen Grey Kafirs
was as insecure now as it had been secure before.

The Cape Times contents itself with giving a resume of the arguments on
which the Natives’ case rests, as follows: The absurdity of legislating
in haste in order to prevent agitation is now be­ginning to appear. “
Mr. Rhodes.” says IMVO, “by his own choice has put the cart before the
horse; and people now find themselves in this position that they have to
discuss the Native measure of last session after it has been passed with
scant consideration—owing to the highway­man’s pistol device—by
Parliament.” Our contemporary declines to accept the counsel of one who
looks to remote as well as immediate consequences and sub­mit to the
inevitable. Constitutional agitation, it is contended, is the
legiti­mate course for securing to the Native people the protection to
which the Crown is pledged by solemn treaty engagements. In this matter,
says IMVO, the case of the Natives is the strongest that could be placed
before any country, [Here follows a quotation from IMVO.]

In a subsequent sub article our Metro­politan contemporary goes on to
say :—

The Governor may give assent to any measure included in the specified
classes if he is satisfied of its urgent necessity but only when it is
not repugnant to the law of England or inconsistent with any obligation
imposed on the Crown by treaty. In the face of this proviso IMVO finds
it hard to understand why Sir Wil­liams Cameron so readily gave assent
in Her Majesty’s name to a measure dis­tinctly inconsistent with “ the
terms of Her Majesty’s treaty with the Native tribes of this country by
introducing the destructive principle of the alienation of their lands
at the mere bidding of a Minister of the day.” We fear that on the
constitutional point IMVO is not right­ly advised; for whatever light
such an admission as fell from Sir James Sivewright in debate may throw
upon the intention of the Bond Caucus in forcing the Premier from his
conciliatory position, the advocates of the Bill will protest that there
is no word in it at variance with the treaties above mentioned. It seems
to be certain, however, that an agitation will be set on foot, while a
judicious delay would have given occasion for nothing worse than the
discussion which is so dear to the Native heart.
