A CRYING injustice has been perpetrated on the voters of Umgwali, in the
District of Stutterheim. The station has a con­siderable number of
voters, and yet it was not included among polling stations at the recent
election for the Legislative Council. This was discovered on Polling
Day. We cannot help thinking that those who had to do with the fixing of
the polling places were influenced by un­worthy motives. They must have
known that Umgwali being near Waterford, where Mr. Hughes resided for
many years, the Natives of that station who know him well, and to whom
they are indebted for the existence of Umgwali as it is, would vote for
him. So it was not a matter of concern whether it was left out. This is
not fair to Mr. Hughes, who, we trust may yet win, notwithstanding such
subterfuges.

MUCH interest has been shown in the Election returns during the last
week; and the difficulty to ascertain the results gave piquancy to that
interest. Absolutely nothing is known of what took place at polling
stations as near Kingwilliams­town as Izele, Mount Coke, Ntsikizini and
Burn’s Hill. This being the case here the obstacles in the way of
getting information from up country field- cornetcies seem obvious. In
their devo­tion to red-tapeism some polling officers wont even say what
the totals are at their stations. The various Civil Commission­ers do
not appear to be one whit the wiser than the outside public, since the
instruc­tions to polling officers are that they should after the closing
of the poll seal their papers and forward them forth­with to the
scrutineers at Cape town. This is but a piece of absurd centralization
of duties in the metropolis which the people of these parts should
resent.

Native Opinion,

UNLESS the Tembu demonstration at  Umtata on Wednesday, 6th instant, was directed at expressing admiration of Major Elliot’s personal qualities, and confidence in the manner in which he uniformly carried out the wishes of the Government, it is difficult to conceive its purport. We fail to see the real grievance of the Tembus in the inauguration of the Circuit Court in the Territories. For long ere now the Judge has adjudicated on criminal cases from the Native Territories, the only difference being that they had to be brought down all the way to the Kingwilliamstown Circuit, whereas now he is to try them on the spot. Again, most people will not be able to discern the hardship under which the Tembus profess to be groaning in the mere transference of the judicial work of the State from an officer, like the Chief Magistrate, who is not necessarily a gentleman learned in the Law, to the Judges of the Supreme Court of the Colony.

It seems to us rather late in the day for Chief Dalindyebo to cite the terms of treaties entered into between his late father, the Chief Ngangelizwe, and the Cape Government Great and, in some cases, almost irrevocable changes, have taken place in the relations of the Native Tribes since those treaties were agreed upon. And it is vain for the grandson of Ngubenchuka to contend, as he did at the Umtata demonstration, that the Tembus came under Government under the expressed stipulation that they were to remain an independent tribe, with a Resident Agent only as the semblance of Government authority,—when the whole territory is now ruled through representative institutions under which magistrates are agents merely to administer the laws agreed upon by the people themselves, through their representatives in Parliament. It seems to us that the Tembu demonstration would have been in time before the annexation, when the Government of Sir Thomas Scanlen wisely determined to lop off the Territories and hand them over to the Imperial Government for administrative purposes. Not that they would have had no Judge under the Imperial Government, for the Judge is indispensable in the proper and satisfactory administration of the Law, but they would, at least, have been kept away from a form of Government from which rapid, and sometimes violent changes are inseparable, and under which such communities as are to be found in the territories would ever feel in a condition of suspended animation. Under the Imperial Government, moreover, there would have been a possibility, which under Responsible Government there is not, of the treaties on which Dalindyebo bases his contention, being observed.
