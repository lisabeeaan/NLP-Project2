  LOCAL OPTION.

  E are pleased to find that
  the suggestion thrown out

in these columns for making the
Local Option measure of the Government effective in Native centres has
been well received by the Press, and other leading men. Our proposal, it
will be remembered was this: that inasmuch as by
far the enormous preponderance of Natives are not Parliamentary Voters,
Native Hut Tax-payers should, under a system of Local Option, be deemed
voters. The principle of the Bill introduced by the Government is to
render it impossible for a new canteen to be stablished unless
two-thirds of the

  voters in the Ward, in which it is sought to plant such canteen, sign
  a prayer in favour of it; and, in regard to already established
  houses, if the same number of voters put in a memorial against the
  continuance of the nuisance, the Licensing Court has no choice but to
  give effect to the prayer. Now, the working of Local Option in the
  form proposed by the Government may work untold mischief among large
  Native populations. We contend that it was primarily at the instance,
  and in behalf of the Natives that Mr. Justice MAASDORP’S Liquor
  Commission was
  appointed; and it is not denied that the Bill of the Government is the
  natural outcome of that inquiry. Before the Commission our people
  uttered no uncertain sound that they wished Government to save them
  from fire water, or as one has not inaptly called it,
  “hell-fire.” Hear what the spokesmen of thousands of Natives who came
  spontaneously into the town on hearing that the Commission, which
  showed a reluctance to face them, was in Kingwilliamstown,

We give the substance of what they said, as repeated by Mr. Justice
MAASDORF to about four or five hundred of our people such as could go
into the Court house. Said the Judge:

You already know this Commission has been sent by the Government to
enquire what the wishes of the people are with reference to the sale and
drinking of brandy and other intoxicating liquors, and whether there is
much drinking among the people, both black and white, and whether the
laws are good, or if there should be new laws. We understand these
teachers from the different schools have come to express the wishes of
the Christian people, and Menziwa, with his great councilor and two
other councilors, have come to tell us the wishes of Dushani’s tribe,
and this is what they have told us. They say there is a great deal of
brandy drinking going on among the people, causing much drunkenness,
fighting, and injury to them, as well as poverty, and that they are
losing their property through it. Then they say the beer-drinking is
very bad for the people; that these big dances do them a great deal of
harm, because when they meet together to drink beer they get drunk, and
that leads to fighting with kerries, and even with assegais, and also
gives rise to a great deal of immorality. They say because of that, they
and you all wish that Government should prohibit the sale of brandy to
the natives; that the natives should not be allowed to get brandy, and
that Government should also put a stop to these big beer-drinkings where
the people drink beer and get drunk. They also say the Intonjanes lead
to drunken, ness and mischief, and that your mission people say it does
a great deal of harm, and you would be very glad if it could be put a
stop to ; that if you had your own chiefs they would put a stop to it,
and that as Government is your chief you want the Government to stop it.
They say these are the wishes you have expressed, and that is why they
have come to tell them to the Commission; We have been told that not
only do the men drink, but the women too, and if that is hot stopped it
will really lead to the ruin of the coloured people. They have said you
wish all this, and the Comission wants to know whether what they have
said is true. We don’t wish you to say it is true without thinking, and
if there are those among you who don’t agree with them you must at once
say so, and that they are wrong, because we are here to find out the
wishes of all of you. What I wish to do is this : to ask those who agree
with what the chiefs and teachers have said to say '' Yes,” and the
others can afterwards say “ No.” Think well before you answer. Now,
those who agree with what the chiefs, councilors, and teachers have told
us will say " Yes.” [Apparently all the people said ''Yes'']

We now want to find out if there are any among you who don’t agree. You
need not be afraid to say what you think. Those who do not agree with
what I have told you has been said, will say ” No.” [There was no
response.]

You have told us your wish, and we will tell the Parliament and the
Government, and they will do what they think right. All that your chiefs
and teachers have told us has been written down, and will be published,
so that everybody can read it.

To all this the hundreds of thousands of natives in this Colony, who
were not present at this meet­ing with the Commissioners would have
responded, Amen ! Now, it is proposed that Parliament should meet the
wishes of the people for protection by granting them Local Option. Let
us give an instance of how it will work, unless some such amendment as
we have pro­posed is adopted. The number of voters in the fieldcornetcy
from which these people—Imidushane— came, is—it will scarcely be belived
—eight, of whom only three are Natives; the remaining two-thirds being
white canteen keepers and a trader. Need we enlarge upon the practical
effect of the Local Option Clauses, if passed in their present form,
among the thousands of our people in the Tamara area? The two-thirds of
canteen people here will have the power of multi­plying canteens to
their hearts’ content; and the last state of the Bushani people will be
worse than the first. This is but one instance, taken among many, of the
way in which the Government Bill will work in districts with large
Native populations. In the Peddie divi­sion where there are 15,000
natives, scarcely fifty are voters; and these are on private farms of
their own. Indeed in Native centres purely it would be found, as in the
Tamara section of King­williamstown, that there are hardly any voters at
all, as they were all wiped off by Sir GORDON SPRIGG’S Disfranchisement
Act. Now there are 350 voters in Peddie; and therefore our people will
be at mercy of the 300 Europeans as to whether there should, or should
not, be canteens among them; and we are as yet not satisfied that public
opinion among our white friends, especially of the type that is to be
found in Peddie, is sufficiently educated on this question to resist the
establishment of new canteens among the Natives. This state of things
can only be remedied in the manner we have proposed, coupled with the
proviso that liquor be consumed on the premises, so as to make it
impossible for it to be carried into places that have banished it.

  VETO.

  WE welcome the support
  of Grocott's Penny Mail for
  the proposal for “Native
  Local Option ” (this is the
  description our contemporary gives it.) The Local option

posed by the Government may suit European communities; but as we have
shown elsewhere it will do incalculable injury in Native centres. So we
must insist upon “ Native Local Option.” This is what Grocott says on
this subject :— “ ‘ IMVO’ is right in its statement that
only registered voters may take part in vetoing the proposal to
establish a canteen in their neighbourhood, or if they alone can take
part in closing a canteen that is
deemed unnecessary or mischievous, the power of the natives will be
little more than nil, as so few of them are registered voters. IMVO
suggests that for the purposes of the Liquor Law all payers of hut tax
should be deemed voters, and should have a voice in the regulation of
the drink traffic in their own neighbourhood. The suggestion is well
worthy of consideration''

  FROM the Frontier Guardian
  GLEN GREY, we gather some idea of the mission of Mr. Francis Graham,
  the Special Commissioner to Glen Grey. Although the terms of his
  commission are naturally kept as a secret,’’yet our contemporary says
  that “ Those capable of forming an opinion, however, surmise— from the
  questions put by him and the nature of the information he was
  eliciting and gathering—that it was for the purpose of endeavouring to
  ascertain whether it would not be possible to huddle the large number
  of Natives now residing in that already overcrowded locality into a
  smaller area, and make the land from which they will thereby be
  cruelly and illegally driven available for European occupation.”

THE Guardian expresses PUBLIC FEELING what we have had oppor- ON
tunities to know is the GLEN GREY, feeling of the people on the Frontier
on the subject. It says:—We sincerely hope that all honest, fair-minded,
and fight-thinking men, of all shades of politics, will protest against
such barefaced rascality and rob­bery, which would simply moan breaking
faith with the rightful occupants of the ground in question, who are an
indus­trious, peaceable, progressive, law-abid­ing class of people, and
whose progress and advancement, more especially of late years and in the
face of almost insur­mountable obstacles, have been truly remarkable.
The recent Census Returns show that the Glen Grey District contains a
Native population of 39,845 souls, who are possessed of 83,4131 cattle
and horse, 262,211 sheep arid goats. Last year these Natives harvested
130,116 muids of mealies and Kafir corn, ” 44,335 muids of other grain
(wheat, barley etc.), and 255,002 bundles of oathay,' whilst their
flocks produced 801,944 lbs of wool and mohair. Facts and figures are
stubborn things to combat. We are con­fident that Mr. Graham will
furnish the Government with an accurate, fair, and impartial report,
made after careful examination and inquiry, and the Natives may rest
assured that any frau­dulent attempt to dispossess them of their just
rights will be resented and fought against by a large and influential
section of European Colonists, who pride them­selves upon being lovers
of fair play, irrespective of class or colour.

suggestion, that under a FEARS. system of Local Option the list of

register, is the fear that “ the first man coming would get the vote

election when they spurned a member of the Govern­ment who dangled his

THE E. F. Herald points A STARTLING out what many, who were FACT. not
aware of it before, will regard as a startling fact, that:—“ The Cape
Colonist pays 12s 6d upon his hundred pounds of coffee, and the Natalian
pays only 4s 2d. and the Cape man pays this extra tax year by year at
the command of the Bond, in order that brandy should go untaxed. No one
can gainsay the fact, and we repeat that this shows the wonderful
influence of the Western Bondsman, for he has made the Eastern farmers
follow him. He has made the Eastern farmer tax himself heavily all round
to keep brandy free, and the East­ern farmer knows, in addition, that
cheap brandy is the ruin of his servants and the cause of nearly all the
stock thefts. It will not last, of course, but the wonder is that it has
lasted for a day.” The fiscal system which admits of this anomaly must
be rotten to the core; but we are under the impression that under the
Customs Union Convention the Cape cannot impose an Excise without the
consent of the Free State, which in all probability would not be
granted.
