Native Opinion

  GOOD deal has been written by newspaper correspondents

  and paragraphists of Frontier journals about the discovery and
  recovery of farmers’ lost stock on Mgwali Mission Station : and we
  confess to having taken part in lecturing the residents of Mission
  Stations generally, and the Mgwali Mission in particular, on the
  dis­grace brought on the fair fame of Missions by such a revelation.
  We were careful, however, to point out in connection with the Mgwali
  in­cident the fact, creditable alike to Missionary work and to the
  inhabit­ants of these Stations, that it was a solitary exception, and
  serves to bring out still more prominently the absolute freedom from
  crime of the areas that have been under the in­fluence of Christian
  Missionaries. Since we wrote in this strain letters have been received
  from the Mgwali people, shedding a lurid light on the whole affair.
  The ac­counts that have appeared in the Press set in the foreground
  the sa­gacity and vigilance of the Cape Police in tracing these thefts
  into the very unlikely quarter, to wit, the Mission Station ; also the
  glar­ing hypocrisy of Mission Natives which is evident in
  psalm-singing on Sundays and pulling long faces, while harbouring
  farmers’ stolen stock. The facts, AS disclosed by our Native
  correspondents, who truly felt the severity of the strictures applied
  to them, are, that it was primarily the action of the Village
  Management Board (composed wholly of Natives) that the thieves were
  brought to book. They called the police and the farmers into the
  Station to identify the sheep in pos­session of the thieves, who, we
  are glad to know, are now paying the just penalty of their deserts in
  pri­son. Now, this is very creditable to our people ; and goes far to
  prove that, as a community, they are as much against thieves as any
  farmer can be. Indeed, their own laws against this species of crime
  are known to have been far more severe than those now in vogue under
  the existing regime. It is only fair to .our people at Mgwali to state
  that they, as a people, have done what lay in their power to expose
  wrong­doing ; and the least they can ex­pect from the general public
  is to get their due It is to the credit of the Mission, moreover, that
  the people to whose door the crime has been laid are, not only not
  converts to Christianity, but interlopers on the Station, the fact of
  their not having titles to land on the place establishing this aspect
  of the case beyond the shadow of a doubt. Such then being the real
  facts of the case, we trust our contempor­aries who have cast
  undeserved dis­credit on this Station on preju­diced and one-sided
  information will have the courage and honesty to withdraw these
  aspersions; and allow that Natives entrusted with some responsibility
  to keep up their good name are as careful as any other people
  similarly trusted and freed from a system of espionage practised under
  the crude and un­satisfactory Location legislation.

  FRANCHISE REFORM.

  EV. D. P FAURE, of Cape-
  town, is well-known as a

  fearless independent advocate of
  right and justice to all, irrespective
  of race, colour, or creed. Conse-
  quently he has been described as a
  negrophilist by certain shallow-
  thinkers whose only fixed principle
  is hatred of the wearers of the sable
  hue. It would not surprise us to
  hear his very sensible and practical
  letter to the Cape Times on “ How
  to Reform the Franchise,” set down
  to his incurable negrophilistic pro-
  pensities and all uncharitableness
  towards the Afrikanders who regard
  him as a renegade and a traitor to
  their cause. Mr. FAURE’S letter to
  which we have referred, opens with
  a statement showing the singular
  absence of foundation for the alle-
  gation that the number of Native
  voters is alarmingly increasing, and
  threatens to completely swamp the
  European vote. Mr. FAURE takes
  up the cudgels, as usual, on behalf
  of the weak in this “ wolf and the
  lamb ” business, observing that—

  Those who during the last session of
  Parliament advocated the amendment of
  the provisions of the Constitution Or-
  dinance with regard to the qualification
  of voters based their arguments in favour
  of the change on the alleged fact that
  the number of native voters is alarmingly-
  increasing, and threatens completely to
  swamp the European vote in several
  districts of the Colony. The electoral
  divisions supposed to be exposed to this
  peril are, of course, only the Frontier and
  Transkeian districts. Now, even if it is
  conceded that in those parts the natives
  are, or will be, masters of situation, it is
  difficult to see how this can have any

practical effect on the legislation of the
future. The natives would then be able
to send ten “ negrophilists ” to the House
of Assembly, and so long as ten men
cannot outvote sixty-four this much-
dreaded danger of having a ‘‘philan-
thropical ” Parliament seems indeed very
remote. If we further consider that the
districts in question have hitherto not
been represented by “Kafir friends”
exclusively, but that even at present such
men as Messrs. Sivewright, M. du Plessis,
Van Rensburg, and Schermbrucker are re-
turned by those constituencies, the
danger of Parliament being overrun with
negrophilists becomes more chimerical
still. The ideal Parliament, in my
humble opinion, is the one in which all
sections of the community are fairly
represented, and surely it will not be
conteded that the hundreds of thousands
of our natives are entitled to no repre-
sentation whatever. If they can return
ten men, that privilege need not be
begrudged them, nor will the Heavens
fall if this privilege is conceded to them.

Next Mr. FAURE states the case of
the present Native voter, and
proves conclusively why he should
not be disfranchised. He writes :

Those who assert that the right to vote
is an inherent and inalienable right of
every citizen take up an indefensible posi-
tion. The franchise is a privilege to be
earned. Yet it is one thing to assert and
to carry out this sound theory, but it is a
very different thing to deprive men of the
power to vote, when they have become
entitled to it by prescription as it were.
And this is what is proposed to be done.
The natives have had their £25 franchise
for forty years; by common consent they
have never abused that franchise; they
have never sent men to represent them
who have disgraced the Parliament or the
country, and therefore, if they are now
deprived of that privilege, they will
naturally regard it as an injustice, and
resent it as such. And so would every
white man who is thus dealt with.

As might be inferred from the fore-
going, like many people, Mr.
FAURE does not see any necessity
for a change. All are agreed that
it would not be easy to defend the
claims of a barbarian to a vote : but

no barbarians are on the register on
the franchise as it now stands. If,
however, wealth is to decide the
matter, we fear there is many a de-
serving Native, who has emerged
from barbarism, who will be left in
the cold, while ''the reds'' will be
in a position to claim by mere wealth.
Unless the Franchise is to be kept
always above the reach of our
people, the present basis is high
enough to admit a small percentage
of the Natives, not enough even to
swamp the Europeans in any
division. For example, the essen-
tially Native district of Peddie can
only muster about fifty Native
voters under the present Franchise.
Mr. FAURE, desiring to shut the
mouths of negrophobists as well,
argues :—

Still it is neither advisable nor wise to
give barbarians the privilege to vote. But
the native voters now registered, having
enjoyed this privilege so long, and having
done nothing to justify their being now
suddenly struck oft the list, it seems to
me that, if the amendment of the Consti-
tution Ordinance on this point is to be
taken in hand at al), such amendment
should not interfere with the rights or
privileges of either white or black voters
now registered as such. Let the £25
franchise be altered into a £50 or a £100
franchise; better still, into an educational
franchise, but at the same time let it be
provided that this new qualification shall
be required only in those who after a
certain future date shall claim to be
registered, and that those who hitherrto
have exercised electoral privileges under
the old qualification shall be allowed to
continue to do so during their lifetime.

To such an arrangement both negro-
philists and anti-negrophilists could
agree. Much dissatisfaction and agitation,
both among the humbler class of whites
and the natives, would be avoided ; the
preponderance of the barbaric vote would
be made impossible as effectually, and by
a less round-about and by a more straight-
forward way, than by the neutralisation
of the native vote by giving an additional
vote to the wealthier and better-educated
classes—which is the proposal now before
the country. The new election law will
then have no retrospective effect, and no
one will have any reason to complain.

It remains to be seen whether the
HOFMEYRITES will accept this com-
promise ; for our part, well aware
of their ulterior aims, we strongly
suspect that they will reject these
overtures with scorn.

LINGUISTIC.

LETTER TO THE EDITOR.

SIR,—I have read with interest the various correspondence that has
appeared in your valuable paper regarding Mr. I. Wauchope’s lecture on
the Kafir Langu­age. It strikes me that to some poor minors, to which
publicity has been given through your paper, Mr. Wauchope has thrown a
gauntlet inside the public arena that has excited, as it were,
unscrupulous conflicts in Kafir Literature.

Nothing, sir, could more clearly define the absence of principle and
careful scrutiny on the part of your correspond­ents who took upon
themselves the duty of criticising Mr. Wauchope’s lecture, than the way
in which some of their arguments and contentions are set forth. They
show much discrepancies in them­selves, and are not worth the papers on
which they were written when first communicated to you. The only best
way for them is to be carefully stored away in some old man’s
tobacco-pouch for future fumigation when they will be let fly up to the
aerial regions and claim no more habitation in the human mind. I do not
care to argue “in extenso” the points on which I make this statement for
I would be truly trespassing on your space when something more valuable
would have been inserted. Some of the statements made by these critics
are most misleading both in history and in facts, and show more
attendency to exhibiting farcical observations than anything else.

It is to be regretted that Mr. Wauchope has been tempted to make any
replies to his critics. Had he withheld his hand from penning these
replies, he would have both saved his time and kept his dignity as a
student, and the two young striplings who have most indifferently
approached him with such insinuations as were evidenced in their
communica­tions would have been brought to sub­jection and shame. Fresh
from the Training Institutions and, no doubt, stuffed with vain
knowledge of various (?) branches of study, our two young friends
attempted to scale the stupendous mass of notoriety that they forgot
their man­ners, their position and their strength in publishing their
opinions on a subject of so great importance. Men of more knowledge,
more experience, more refinement and more taste than them­selves, found
no fault (for if they did, saw no occasion for making known their
finding) in Mr. Wauchope’s lectures.

Mr. Editor, I drop you these few lines because I cannot tolerate the
action of your two young correspondents towards an honourable and
widely-known per­sonage as Mr. Wauchope. The fact that they are juniors
ought to have hindered them from making such uncalled for criticisms on
their senior. If this kind of thing be allowed to pass unnoticed it
might lead to a political scandal and utter embarassment to the cause of
native education. I write this from the bottom of my heart. And if our
young cannot learn better in the way of respecting those above them,
there is no knowing to where this may lead. Freedom of speech and
expression of opinion are quite admissible to the public, but whether
speech, or opinion or criticisms must be exact and to the point when
brought through the channels of the Press. The liberty of the Press
should not be ample scope for ambitions youths to publish their puerile
thoughts. If my two young friends had studied Trench on the Study of
Words or Peille's Philology, or Morris’s Grammar, or Max Muller on
languages, they would not have venture on Mr. Wauchope’s lecture in the
way they did. The above works although having nothing to do with South
African languages would have enabled them to see the fundamental rules
of the formation the growth and the existence of languages. There is
nothing, as yet, brought forth by our young critics to disprove what Mr.
Wauchope wrote.

Being a Zulu I happen to know of a certain word isigqila among the
disputed words. The lecturer is quite correct to to say there is no
translation of ‘ slave ’ even in the Zulu. We use isigqili for slave
because it is the only word with an approximate meaning to slave.
Literally isigqila means a ‘ handmaid,’ it might also means a ‘maid
servant generally.’ It was customary for the Zulu royal­ladies, or some
ladies of high-rank to take with them as part of their parapher­nalia a
girl or girls to do work for them in the domestic duties of their new
homes. It was disgraceful for a lady of rank to touch any kind of work.
Hence such ladies were always well equipped with girls of this sort, who
were called izigqili or ogqilazana. The word was never applied to males,
and there is no male isigqila. The corresponding word for males would be
isikweqe or isikwengqe. The absence of the custom, itself shows that
there could have been no possibility of having a word for slave. Excuse
me Mr. Editor for taking up so much of your space.

Yours &c.,

P.S.—I do not wonder that one of the critics Is the same person who at
one time made some wild and fanciful calcu­lations of the number of
words in the Xosa language. Good job that the matter collapsed and
received no public estimation.
