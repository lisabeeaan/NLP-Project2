return in a signal manner—by the most demonstrative expression of
respect and confidence. Mr. PEACOCK is generally known as a cultured
gentleman of fair, enlight­ened and satisfactory views towards all
sections of the communities in­habiting the Eastern districts. Above
all, judged by his past Parliamentary career when he sat in the Assembly
for Kingwilliamstown, he possesses the requisite ability and courage to
give ex­pression to those views. For these and other reasons it is no
surprise to us that Mr. PEACOCK’S candi­dature is being hailed with
satis­faction from all quarters and by various sections of electors; and
certainly no candidate could come with a stronger claim for support.
Referring to it the Frontier Guar­dian (Dordrecht) says the
announce­ment of Mr. PEACOCK’S standing is good news, and adds “ A
better “ selection could not have been made, and nothing must be left
undone in order to secure his

“return.” And so say all of us.

A BILL has been introduced into the American Senate to establish
Industrial Training Schools, and to provide lands for Negroes, to be
held under lease, with the privilege of subsequent purchase. It proposes
the sum of £200,000, or as much as may be necessary to be paid over to
the Freedman’s Board, the organisation of which is provided for by the
Bill, on warrants countersigned by the Com­missioner of Education, and
approved by the Secretary of the Interior and Secretary of the Treasury.
The motive is excellent; and if we could have but a few Bills, conceived
in this spirit, and introduced into the Cape Parliament to help the
Natives along the path of pro­gress, the whole aspect of the Native
population in this country would be changed for the better in no time.

A COMMITTEE of the Wesleyan Con­ference has been sitting in Queenstown a
week or two back, presided over by the President of the Conference, Rev.
S. Evans Rowe. It had to consider and formulate a scheme for the
training of young men for the Methodist Ministry in connection with the
South African Conference. The result is that Queens­town has been
selected as the most suitable place where arrangements might be made for
the Theological students of European extraction, while Lesseyton was
chosen for that of the Native students. Questions of property and
finance came under review, and also various changes in the Constitution
of Methodism in South Africa, such as the enlarging of the powers of
District Committees so as to form District Con­ferences, and the holding
of a General Conference every three years, upon which changes the
Committee were unanimous in their recommendations. : They report to the
Conference that is ' to sit next year at Maritzburg, Natal.

THE Governor and party start from Cape town for “the interior” on Monday
next, accompanied by the Premier, whose duties will, during his absence,
be dis­charged by the Hon. J. W. Sauer so far as the Premiership is
concerned. We understand that the Hon. J. Sivewright, who has been
heretofore Minister with­out Portfolio, will be sworn in Commis­sioner
of Crown Lands and Public Works, the Hon. Cecil Rhodes holding the
position of Prime Minister only, as was previously intended, the highest
legal opinion in the country being de­cidedly against the Speaker's
ruling that the position was untenable.

WITHOUT doubt, about the most sen­sational event of the week has been
the failure of the Cape of Good Hope-Bank, which stopped payment last
Saturday with tremendous liabilities. This Insti­tution had many
branches throughout South Africa, and many people who had kept their
earnings in it will suffer, more or less severely—some losing their all.
Great distress is said to be the result in Cape Town, which only a few
weeks ag® had the Union Bank—a local concern— falling about its ears
with its unlimited liability on the part of shareholders. Strenuous
efforts are, however, being made by moneyed companies interested, like
De Beer’s, and it is possible a modus vivendi may be discovered to ward
off the misery that would be caused by the in­stitution putting up
shutters. South Africa had long been free from such catastrophes, and
present inhabitants are acquiring a new experience.

SIR J. A. DE WET has not been able, owing to ill-health, long to hold
the post at Pretoria to which he was recently appointed. Consequently he
sent in his resignation, which His Excellency the High Commissioner has
not accepted, granting the British Resident a three months’ leave ol
absence instead. There were, at the time of Sir Jacobus’ appoint­ment,
not wanting those who could not understand his resignation of the
position of Secretary for Native Affairs in the late Ministry, for the
very reasons that have now led to his vacating his office, only to take
up the duties of the Pretoria post. He had then just returned from a
year’s leave of absence in Europe. If twelve months could not set him
up, it is not clear how three months are. It is to be regretted that the
facts are as they have been related; but the conviction cannot be
escaped that a successor will have to be found to Sir J. A. de Wet.

IT was quite in keeping with the sense of justice towards all which
dis­tinguishes the' present Attorney-General to try to move the Supreme
Court to declare the Natives, who say they have been kidnapped by the
Portugues near Zanzibar, free. A mail steamship, Rei de Portugal entered
the Docks in Cape Town last week having on board a number of black men
who were being taken from the country in question for disposition —
goodness knows where. Some of these men escaped in Table Bay and tried
to move the machinery of the international law against slavery. Mr.
Rose-Innes, as might have been expected, took the matter up throwing his
heart into it. But the court, fearing to raise international
animosities, saying it might be ordinary compulsory service under the
laws of Portugal, declined to give the order, although it would not
assist the officer of the steamer in getting back those who bad escaped.
The brightest thing about the incident is the alertness and energy of
the Attorney- General in securing the rights of people under the law;
and in this wo feel tempted to pray, may his shadow never grow less.

THE Hon. P. H. Faure, M.L.A., Secre­tary for Native Affairs, passed
through Kingwilliamstown en route to the Trans- keian Territories,
somewhat unexpectedly early yesterday morning, having arrived at noon on
the previous day. He travels with Mrs. Faure and Mr. S. J. Hofmeyr, his
Private Secretary. The honourable gentleman is travelling in fulfilment
of his promise in Parliament to make him­self personally acquainted with
those place under his charge in the Govern­ment of the country. Never
living been in these parts previously, he appears to be agreeably
surprised with the physique of the Frontier Natives, the progress made
in the direction of the use of Euro­pean clothing at School Stations,
and the extensive cultivation by Natives along the route. He goes on to
Butterworth, whence he will be accompanied by Mr. Liefeldt, on to
Umtata. Kokstad, via St. John’s estuary, to Matatiele, back by Maclear,
Cala and Queenstown by the Indwe Coal Mines. From Queenstown Mr. Faure
will come to Kingwilliamstown about the end of October to join his
col­league, the Attorney-General, Mr. J. Rose Innes, who will then be
visiting these parts. Mr. Faure appears to be an amiable gentleman, who
seems anxious to do the best he can to render those con­fided to his
care happy and contented; and we are sure our people will be favourably
impressed on meeting the new Secretary for Native Affairs.
