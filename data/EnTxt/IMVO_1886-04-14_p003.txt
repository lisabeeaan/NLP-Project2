NOTWITHSTANDING the reiterated promises of the Ministry to treat Natives
with consideration in the matter of the
forcible collection of arrears and current
taxes, we regret to observe that the useless seizing of stock and crops
is still
going on in the Frontier districts, the
Government officials on the spot not
having been instructed to desist.

THE press is pretty unanimous in condemning what is being done, on the
ground that the forcible seizure of crops
and stock is inopportune. This is what
Grocott's Mail (Graham’s Town) has to
say on the subject, and we venture to say
all sensible Colonists will concur in its
closing observations :—“ That neglect of
duty, if continued long enough, is prolific
in mischief, is receiving abundant illustration in Kafirland, and in
many parts
of the frontier. According to the King
William’s Town Chamber of Commerce,
Native occupants of land in that neighbourhood have been allowed to fall
into
large arrears of rent, those arrears, in
some cases, spreading over a period of
fourteen years. Now the Government
is coming down upon the delinquents
without mercy. Writs are being issued
by the hundred. On the Mission Station
of Kama no less than eighty of those
documents, alarming and ruinous to the
Natives, have been served. In one case
a cow sold in execution fetched only 8s
6d. Gardens have been sold for 5s or 6s.
At Peelton, property which was distrained for £148, realized at the sale
£11,
and when the expenses of the distraining
and sale had been paid for, 43s were the
sole residue for Government. The
Treasurer-General and Secretary for
Native Affairs must mind what they are
about, or they will utterly impoverish
and exasperate the Natives, without
bringing anything into the Colonial
Treasury.”

Native Opinion

The NATALIANS are sympathetic to the

circumstances or their Natives, and it is
perhaps from this fact that they find it so
comparatively easy to manage them.
This is what the Natal Mercury says on
the subject of “Native Hut Tax.” “ A
few days ago we expressed a fear that the
Government will experience some difficulty this year in collecting the
hut-tax,
and we indulged in a hope that due
regard would be paid to the special circumstances of the time. We know
as a
fact that in some districts—possibly in all
—the natives complain that they cannot
get work, that their wagons are unemployed, that cattle are unsaleable,
and
that mealies when harvested will only
command nominal prices. Poultry also
has failed amongst them. Should the
difficulty we have apprehended be
encountered it will probably be wise to
make use of the occasion as another
opportunity for explaining to the natives
the true principles and purposes of taxation. It will also be an
appropriate time
to advise them as to the means by which
they can best meet them, and other
changes that civilization and progress
have brought in their wake.” After
making copious extracts from what
appeared in these columns on the subject,
our contemporary concludes by urging
upon the Natal Government the wisdom
of dealing considerately with the people
in view of the present depression, and
does this in the terms of our article addressed to the Cape Government.

Speaking at a meeting, the Rev.
G. Kakaza (Heald Town) now appointed
here—a native minister of considerable
education made the following practical
remarks on the vexed question of the
amelioration of the Native races of this
country: “These races were indebted to
the European races which had colonised
South Africa. Some might think the
native people were not grateful, but he
could assure them of their gratitude.
They remembered that it was through the
Europeans that the Gospel had been
taught to them. The Gospel was the only
power that could change the character of
any man. It was all very well to legislate
against particular vices, but only the
Gospel could deal with the root all vice,
and change the hearts of men. They were
thankful too for the civilization which
the Europeans had brought to this country. They knew there was sonic
other
things which came with it for which they
were not grateful. They were not grateful for the “ liquid fire,” and he
was glad
to see that a petition was being presented
to the House of Assembly, praying for
deliverance from those who would force
the liquor upon their country. The
speaker proceeded to shew that the
natives were grateful for the education
which they had received by means of the
Europeans. It had been said that education spoiled the natives and made
them
conceited. He did not think that this was
the fault of education. No doubt a little
education helped to make men vain, but
those who were properly educated would
not be conceited. If education did not
make Sir Isaac Newton vain, he did not
think that it was to education that the
conceit of people who talked of their learning was to be ascribed. The
more people
knew the more humble they would be
The true remedy for such "conceit wash
more, and not less, education.

OPENING OF PARLIAMENT.

IN the absence, through ill-health,
of his Excellency the Governor, the Parliament was, last Friday,

opened by the Administrator, Lieutenant-General TORRENS. He did
this in a speech put into his mouth
by the Ministry—a speech from
which the country are wont to look
for the programme of the session.
From the tone of this speech we
gather that, in view of the commercial depression, Ministers will
propose measures “to encourage
“ enterprise in agricultural and
“ pastoral pursuits,” and to develop the “ mineral wealth of the
“ country.” The representatives of

the people will also “be asked to
“ consider the advisability of giving
“ encouragement to the establish-
“ ment in the Colony of manufac-
“ tories of woollen cloth from wool
“ grown here.” Bills for the encouragement of the production of
cotton goods from Colonial-grown
cotton will also be submitted to the consideration of Parliament.

Government also propose to erect a
school for teaching wine farmers to

make wines for the European
market. The Government propose
to introduce a Bill to suppress the
waggon traffic near the Railway
lines. The question of the Representation of the Transkeian Territories
will again come forward, and
will probably be passed. The
Ministers promise, for the first
time, to take Parliament into their
confidence on Pondo affairs. The
speech concludes with a vague allusion to the matter of the protection
of grain and breadstuffs by a heavy
impost on the imported article.

Now, if this programme will be
seriously adhered to it means a
perfect transformation of the
Colony. For our part we shall be
surprised if, at the end of the
session, this heavy catalogue will be
anywhere near the point of realisation. To begin with Ministers have
not the capacity for the work, nor
have their supporters. Casting our
eyes on the benches in the House
we find that all the intelligence
and ability is ranged on one side—
the Opposition side—and the work
of the Opposition is easy and satisfactory. It is to criticise and pull
down, and nothing but what is of
real value survives this process. If
the Land measures, the Transkeian,
and the Bill to establish the portfolio of Minister of Agriculture,
succumbed to the criticism of the
Opposition last session, what guarantee is there to make us cherish a
hope that some of the things
now proposed will not meet with a
similar fate ? The aim of the Government may be perfectly unexceptional,
but what means will they
suggest for its attainment. There’s

the rub. It is in this that we think,
that they will break down. And
their fatuity in not disclosing their
plans some time before the meeting
of Parliament is all the more apparent, since by adopting the other
course they profit by the criticism
of the Press, of their friends, and
of public men. These measures not
having had the wholesome light of
