  JOHNSON'S best friends have ever

  public life generally. We refer, of

  Capetown; that is, if report is correct
  that he is standing as a candidate
  soliciting the suffrages of the elec-
  tors of Victoria East. Apart from
  the advantages to a constituency in
  having a representative resident at
  the seat of Government to watch
  and promote the interests of his
  constituents during the long weary
  months when Parliament is not
  sitting (and Victoria East has had
  a foretaste of this when the present
  Attorney-General sat for her in the
  Assembly), the electors would, in
  many other respects be most for-
  tunate in securing Mr. HAY as their
  member. Mr. HAY, a gentle-
  man who has received an educa-
  tion of a far-reaching kind, for
  many years occupied the chair
  of the Cape Mercury which he
  edited with conspicuous ability and
  conspicuous courage making it a
  real power in the land. He has
  thus been in the best possible
  position to thoroughly understand
  public questions; and these ex-
  periences, placed at the disposal of
  a constituency, would be of tre-
  mendous service. It has always
  appeared strange to us why some
  of the ablest men in the country
  who drive the quill in the columns
  of some of our leading contempor-
  aries seem disinclined to take to
  Parliamentary life, seeing that the
  very nature of their business, neces-
  sitating a careful, painstaking, and
  incessant examination of public
  questions, would make them better
  fitted for the career of politicians
  than many a man now pursuing it;
  and here, we frankly confess that we,
  for one, should have braved the ele-
  ments long ago, did we not groan
  under the sentimental, but palpable,
  deadweight of our colour being
  black. This, however, by the way.
  But. joking apart, it strikes us that
  the marked success as public men, of
  erstwhile journalists like Mr. Gon-
  LONTON, Mr. FAIRBAIRN, and Mr.
  BIDWELL, of the Uitenhage Times,
  in the remote past; and Mr. HOF-
  MEYR, Mr. SCHERMBRUCKER, and
  even Sir GORDON SPRIGG to-day,

  men connected with the Press to
  come forward as candidates for seats
  in Parliament. Then, legislation
  respecting matters affecting the
  craft would not be so much directed
  against the industry as has been
  the case these past two decades,
  when good paying advertisements
  have, by some singular fatality,
  been torn, as it were, from the very
  mouth of the newspaper man and
  swept into the capacious maw of the

  ment Gazette. Moreover, as a
  worthy Colonist, of good repute,

  East or any other constituency
  which appreciates real merit, the

  neither here nor there in the at-
  mosphere of the House where mem-
  bers have to do, not so much with

  returning him the Electors would,

  THAT NATIVE VOTE.

  UR good friend the Cape Times
  closes an article noticing the

Government proposals respecting
the reform of the franchise with a
warm tribute to our people for the
way they have always discharged
their public duties as electors;
and it looks forward with a degree

of anguish and concern to the prospect when the Native vote shall have
been swept from the Registers of the country. The grounds for this
feeling are set forth as follows :—

The aboriginal vote has returned some of the most useful members to the
House of Assembly ; the important constituency in which it is unknown
lias always been the worst represented in our legislature. It is, of
course, not because of the political sterility of the aboriginal Natives
at Kimberley that the Parliamentary repre­sentation of the place is so
miserably in­adequate ; the fact, nevertheless, suggests the possibility
of the introduction of worse evils by the disfranchisement of the the
Natives. Happily this event is post­poned by the provision for which Mr.
Sauer appears to have adopted a need­lessly apologetic tone. We only
trust that by the time the registers are cleared by death the demand for
constitutional reform may have gathered such strength that no government
will venture to delay the general overhauling of the machine, inevitable
one day in any case but forced upon us by the results of a piecemeal
operation.

One fact may be stated here to show that the sorrow of the Cape Times at
efforts to minimise the wholesome influence of the Native vote is not
groundless. Of the limited number of men returned to Parliament mainly
by the votes of the Natives since Responsible Government was introduced,
it is highly creditable to our people that nearly 90 per cent, of the
gentlemen they have been instrumental in electing have acquitted
themselves so well in the House as to be offered office in one or other
of the Minis­tries that have been formed. In the MOLTENO Government the
Hon. C. ABERCROMBIE SMITH, who held a seat in the House principally by
virtue of the votes of our people in Kingwilliamstown, was
Commis­sioner. The second Responsible Ministry numbered the Hon. JOHN
LAING (Commissioner) and the lion. WILLIAM AYLIFF (Native Affairs) who
had received the sup­port of the Fort Beaufort Na­tives at their
election. Sir T. SCANLEN’S Government included the Hon. J. W. SAUER
(Native Affairs), who, at certain junctures, has had to rely solely upon
the Her­schel Natives for his Aliwal North seat; the Hon. C. W. HUTTON
(Trea­surer), who also obtained the votes of the Fort Beaufort Natives^
But for the well-known perspicacity of the Victoria East Natives the
country would long have remained in ignorance of the latent abilities of
the Hon. J. ROSE-INNES, QC., Mr. RHODES’ Attorney-General ; and had not
the natives of Umzim- kulu and Mount Frere supported the Hon. J.
SIVEWRIGHT the Com­missioner’s office would not now be adorned by a
gentleman who has the reputation of thoroughly under­standing the
business of his Depart­ment. We have no space to enlarge upon members
elected by Natives who, although they have been shining lights in the
House, have, nevertheless from no fault of their own, not been advanced
to Cabinet rank. The reason for the Natives’ apparent keenness in these
matters is not far to seek. It is admirably given in another extract
from our brilliant contemporary quoted above occurring in a different
article, to the effect that:—

“ The Athenian citizen, though perhaps unable to read, had a far higher
intel­lectual standard than we can find an example of in any other mass
of men, ancient or modern. And this superiority was due to the part he
took in public affairs, in the dicastery and the ecclesia. . . . If
circumstances allow the amount of public duty assigned to the individual
to be considerable it makes him, apart from the services of the
schoolmaster or lecturer, an educated man.” Substituting Natives for
Athenians you have what accounts for the evident penetration of our
people in elections.

THE sum total of the NATAL work of the special GOVERNMENT, session of
the Natal Parliament, summoned to re-consider the Responsible
Govern­ment Bill, has been the adoption of the suggestion of the
Secretary of State that provision be made for an Upper Cham­ber ; the
setting apart of £10,000 per annum for Native purposes. One point on
which the Imperial Government in­sisted, the reserving of powers of
Supreme Chief to the Governor on legislation affecting Natives, that is
that the Gov­ernor should dispense with the Minis­terial advice in such
matters, has been rejected. To our mind this is the all important
condition, and on which we hope the Imperial Government will not budge
an inch. Since our people in Natal have no audience in the Parliament of
the country, it is but right that everything should be done to protect
them from harsh legislation de­vised for them by a body that is in no
way answerable to them.

sort would do East London great credit among natives far and near.
