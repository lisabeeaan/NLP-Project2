HENRY B. LOCH.

I ADDRESS YE RULUNELI.

  T is with deep concern
  that we observe the

  Natives earnestness with, which
  our Port Elizabeth con-
  temporary, the E. P. Herald, has
  taken up, during the past two or
  three weeks, the advocacy of com-
  pulsory labour for the Native com-
  munities of the Eastern Districts of
  the Colony and the Transkeian
  Territories. A fortnight ago we
  took over from a local contempor-
  ary, the Cape Mercury, a sensible
  article directed at opening the eyes
  of our Bay friend as regards the
  real facts of the subject, on which it
  seemed to be dogmatising in blissful
  ignorance. The effect on the
  Herald writer has been to
  send him to England to extol
  the working of vagrancy laws
  in force in the Mother Country,
  quite oblivious, for the nonce, of
  the disastrous effect of putting new
  wine in old bottles or, that laws
  fitted to the present state of Eng-
  lish civilisation might work mischief
  when applied to a people in the
  position of the Natives here. It is
  all very well for the Herald to enter
  the province of Utopia, and write
  sweet things about its faith in the
  stamina of the Natives to pass
  the fire and water it would in a day
  put the millions of Natives in South
  Africa through; but those who
  ever keep before them the inexor-
  able fact that all growth is gradual,
  and cannot be forced on by artifi-
  cial means such as those devised by
  the Herald, must feel a pang at
  witnessing the efforts of a leading
  organ of public, even if it be com-
  mercial, opinion in this land—efforts
  worthy of a better cause—devoted
  to advocating the impracticable.
  As in all matters of this kind, we
  are one with the Herald as to the

ends our contemporary has in view ; but we differ as to the means.
Rather we incline to the statesman­like view taken by the Cape Mercury,
a view we are sorry to see the Herald ridicules, that people can only be
taught to work by educating them to feel fresh desires and to have fresh
needs. We should have thought that this was unanswerable. The Herald,
how­ever, deems this putting the cart before the horse. If, it says, “
the Native is taught to pine for '' the luxuries of civilisation before
'' he is taught to earn the money to “ pay for them, he will either have
“ to go without or attain his object “ by illegitimate means.” Our
contemporary seems to see a differ­ence between teaching ‘‘ to pine” for
civilisation and teaching to work. We confess we do not see it. Any
teacher is bound to make full use of the “ pining chord,” otherwise all
work is drudgery. Even the Herald admits the soundness of our contention
when it goes on to re­mark that

AS iu polishing some old piece of rusty metal, one get first a bright
spot here and there, glistening out of the dark surface and seeming
brighter than it is by the very con- trast, so we gets here and there a
native whom education and civilisation has elevated and raised out of
his surrounding darkness. He serves as a star of hope, which gives
encouragement to the worker, which spurs him on to further effort and
enables him to possess his soul in patience, knowing that the time most
come when the bright spots extend and increase and the whole be made
bright.

Exactly so. In attaining this re­sult, however, no heroic remedies,
which are worse than the disease, such as those now suggested by our
contemporary, were resorted to. For ourselves, we are satisfied that
persistence and further develop­ment of the process that has pro­duced “
here and there a Native '' whom education and . civilisation “ has
elevated,” will most assuredly be the safest and most satisfactory in
the end. Meantime let the Herald not disturb its equanimity, for, as in
the past, so in the future, these Natives will go on providing, by what
labour their present social position calls for, for their daily
returning wants. For it is a libel upon the hundred thousands of Natives
who live in their various homes, practically without police supervision,
to say they all manage by stealing.

CAPTAIN KEITH-FALCONER, His Excel­lency’s Aide de Camp, has shared
honour with the Governor during the recent tour. It is not to be
wondered at that the uninitiated among the Natives were led by the
peculiar official dress of the Aide de Camp to suppose that the Captain
was the Governor about whom there was such ado; and a great many of our
people will needs be disabused before they transfer their veneration
from the Mili­tary Secretary to his chief.

FOR the second time within a short time the Government of Natal has
shown that it is not slow to recognise merit even in a Native. It was
our pleasing duty not long ago to chronicle the appoint­ment by that
Government of a Native medical man, Dr. J. Mavuma Nembula to the
District Surgeoncy of Msinga. Dr. Nembula must have acquitted himself
well, or we should not so soon after hear of the gazetting of a Native
young man —Cleopas Kunene—to a responsible posi­tion, as Resident
Magistrate’s clerk at Stanger. We sincerely pray Kunene may give a good
account of himself in this important position. But what a con­trast in
this respect between the position taken up by the Natal Government and
our own Government. In this Colony of the Cape of Good Hope, where
Natives are somewhat more advanced—education­ally—than in Natal, the
Government by the Colonists has simply closed its eyes against seeing
merit iff the black man. Hence the archives of the Cape Colony might be
ransacked in vain by anyone desiring to discover appointments of Natives
to really responsible positions made by the Cape Government. And this
not that there is no field where Native young men might be employed
usefully' among their countrymen; but it is because Government has not
seen its way to develop this agency. Natal has, however, led the way.
Thanks to the Crown Colony system for this mercy at least if for no
other. It proves that the principle of no distinction of colour or caste
is, under Her Majesty’s direct sway, a reality and not a sham.

IT was during the administration of Sir Thomas Scanlen we believe that
the Cape Colony was beginning to make no dis­tinction in respect of
colour in making appointments to responsible posts in the public service
of the country. Under that Ministry two very im­portant appointments of
Natives were gazetted; viz., that of Rev. Elijah Makiwane of Macfarlane,
Tyumie who was put on a Commission to supervise the survey of Mabandla’s
Location, his col­leagues being Mr. A. Stewart now Civil

Commissioner of Uitenhage, then Resident Magistrate of Victoria East,
and Captain Hughes, R.N., Government Sur­veyor; the other appointment is
that of Mr. William N. Seti of the Special Magistrate’s Court, King
Wil­liamstown, who was gazetted Pass Issuer, a post which carrys- no
extra remuneration we believe. The noise oc­casioned by this appointment
amongthose purists the Kei Road Association o Farmers is well-known.
Since the as­sumption of office by the Upington and Sprigg Ministries no
further appoint­ments of Natives have been made. Will Sir Gordon be
content to be behind Natal in the matter of doing justice to Native
talent? We shall see.

IN other British Colonies where Natives are living side by side with
their Euro­pean benefactors—the Colonists—Natives are largely employed
in the public ser­vice, more especially as a special pre­judice stands
in the way of their employ­ment in commercial spheres. It is the Cape
Colony alone that is still behind hand in this respect. In India every
learned profession, posts of distinction in the army, and even the Bench
of Justice have been thrown open to the Natives of India who are able
and willing to qualify for them. This is, however, not the case in the
Colony of the Cape of Good Hope;

AT Alice, as the Government party was passing through that hamlet, Sir
Gordon Sprigg was interviewed by our country­men on subjects on which
they are chiefly interested. In the course of the interview a Headman,
Kapok, complained that in regard to public business Natives were not
notified in the same way that those who understand English and Dutch
only are notified. All Sir Gordon could say was “the notices were
published, and they had an Inspector of Locations. They ought not to
expect to be taken out of their huts and brought in their blankets to
see about being registered.” This reply will certainly not satisfy the
Natives, who are fast beginning to see that this is a question of moment
with them. True the notices are, as Sir Gordon says, published, but not
in the language that these people can under­stand. As to Inspectors,
they inform them only of such things as it may please them, to do so.
Are there no members of Parliament to take this matter up and help our
people?

Cape Times.

Not the least pleasing feature in the hearty welcome given to Sir Henry
and Lady Loch at Port Elizabeth was the pre­sentation of an address from
the Native people expressing their sense of security under the British
flag and their apprecia­tion of the privileges they enjoy under our
constitutional system of government. Not many days before an instance in
point had occurred on the frontier, an in­stance exhibiting both the
Native jealousy for constitutional right and the advantage in that
respect shared equally by them with the Colonists of a discreet and
impar­tial administration of justice. At the Court held by the Resident
Magistrate of King Williams Town on the fourteenth day of this month for
the revision of the voters’ roll about eighty Native appellants against
the field-cornets’ decisions were represented, and in nearly every case
the appeal was successful. The Editor of the Native newspaper, Imvo,
appeared on be­half of his countrymen on this occasion; and it was only
natural that he should manifest, through the channel at his com­mand, a
reasonable gratification at the re­sult of his labours ; it was natural
also that, while commending the righteous judgment of the magistrate, he
should deliver a testimony against the field­cornets by whom the claims
had been in the first place rejected. It may be noted however, that the
newspaper in organic sympathy with the Government at King Williams Town
is disposed to censure the magistrate severely for overriding the
field-cornets’ valuation of the property upon occupation of which the
claims were based. It appears that the magis­trate accepted the
valuation of a sworn appraiser in preference to that of the Divisional
Council; and at the first glance one would be inclined to admit that the
objection to this preference was well founded. Imvo, however, shows that
the magistrate’s decision was fully justified. The valuator for the
Divisional Council, our Native contemporary asserts, for years past
fixed the value of Natives’ holdings at £25: but when the occupants
began to claim the franchise he suddenly lowered it to £8. It is true,
as urged by the Kaffrarian Watchman, fol­lowing Sir Gordon Sprigg, that
the field­cornets are acquainted with the several wards and with the
circumstances of the people living in them, and are therefore presumably
well qualified to judge of the validity of claims. But the presumption
takes no account of the variety of motives in human nature; and it is
just possible that the desire to keep down the native vote may bias the
judgment of a field-cornet in the valuation of a native right. Indeed
there were some, possibly predisposed to harsh judgment, who averred
that the motive which induced the framer of the Registration amend­ment
Act to setup the field-cornets and registering officers was not
unconnected with this bias. The jealousy for the Divisional Council
valuation now dis­played is somewhat diverting when one recalls the
commendation bestowed on the action of the Resident Magistrate at Alice
in accepting a lower valuation tendered by irresponsible individuals
against the valuation of the Divisional Council which would have made
good the native voters’ qualification. It would appear that in the
opinion represented by the Watchman the just valuation of pro­perty is
that which brings it below the qualifying level: the primary object
be­ing to deprive the native of his vote. The native expression of
confidence how­ever in the assured enjoyment of their constitutional
privileges, and in the im­partial administration of justice, has been
happily illustrated at King Williams Town. So long as the magistrates
are faithful to their trust—and competent in­terpreters of the law — the
sense of security professed in the native address to the Governor is
beyond fear of disturb­ance.
