he had been placed by Government, is looked upon by many thousands ol
Fingoes in the Colony anS in the Transkei, and even as far as Natal, as

some Englishmen held in the seventeenth cen­tury, that ‘the kitig can do

such should find no place among paid officials as at present.”

  TERRITORIAL GOVERNMENT.

THE annexation of Pondoland to the Colony renders acute a question that
has long lain dormant—as to the best form of Government for the Native
Terri­tories beyond the Kei, bordering on Natal in the East and the Free
State on the North ; for the

present arrangement of granting them Responsible Government as it is
carried out by the Cape seems to be an anomaly and an absurdity. We
think we are not wide of the mark in saying that by the bulk.of the
people in this Colony Responsible Government is not perfectly
understood. Public opinion does not appear to have sufficiently
developed within the old boundaries of the Colony itself for the proper
carrying of the great principles of this form ofGovernment. Thus we see
many men elected to Parliament, not so much for their views as for their
personal popular­ity. True, they do go through the form of announcing
certain views ; but we contend it is not so much on the views they
declare that they are elected or rejected ; nor have constituencies been
educated up to the true appreciation of the prin­ciples of the form of
Government they are supposed to enjoy—to the point of insisting upon the
com­bining of Parliamentary men who hold like views on most great
questions, and sinking minor differences, to have their views translated
into legislation. To our mind Responsible Government within the old
Colony is still in an elementary and chaotic state, with uncertain and
doubtful results. If such be the case in regard to the older portion of
the Colony, what can be said of the extension of this form of Government
into territories said to be habitations of barbarism ? The question is
an important one; for it is intolerable to think of the future of the
large Native population in the Transkei, Tembu- land, East Griqualand
and Pondoland being subject to the uncertain action of a chance majority
in a Parliament still feel­ing its way in regard to the best manner to
carry out Responsible Government. Not that we have any doubt as to the
capacity of our people for self government, by no means; for anybody
with even a superficial knowledge of the Native knows that from time
im­memorial our people have enjoyed among themselves such a measure of
autonomy as laid on each in­dividual some sort of responsibility for the
good name of his state. But the step seems to us a gigantic one from the
simple Native system of self-rule to the granting of a full­blown
Responsible Government still imperfectly understood by those who grant
it. Then, what is to be said of the anomaly of such a large Native
population being represented in the Cape Parliament by one gentleman —Mr
CHAS. MOLTENO—in whom the Natives can profess to have any confidence.
And yet even this is begrudged them, by those who hold that the members
for the Native Territories should not represent the Natives but the
sporadic European trader there ! This opens up a question, broached in a
recent article in The Times (London), very likely coming from the pen of
that accomplished lady Miss SHAW who visited this Colony some time back.
In that article the suggestion is thrown out that Sir MARSHALL CLARKE of
Basutoland fame would be the best officer to administer the Government
of Pondoland. No doubt The Times argument proceeds on the assumption
that the newly- acquired territory is to be ad­ministered on some sort
of pre­paratory state before final absorp­tion into the Cape system.
This seems to be the correct view ; and it would work all the better if
all these Native territories were lumped and given some sort of
preparatory form of representative Government under a
Lieutenant-Governor with an Executive Council and a Parlia­ment of their
own ; for from ad ministration from Capetown they can hope nothing in
the way of reaping any material benefits.

undertaken are concerned. This is more to be regretted, since important
Native measures like the Glen Grey lands settlement are to be introduced
in the ensuing session; and in connection with such legisla­tion it
would be a great pity if the Government were to bring forward and force
through Parliament by means of a majority, uninstructed in the true
requirements of our people, legislation that would be prejudicial and
unacceptable both to the people themselves and to their friends.

The latest move of the Bond is to force on Government the rectification
of land disorders, which have arisen in the Kat River Settlement, The
case which they wish to isolate and deal with by itself, is one and the
same with that of the mean Whites. They and the Hottentots must stand or
fall together. If there is to be legislation, one measure must be served
out to all.

It is the fact that in many instances among the Hottentots there are
boedel erven, or holdings possessed without titles, on which a large
number of human beings are crowded in a state of semi­starvation, and
they either pay no rates or are hopelessly in arrears. As they never
recovered themselves from the loss of their cattle in the war, they let
out for the sake of their commonage parts of their ground to Kafirs who
have stock, some of whom are thieves and rob the farmers.

Things cannot continue in this state, but it is only fair to hear the
other side.

All this has come about by the opera­tion of two bad laws—the Dutch law
o f Inheritance, and the law of Succession and of Transfer, According to
the Dutch law of Inheritance all property must be divided equally among
the children, and in landed property if the eldest cannot pay in money
the shares of the others, (and usually he cannot), the land is
sub­divided among them, and a small piece of ground falls to each, which
becomes to him a “ White Elephant ”—he can get no money for it, and he
will not abandon it to make a living elsewhere. The mode of Succession
and of Transfer is just as mischievous. The dues are heavy enough, but
as these things have to be effected in the Deeds Office, in Capetown,
and the man himself cannot do them without employing an agent he is
exposed to the most shameful and disgusting extortion and robbery by
unscrupulous agents who prey upon his ignorance. Had there been the Law
of Primogeniture, and could Succession and Transfer in these small
holdings be effected on the spot by the R.M., who would arrangs with the
Deeds Office, things would never have come to this pass. Besides, the
harbour­ing of thieves is not peculiar to the Hottentots. For the sake
of occasional labour the farmers allow numbers o Natives to squat on
their farms, and there are thieves among them too, only they are astute
enough to steal cross-wise, that is, not from their own employers.

Among the possible solutions of this difficult problem, it has been
suggested for consideration, whether Government should not resume
possession of all the boedel erven, on the ground that no rates or dues
are paid, and should compensate the occupants by dividing among them the
Divisional Council value of the land. Then, if any of these heirs can
pay the money, he can have the whole erf; other­wise let it be put up
for sale. This plan is good in intention, but cruel in execu­tion. Its
effect would be the eviction of the Hottentots from Kat River; for nene
of these people can pay so much, and the lands would at once be bought
up by the Dutch farmers—the very thing they want. Its legality is also
doubtful. The title of the original erf is unconditional, and Parliament
cannot confiscate private pro­perty in that fashion. Besides, what are
the evicted Hottentots to do. Any cash they might get would soon be
spent, and they would issue forth on the Colony as paupers. But it is
supposed they could go and find work amongst farmers. Yes, at four-pence
a day, and two-pence worth of maize, an exhilarating prospect for a man
with a family.

However, something must be done, and there is a choice between two
things. Either pass a measure in Parliament to apply to the whole
Colony; for the “ Poor Whites” in Oudtshoorn and Zwagershoek are exactly
in the same situation as the Hottentots—and the Dutch must face that
fact.

Or rather, the subject should be considerately and gradually dealt with
by Government in a series of measures, including Primogeniture, Minimum
size of holdings to stop endless subdivision, easy Succession, and (if
possible) Trans­fer, through the R.M., and Landboards with powers, and
aid from Government in money. As these boedel erAholders died out, or
went away, the landboard could give compensation in money to whom it was
due, and either sell the ground to any other on the same original Erf,
lease it to him.

If the English members allow the Bond to push the Ministry into a scheme
of Eviction, that will turn this country into another Ireland, and make
large sections of the Co.lo,ny a hive from which paupers come forth to
bo a curse wherever they go.

The Native community had better bestir themselves on this laud question,
for it will come round presently to the Kafir locations and to the
