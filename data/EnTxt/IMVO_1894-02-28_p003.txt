POSITION OF PARTIES.

NOW that the elections are
virtually over politicians

I naturally turn to counting the

scores made by the various Parties. We notice that a great many of our
contemporaries have largely de­pended on a sort of political
meteor­ological list kept somehow by the Eastern Province Herald while
the elections lasted, showing gains to the various Parties into which
the House is at present divided. To show, however, how unreliable our
contemporary’s catalogue is it may suffice to mention that it has a long
list of what it calls Ministerialists ” in which members of the Bond
Party are jumbled up with many who would spurn to be numbered with the
Bond, perhaps because, although differing on principle on many
questions, yet they agree in keeping certain men in office for a given
time—to do what it is not for us to enquire at present. But the futility
of the Herald's list of a Ministerial Party reveals itself clearly when
one consid­ers the Queenstown and Somerset East emotions. In the former
a mem­ber of the Ministry was opposed tooth and nail by Bondmen, who are
sup­posed to support that same Ministry; while at Somerset East an
adherent of Sir Gr. SPRIGG’S was unseated by the same Bond which
supports that gentleman. The fact of the matter is that the Bond arc a
Party by themselves, holding certain views on public question against
the Op­position ; also a distinct Party pro­fessing to have definite
convictions on matters such as the Native Land Question, the Liquor
Traffic, the Excise, and the Taxation of necessities. What it is exactly
that men like Messrs. HOCKLY, FROST, LAW­RENCE, SCHERMBRUCKER, and
others, hold, as between the two views of the Bond and the Opposition
has yet to be learnt. Meantime this third group has, for convenience
sake, been called Wobblers—with a capital W. Rejecting the
classification of the Herald, then, and classifying the members under
the three head­ings of Bond, Opposition, and Wobblers one finds the
nearest con­ception of the state of Parties in the new House. Even then
there are half-a-dozen or so members who have entered Parliament for
Western constituencies, although most strenu­ously opposed by the Bond.
It would be more than human for these members to go on identifying
themselves with a party which has fought them so valiantly, and
there­fore we have taken the liberty to rank these gentlemen with the
Op­position with a cross (t). Some surprise may bo occasioned by Mr. VAN
DER WALT’S appearing among the Opposition ; but those who know him well
know that that in­dependent thinker is more with the Party of which the
guileless Mr. ROSE-INNES forms a prominent Member than with any other.

In these circumstances, at any rate, until the Wobblers settle down
definitely to the carrying out of certain fixed and defined views and
amalgamate either with the Op­position or with the Bond the Government
must continue a sort of a coalition between the Bond Party and the
Wobbler Party. The elections have thus succeeded in readjusting the
politics of the country. Whereas before the power lay with the Bond, as
a balancing factor; it is now with the Wobblers, for with whichever side
they elect to side that for the moment must triumph. That Mr. RHODES has
already seen this may be gathered from the remark he let fall in his
Klipdam address that on the Scab Question his Government may have to go.
Now it is well known the Bond are against a General Scab Act, while the
Op­position are pledged to it Should the Government introduce the
measure therefore, as parties arc constituted it will be risking
alienating the Bond to fall back on the Opposition for support. The
situation brought about by the elections is thus in many ways an
interesting one.

THE PONDOLAND SITUATION.

  ROM all we gather from the
  newspapers there is no question

  that nothing more popular could be
  undertaken by the Government
  than a war for the subjugation of
  the Rondos. Up to last week this
  journal has been as a voice of one
  crying in the wilderness in demand-
  ing that the usual formalities of
  giving the Pondos a chance to reply
  to the Government ultimatum should
  be gone through. Before then
  all were for the grim alternative
  of marching an army into Pondo-
  land without warning, a la the
  invasion of Matebeleland. We are
  glad to welcome the support of the
  Eastern Province Herald in our
  contention, although it seems to
  smack strongly of wisdom after the
  event, seeing that our contemporary
  wrote after it had become known that
  Major ELLIOT would be sent to
  convey the decision of the Govern-
  ment (which is all we strongly
  insisted upon). It says :—•

  There is uncertainty about the action
  of the Government with regard to Pondo-
  land, and no possibility of any misunder-
  standing on the part of the Pondos. It
  may be admitted that there is something
  in the complaint may by Imvo that the
  Colony has not been altogether consistent
  in dealing with the Pondos. These
  revelling Alsatians have been threatened
  so often that they have taken comfort in
  their own adaptation of the proverb that
  threatened men live long. They have
  listened to reprimands from the Cape with
  their tongues in their cheeks, and they
  have gone away to repeat their offences.
  Their offending has not been so much
  against the Cape as against humanity.
  Still they have never been quite suffi-
  ciently impressed with the fact that the
  Cape must and will put an end to
  the state of things which has so long
  scandalised the people, and this state
  of things is going to be put an end
  to now. If the Pondos like to listen to
  the final word of Major Elliot, well and
  good, they will be allowed lo set their
  own house in order. That there is the
  least likelihood of their doing this, we
  are not sanguine enough to believe. It
  is, however, necessary to go through the
  formality of giving them the opportunity,
  and if the opportunity is not grasped the
  Cape will have no option but to take over
  the country and govern it.

  The Government has, after all
  not followed the LOBENGULA pre-
  cedent of blazing away at the
  Pondos without warning: and in
  this we think they have shown
  wisdom. We gave the terms of the
  official decision in our last. To the
  announcement was added the de-
  claration that:—

  This step has been taken in accordance
  with an understanding with the Imperial
  Government that the Colony should at
  the proper time adopt such measures as
  might be doomed necessary for the
  establishment of law and order in Pondo-
  land.

  In support of the action taken by this
  Government, the Government of Natal
  made strong representations to tho eftect
  that tho loss and unrest caused by tho
  continuous invasions of Natal territory
  by the Pondos, for whom tho Cape
  Government is responsible, can no longer
  be tolerated.

  Major Elliot, C.M.G., who has been
  commissioned to convey the decision of
  the Government to the Pondo chiefs, will
  proceed forthwith to Pondoland for that
  purpose.

  Now, it seems to us that if the
  Pondos were wise, they would at
  once accept the situation forced
  upon them, and make what they
  can out of it. A strong deputation
  to Capetown to arrange and fix the
  conditions of coming under Govern-
  ment as to land, liquor, represent-
  ation appears necessary; and
  generally we see no reason why the
  Pondos should not come under the
  Government in a way that would be
  decent and honourable to both them-
  selves and the Government.

[LETTER TO THE EDITOR.]

  SIR,—A case of very great importance
  to Natives in general and to those of East
  London in particular came in for hearing
  before the C.C, and R.M., of ‘Russia’
  within Her Majesty’s dominion — East
  London—on the 19lh instant. Mr. Paul
  Xiniwe of Kingwilliamstown was “run
  in ” on the lltb, charged with the heinous
  crime of putting in an appearance and
  seeing a friend oil’, at the Railway Station
  platform. There and then, all of a
  sudden, a petty railway official, armed
  with very little authority, wishing no
  doubt, to make himself a name and be
  promoted for the heroic service rendered,
  laid violent hand on Mr. Xiniwe in the
  roughest manner possible, assisted by
  two others interested in “running in” a
  well behave Native gentleman, and
  without even consulting the Station-
  Master was locked up in goal. This
  official refused bail as he said : “ no nigger
  impudence here sir.” Our friend, hav-
  ing spent 3 miserable hours in goal, a
  good Samaritan turned up at last in the
  person of Mr. Cochrane, and hearing of
  this dirty business made speed to help
  him. At Court, on the 19th, tho evidence
  against him was fearfully lame and the
  verdict of “not guilty” was found—to
  the great joy of darkies present. Mr.
  Xiniwe lias fought the good fight and
  taught East London people a lesson or two
  on their hostility to Natives and what I
  would like to see him do now is to sue
  the Colonial Government Railways for
  .£100 damages for unlawfully, maliciously,
  wrongfully, spitefully (and a dozen other
  adverbs) for assaulting and imprisoning
  hi tn. East London is a hotbed for negro-
  phobism which must be mended or ended.
  This very same town had a stick case
  business not very long ago which was
  detrimental to it. And now it turns an
  awful leaf in “ running in ” well behaved
  gentlemen for nothing. It is, therefore,
  Mr. Editor, with acme of bliss, that I pen
  this letter in the fervent hope that you
  will find space for it in the columns of
  your lively and respectable paper. East
  London people must know that there are
  Natives and Natives and as long as I am
  spared I will watch them with a very
  watchful eye and teach them, though
  black, a lesson or two on negrophilism.
