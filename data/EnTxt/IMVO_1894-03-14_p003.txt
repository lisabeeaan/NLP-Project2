The recent flogging of our AGITATION countrymen at Johannes- burg on a
frivolous pre tence that they walked on public footpaths is begin­ning
to react on the Government of the Transvaal under whom the outrage has
taken place. That old friend of justice, Mr. W. B. Chalmers, has written
a scathing letter to the Cape Times calling on the people of this Colony
to enter a vigorous protest against the enormity; and he is ably backed
up by our con­temporary in denoucing these deeds of shame. Over and
above this meetings have been held in Capetown—one, under the auspices
of the Forward Movement presided over by Mr. W. Hay, M.L.A., and another
by Natives at present resi­ding in the metropolis under the chairmanship
of Rev. Father Osborne— with the result that the Premier as Secretary
for Native Affairs and the Governor have been communicated with, We wish
the agitation a successful issue.

Sir Henry Loch’s reported GOVERNOR visit to England turns out SIR H.
LOCH, now to be something more than a visit; but would seem to be a
return home. At all events we gather as much from certain remarks that
His Excellency made at the laying of the foundation stone in Capetown
last week. Together with this must be read the intimation of the Star,
an authority in Cape Government matters, that his successor is already
known in official circles in London. The full term of a Governor's
tenure of office is six years; and Sir Henry Loch only came to the Cape
at the end of 1889. Thus he had two years of his full period to run ;
and the Governor’s retirement in these cir­cumstances has the appearance
of a recall, which we should not at all be surprised at now that he has
developed a policy of settling by the sword every little difficul­ty
with Native tribes who trusted to the faithfulness of the British
Government to deal fairly and even with forbearance towards them.
Previously Sir Bartie Frere acted in this manner and drew the Imperial
Government into difficulties long ere they knew where they were; and
like a burnt child the British Govern­ment naturally knows what to do
with a proconsul who commits them in the way the High Commissioner has
done in Matebeleland and Poadoland with scarce any provocation.

Some weeks ago Messrs. FLEECE-WASHED Peacock Bros. & Weir WOOL.
addressed to our Kafir readers an important letter which has attracted
no ordinary attention among our readers. We shall let it speak for
itself:—“ Sir,— Permit us to draw the attention of your readers to an
evil that has assumed con­siderable proportions during the last wool
season by mixing unwashed and port washed with fieecewashed wools.
Native fieecewashed wool has had a good name here and in the Home
Markets for many years, because it has been what it has professed to
be—wool fairly washed before shearing, and shorn while still free from
grease or yolk. But its good name is in dinger of being lost. For some
seasons past many parcels of fieecewashed wool have been found to be
mixed with grease woo), i.e., wool that has never been washed in the
river—or wool that has been left on the sheep so long a time after being
washed that it has become grease wool again. This last season a
considerable proportion of the clip of wool offered as fieecewashed here
has been condemned as mixed, and dealt with accordingly. We would advise
all owners of sheep, in their own interest, either to wash their wool
well and then shear and pack it separately from all grease wool, or else
not to wash it at all. Buyers here and in Europe can value it nauch more
confidently when either one or the other—but when it is “mixed” their
judgment is at fault, and they must then value with a margin against the
seller. Mixed wool will damage the whole clip of fieecewashed wool, and
the man who will suffer in the end, is the grower. The merchants may be
done once or twice and the traders also; but they will protect
themselves in the end at the expense of the grower. If all traders would
refuse to buy mixed wool as fieecewashed, they would act wisely; but
some of them do not, and so, fora time they encourage dishonesty among
the mixers. Yours faithfully,—PEACOCK BROS. & WEIR,”

ABOUT PONDO MISRULE.

  UROPEAN colonists seem to
  attach great importance at

the present time to the civilisation
of Pondoland. Indeed the quarrel
which the RHODES Government are
trying to pick up with the Pondos
is said to be for putting down
misrule, and introducing into that
country civilised Government. At
so laudable a motive no one
desirous to see the country improve
can grumble ; and as a matter of
fact the attitude of this journal on
Pondo affairs has been called in
question because it was suspected
to be unfavourable to the bringing
about of civilisation in Pondoland.
Now, the Pondos are not the first
Natives to come under Colonial
rule ; and knowing as we do that
Natives under the Government have
not, in the main, been raided in civi-
lisation much higher than the
Natives under the so-called Pondo
misrule, we confess we are not very
enthusiastic over what is now being
done in that, country in the name
of civilisation. For example take
the 16,000 Natives of Peddie who
have been under the Government
for ever so long. There the net
result, as seen in the prosperity of
the Natives, of boasted civilised
Government is that only ninety of
our people, under the old £25
property qualification, could be-
come Registered Voters! The
rest were still below that figure,
steeped in the barbarism which has
so incensed the Government in
Pondoland as to cause it in so tight
a financial Railway push to embark
on extra expenditure to send forces
to bring the Pondos under civilisa-
tion. We shall be told that in the
Colony there are no smelling out
outrages, etc., which exist to a
great extent in Pondoland ; but it
has been maintained on behalf of
the Pondos that inquiry would
show these to be largely exagger-
ated for the purposes of bringing
about a Pondo situation. Be this,
however, as it may, we nevertheless
submit that outrages and boycotting,
growing from beliefs in witchcraft,
are still prevalent under the oogis of
the very Government which is mov-
ing to chastise the Pondos. Who does
not know that Colonial judges have
condemned a number of Natives in
these parts to the gallows for
having killed their neighbours
because of these very beliefs ?
But since the Pondo way of
dealing with these is not draped
in the whole of the paraphernalia
of the hangman, they must needs
be wiped off the earth. It seems
to us that it doss not lie with
a Government which has done
nothing for Native government
beyond placing the people under
a number of officials who draw
salaries, doing precious little else,
and then called this the civilising
of the Natives, to pick up a quarrel
with the Pondos for acting precisely
in the same way, simply because
instead of officials there they have
Native chiefs These are but some
of the reasons which weigh with us
and make us sceptical that any
good cither to the Pondos or to
Government is to bo harvested
from the heavy expenditure now
being incurred by the Ministry
in the mobilisation of the. Colonial
forces to humble people who needed
not the humbling. The Cape Gov-
ernment had its house to put in
order ; and not until it had suc-
ceeded in this will any fairminded.
man admit that it had a call from
anywhere to proceed to Pondoland
on the wild-goose chase of civi-
lising ” the Pondos.
