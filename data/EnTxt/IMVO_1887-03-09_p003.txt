Native Opinion

Mr. R. Solomon’s A SENSE of much Candidature. satisfaction will,

we feel sure, pervade the native mind throughout South Africa, when it
becomes generally known that Mr. RICHARD SOLOMON has consented to be put
in nomination for the seat in the Assembly vacated by Mr. DYER. Mr.
SOLOMON, who is one of the cleverest lawyers of the present day, is a
son of the late Rev. E. SOLOMON of Bedford, and nephew of Mr. SAUL
SOLOMON, both of whom have been, in their day, the staunchest and ablest
champions of native rights that the Colony has ever had. With our people
the interest centres in the fact that his consenting to stand seems to
indicate the arrival of the time when the noble traditions of the
SOLOMON family must be revived and perpet­uated in the Parliament of the
country. And certainly Parliament became very poor indeed when it lost
the ability of the SOLOMONS, the PORTERS, the PATERSONS, the BU­CHANANS,
the SMITHS and the DE VILLIERSES.

Mr. RICHARD SOLOMON received his early training at Lovedale, and
afterwards at the South African College, Cape Town, from which, on
obtaining the First Class Certificate in Literature and Science of the
late Board of Examiners (which is equivalent to the M. A. degree of the
Cape University), he pro­ceeded to the University of Cam­bridge where he
graduated M.A. He then qualified for the higher walks of the law, and on
being called to the bar he returned to the Co­lony, and began to
practise in the Eastern Districts Court, where he soon achieved
distinction by his eloquence, energy, and ability ; rose to the foremost
place in the pro­fession ; and not so long ago His Excellency the
Governor recognised his legal acumen by taking him on his suite to
Mauritius as legal ad­viser in connection with the HEN­NESSY-LLOYD
episode.

It is this distinguished Colonist, born and bred, who now solicits the
suffrages of the electors of King William’s Town—all efforts to se­cure
Dr. BERRY, whose name has been frequently mentioned in con­nection with
the vacancy, having proved abortive. We earnestly trust that the
electors in this dis­trict will have the good sense to return Mr.
SOLOMON. In Native Policy he will, we believe, be found to have embraced
the views that have long been associated with the illustrious name of
SOLOMON—views which have been contemptuously described as negrophilistic
by un­scrupulous opponents, but which, so far as we know, have been
nothing more nor less than the result of a strong conviction that fair
and just

treatment for the Native is all that is necessary to make a prosperous
and a contented citizen of him. On other matters affecting the
well­being of the country generally, Mr. SOLOMON’S profound learning and
experiences on the Eastern Circuit ought to be the best guaran­tee that
all such questions shall re­ceive earnest consideration at his hands,
and we may rest assured that, at any rate, he will record an intelligent
vote on each. In sup­porting him we confidently feel that the Native
people will be electing a man after their own heart—one whose love of
justice has been inbred in him; a colonist of much promise; and a
gentle­man of whom any constituency may be proud. By assisting in Mr.
SOLOMON’S return our countrymen will, moreover^ afford further and
stronger demonstration of their intelligence and sagacity to send the
most useful representative to the Parliament of the country, and thus
give the lie to the oft-repeated calumny that they were miserable
barbarians who had no notion of the vote, and of matters relating to
their own good government.

WE observe that the Missionary Kafir periodical printed at the Lovedale
Insti­tution deals out some hard blows in its issue of this month to the
deputation from the Native Educational Association that waited upon Sir
Gordon Sprigg at Grahamstown. After heaping ridicule on the members who
composed the deputation for having lost their heads, as it says, in at
all attempting to interview the Premier, our contemporary proceeds to
satirize the incident in the following language: “We are sent by the
whole native community (the deputies are re­presented to have said) to
come and say ‘Hail! Hail!! Hail!!!’ How nice, Sir Gordon, is your
Government. Our coun­trymen have sent us because of what we are to
express their confidence in your conduct of affairs, as we have no­thing
to complain of in your adminis­tration. It is because the deputation saw
Sir Gordon Sprigg, clad in ‘ tiger kaross' peculiar to office that they
misrepresented their people, and came away perplexed and depressed,
making use of Mr. Mzimba as the small branch that the monkey uses in
hiding its face, saying that it is his paper that hampered them.” In
another paragraph Isigidimi goes on to say: “ If the bulk of the natives
were able to read the English papers they might have seen Sir Gordon
Sprigg went and told his countrymen the following‘ I have met a native
deputation who have expressed confidence in my Government, and that the
Natives are satisfied with my admin­istration ’ It is this declaration
of con­fidence that has offended our educated countrymen, because they
are able to read all the papers for themselves. But Imvo hides this
enormity, and they are hiding their shame behind Mr. Mzimba, who is
falsely accused. Let our countrymen understand clearly that the language
used by the deputation in expressing thanks to Sir Gordon Sprigg for the
beneficence of his Government has never been contra­dicted : it stands
as it was before, and has gone forth to the world in its prestine
nakedness. And now that they are at home—heigh presto!—they urge it as a
matter of complaint that harm had been done to the community by the son
of Mzimba, while all the time they were giving them the head of a coney
(delud­ing) them, for they had done the greatest injury to the people by
expressing confidence in Sir Gordon Sprigg ! ” With reference to the
above we can only say all that transpired at the interview was recorded
in these columns at the time, and had Isigidimi compared our report and
the speech of the Prime Minister, it would have seen the wisdom of
moder­ating its remarks. But Sir Gordon and the delegates of the
Association must be abused. Hence the desire to misrepre­sent. But we
always thought the Isigidi­mi was not free to deal with party politics.

THE genuine grief occasioned by Mr Irvine’s untimely removal found vent
on Saturday last when all that was mortal of him was carried to the King
William’s Town cemetery. The public attended in considerable numbers,
and from remote corners of the vast district of Kaffraria.
Representative natives were also present to testify to their intense
sorrow at the irreparable loss of a faithful friend and true spokesman.
Melancholy interest clings to the fact reported by the Cape Mercury that
“ there was another repre­sentative following whose presence was
specially arranged for by his father,” namely the eldest son of Mr. Saul
Solo­mon, who came from Lovedale with Dr. Stewart. And it may not be out
of place here to record the conviction which widely prevails among our
countrymen that native rights have never had in the past more vigilant
advocates than Messrs. Saul Solomon, John J. Irvine, and Mr. Sauer.

THERE is an old fellow in Grahamstown who is stingy, and has a very
homely wife. As he was leaving his domicile, to which he had just
removed, last Thursday morning, his wife asked him for some money. “
What do you want money for ?” “ To buy curtains with," she replied. What
do you want with curtains ?” “ To put up on the parlour windows.” “ What
in the world do you want with curtains up on the parlour windows ?” “ So
that people across the street cannot see me.” “ Have they seen you yet
P” “ Not that I know of.” “ Well, you may depend that when they see you
they will certainly send you money to buy curtains.”
