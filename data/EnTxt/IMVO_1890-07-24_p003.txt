Native Opinion

A NEW Ministry has been installed Ministry, in office since our last
issue. Then we were able to state positively that the Hon CECIL JOHN
RHODES, the well-known member for Barkly West, had undertaken to
construct a Cabinet, on Mr. SAUER finding that the country’s interests
de­manded that a gentleman enjoying the confidence of a larger circle of
the members of Parliament should be sent for. This self-denying act on
the part of the Member for Aliwal North in patriotically re­linquishing
an unquestioned claim to the Premiership, from a desire to subserve his
country rather than his personal reputation, has lifted Mr. SAUER in the
estimation of the country generally. A less unselfish politician would,
for the honour of the position, have grasped the offer like grim death,
even if he felt he could only sustain it for a twelvemonth. Those who
know Mr. SAUER well, know that he is above being affected by puny
am­bition of this sort; and, a quondam enthusiastic disciple of Sir
GORDON SPRIGG’S. who in 1879 parted from his chief in obedience to the
dic­tates of his conscience, just when the country was resounding, and
resounding again, with applause for the then evangelist of “ Vigour ”— a
gentleman who thus scorned a cheap popularity, and was content to wander
in the dreary deserts of Opposition for an indefinite time— was scarcely
the statesman to snatch at the Premiership, and to secure it by a fluke.
So he resigned the honour in favour of his more popu­lar colleague of
Diamond and Interior fame; and it is now a matter of history that Mr.
RHODES —Amalgamator-General that he is —has succeeded in framing as
strong and attractive a Cabinet for brains, wide research, and,
conse­quently general and generous sym­pathies as has ever graced the
Treasury Benches in those gorgeous Houses of Parliament under the shadow
of Table Mountain. Versed in schemes of amalgamation, the new Premier
has managed to bring together long-headed men who, a short while ago,
were supposed to be in such antagonistic political re­lationship as to
preclude the hope of their ever putting their heads to­gether to assist
their country. As a matter of record, we give the names of the
newly-constructed Government. On the 17th instant it was made known in a
Gazette Extraordinary that His Excellency the Governor had been pleased,
in the name and on behalf of Her Majesty, to appoint

The Honourable John Xavier Merriman, Esquire, to be Treasurer «f the
Colony ;

The Honourable Jacobus Wilhelmus Sauer, Esquire, to be Colonial
Secretary.

The Honourable Cecil John Rhodes, Esquire, to be Commissioner of Crown
Lands and Public Works, and Premier.

The Honourable Pieter Hendrik Faure, Esquire, to be Secretary for Native
Affairs. By command of His Excellency the Governor, G. SEYMOUR FORT,

Private Secretary.

Another intimation by His Ex­cellency notified the appointment of Mr. J.
ROSE-INNES, Q.C., Mr. JAMES SIVEWRIGHT, C.M.G., and Mr. P. H. FAURE to
be members of Her Majesty’s Executive Council of the Cape of Good Hope.

As for their policy the country is bidden by the Premier to judge by
deeds, not by words, his doings in the past being an earnest of
achieve­ments in the future. This being the case, we may rest assured
that our people will be allowed to work out their development free from
petty and useless interference of Government, for keeping up the
evidences of Native policy. The very fact that Ministers are men of
education, capable of taking a wide view of matters, is a sure guarantee
that needless interference with a subject race, from motives of
jealousy, will not come into play, and we may look for the sympa­thetic
assistance of Government in such matters as those in which help may be
asked for by Missionaries, Magistrates, and others entrusted with the
interests of the Natives, with a view to improve their condition. Mr.
RHODES’S mind is not capable of grasping small matters which are easy of
elimination in the work­ing out of a great problem such as that of
asserting the premier posi­tion of the Cape Colony among South African
States. It is from this source we draw the comfort we do for our
countrymen. Besides, with gentlemen such as Messrs. SAUER, INNES, and
SIVEWRIGHT, (we know little about Mr. FAURE) whose principles are
opposed to any­thing savouring of injustice to Natives in the Cabinet,
we have, we think, no reason to be apprehensive.

EVEX before Mr. Cecil Rhodes, the new Premier, ha4 had an opportunity to
an­nounce the Government policy, the enemy, we believe, had flashed
along the wires to one of the papers the news that one of the planks of
the platform of the Administration would be the raising of the
Franchise, this being the bait by which support was successfully angled
for. But no allusion was made to it in the Premier’s programme as placed
before the House on Thursday last, and we choose to disbelieve it until
further orders. The general drift of legislation in this respect
throughout the British Empire is to lower the franchise to secure
popular representation in the Govern­ment. To raise it is simply to
exclude the people, and to establish an oligarchy, which no fair and
liberal mind can con­template as among things which it would be politic
or desirable to do.

BECAUSE Mr. Sauer firmly believes in the principle of Government acting
with unscrupulous justice and fairness to all sections of the people of
the same State as the only way to establish contentment and peace he is
called a negrophilist by those who would stir up against him the
prejudices of the crowd. It is in this light that we construe the
following extract from the Queenstown Free Press:— Messrs. Sauer, Innes,
and Merriman, who are pronounced friends of the aborigines of the
country, and who have always fought for their rights in Parliament, are
in no better plight with respect to the support of the Dutch party,
although there has been a tendencv on of these gentlemen to fraternize
with the Bond in order to defeat the recent Gov­ernment, and get a spell
in office for themselves. If they can conscientiously throw aside past
convictions with respect to our native policy, and run as near the wind
as possible with the Bond party in their views as to how the native
popula­tion should be governed, then there would be some chance of
forming a Ministry, and a prospect of holding office, but what would be
the position be worth at such a sacrifice, just the £ s. d. to which
everything else would become subservient.
