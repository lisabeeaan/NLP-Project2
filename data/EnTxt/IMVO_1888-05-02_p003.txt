during that period are the eminent names of Bishop COLENSO, Lord

the RightHon. W. E. FORSTER, Mr. SAMUEL GURNEY, General COLIN MACKENZIE,

Within the same given time we have with much grief lived to see Mr. SAUL
SOLOMON disabled.

It is our melancholy duty to-day to chronicle the premature death of Mr-
F. VV. CHESSON, Secretary of the Aborigines’ Protection Society, who in
that position may be said to have been the motive force of the cause of
philanthropy in recent years. It would seem that Mr. CHESSON’S death
took place in London on Sunday last. His death is an event that will
cause the spontaneous and general mourn­ing that ever united in a common
emotion the thousands and tens of thousands of the aboriginal races
throughout the reading-world.

Mr. CHESSON’S name is so closely connected with the history and work of
the Aborigines’ Protection Society that it is impossible to refer to his
death without in some way glancing at the work of the Society. The
Society was established in 1837 by the great Sir THOMAS FOWELL BUXTON
whose namesake and re­presentative is still an active member ot the
Committee of the Society. Sir T. F. BUXTON’S health shortly’ after­wards
broke down, and his place was taken by Dr. HODGKIN who conducted the
Society to bis death The task which Dr- HODGKIN was compelled to drop
was taken up by Mr. CHESSON who has carried it on with amazing tact,
energy and ability. The work of the Aborigines’ Protection Society lies
in the direction of softening th« antagonism of race, which is the
inevitable result of the colonising enterprises of the nations of
Europe. Great Britain, too, through her numerous colonies, is being
brought, year by year, into closer intercourse with uncivilized man, and
as therefore causes of misunderstanding between the two are perpetually
liable to arise it is surely desirable that a body should exist with the
object the Aborigines’ Protection Society has made it its duty to
compass. As Secretary of the Society, Mr. CHESSOM has done excellent
yeoman’s service. With correspondents all over the world constantly
claiming his attention and services, and having to deal with the most
intricate, delicate and divergent questions, arising out of the
relations of civilized and uncivilized races, he exhibited an
extraordinary capacity of mastering and assimilating the information
sent him. This would be conceded to him by the most inveterate among his
opponents who have resented his advocacy of justice for the Natives of
South Africa, as interference. That all may not have agreed with the
deductions he drew from facts before him is natural enough, but no one
can for a moment contend that he has been inaccurate as to bis facts. It
has been his faculty to grasp facts which constituted him in the eyes of
friends and foes, an authority on aboriginal questions.

The late Secretary of the A. P. S. possessed in the highest degree, the
happy knack to unite in the promo­tion of the cause of humanity, men of
all religious and political creeds.
