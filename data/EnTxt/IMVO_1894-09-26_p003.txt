Topics of the Day.

“ Umhlobo ” (Friend) is the Kafir name that the Tembu tribes under
Matanzima and Ndarala, residing in the Magistracy of St. Mark’s, have
agreed to confer on their esteemed and deservedly popular magistrate,
Mr. II. H. Bunn. An obser­vant Native friend of ours remarks that the
name is descriptive of what Mr. Bunn is, and sits well on him ; for, as
a magis­trate, he is marked for his extreme im­partiality ; neither
colour, creed, high or low estate, receiving special treatment from him.
He is kind to all. It is for these characteristics that they have given
him so magnificent a name. The wish of these people is that all
magistrates were like Mr. Bunn, and that he may not change this worthy
attitude. We very much felicitate our people who are fortunate enough to
have such a magistrate.

The Cape Governorship

When his Excellency Sir Henry Loch left these shores on his present
furlough, it was generally stated that he would not return. There were
not wanting those who disbelieved and contradicted the re­port, But that
there was a certain amount of truth on both sides is evident from the
fact that no surprise whatever, and but little comment, has been evoked
by the announcement that Sir Henry Loch is to

return to South Africa. It is stated in the conventionally inspired
fashion that Sir Henry has for a considerable period been anxious to
resign his appointment, leav­ing the public to assume that he has
graciously consented, at the special re­quest of some power, to swamp
his per­sonal desires and oblige the Government and the Colonial Office.
Th's is all very line, but who does not know that if Sir Henry desired
to get out of harness Lord Ripon would probably be able to find without
much difficulty a suitable person to accept an office worth about
£10,000 per annum. At present it is understood that his Excellency
leaves England on the 13th or 20th prox, for the scene of his labours.

The ‘ Journal” on Inspiration-

Somehow the Journal (Grahamstown) has of late thought it an unanswerable
argument in dealing with matters on which itself and IMVO have differed
to say that the latter was inspired by the Cape Mercury. For ourselves
we confess we fail to discern the force our Grahams­town friend appears
to attach to such an argument. But this is what the Cape Mercury has
written on this score: — “ The journal says that the Mercury in­fluences
the IMVO in political opinion. It it did there is really nothing to be
ashamed of, but as a matter of fact the proprietors of this paper have
exactly the same connection with the Native organ as the Journal has
with the periodical of the Law Society—a printing contract. All the
claim we have on the IMVO is the payment of the monthly account for
printing, and we do not control or in­fluence it in any way.”

The Watchman” Wincing-

That the Kaffrarian Watchman should wince as it did in two paragraphs in
its last issue owing to our pointing out the unreality of the Colonel’s
and its own politics we quite expected, but -were hardly prepared to
hear it say our article was a whole bushel of chaff; without a grain of
sense in it. The very fact that our remarks have made it wince thus is
proof positive that our local contempo­rary in its heart of hearts
believes no­thing of the sort. But to show that there was abundant
reason for what we said about the Kaffrarian free lance politicians,
with the swashbuckler Colonel for their fugleman, we may cite the P. E.
Tele­graph, with which we cannot be said to be in league to run down the
Colonel’s claims as a politician. The Bay organ to hand says, “ When the
Colonel ventures on an attack one is tempted to think that the attack is
merely a sham one. undertaken merely out of a spirit of sport, or in
order to raise a momentary dust We are afraid that the

country will never learn to take him seriously.” This is no more than
what we said in our article; but the Watchman— the dullest of
dullards—finds nothing but chaff in our article. Well, we can’t help it
even if we would.

A Singularly Generous act-

The Frontier Guardian (Dordrecht) re­ports an act of rare liberality on
the part of Colonial farmers towards Natives, as follows:—“ The Natives
resident in the Location here have been anxious for some years past to
have a building in which to worship, suitable to their wants and
requirements, but the bad times and general depression so prevalent all
over the Colony have stood in the way of their accomplishing this
object. Not long since several Europeans who take an interest in the
Natives came forward and made an arrangement to the effect that if the
Natives succeeded in making 40,000 bricks and raising a certain amount
of money—something like £40—they (the Europeans) would provide the means
for completing the building. But the times getting worse, instead of
better, it was found impossible to do even this. And so matters remained
until Friday even­ing last, when our Wesleyan friends met together to
welcome and consult with the Rev. R. Lamplough, District
Super­intendent, upon the occasion of his annual visit to these parts.
At this meet­ing the building of the proposed Native Church at the
Location was one of the subjects for discussion, and it was thought that
it would have to remain in abeyance until things improved somewhat. Our
good friend Mr. J. K. Stretton, how­ever, camo to the rescue and solved
the difficulty. The brothers Stretton (Messrs. John K., James, George,
and W. Henry), had only recently to deplore the death of their revered
and affectionate father, who had left them ail in very comfortable
circumstances. The brothers met to­gether, and, after taking counsel
with their aged and respected mother, came to the decision that, as they
knew the pro­ject would have been most pleasing to their father had he
been alive, they would build the Native Church at their own cost and
hand it over to the Trustees. The work will be undertaken almost
immediately, and it gives much pleasure to be able to chronicle the
noble generosity displayed by the Messrs. Stretton, who are almost
foremost in good deeds,”

THE NATIVE LANDS AGITATION.

The Free State Express

IMVO’S proposal to stump the United Kingdom and rouse the public
conscience on the Glen Grey Act reveals the fact that the champion of
the Natives has failed to read the signs of the times. A few years ago,
when the fear of Boer expansion was still in the air, Exeter Hall spoke
fre­quently, and was something like a power in politics. Since, however,
the job has been taken in hand by Mr. Rhodes, Bishops ride with the
invading hosts, and the Churches are more concerned in getting
contributions and grants from the Company than in reporting to their
London angencies the trifling eccentrici­ties of the Chartered
freebooters. IMVO should bear all this in mind, and not try to fool away
the Natives’ savings in what will only amount to a veay second-rate form
of notoriety-hunting.

The South African Methodist (Gahams- town):—

The Glen Grey Bill has passed into law, and has been proclaimed. I must
confess that in one particular it is a decided disap­pointment, namely
in the fact that the lands granted to Native erf-holders are to be
rendered liable to be taken in execution for debts. This provision has
caused great alarm to the Native people them­selves, and a petition is
spoken of to the Queen, to be taken Home by a deputation of Natives, to
urge that this clause should be abrogated. The Bill was a compro­mise ;
and I am assured on good authority that it could only have passed in the
present form, and had it been proposed that the lands should be exempted
from being attached for debt, the majority in the House of Assembly
would have rejected it. That majority were perhaps not without
difficulty brought to do justice to the Natives at all, and their
argument is that there is no reason or fairness in granting to Natives a
special privilege which white men do not possess. They further say that
if idle men in Glen Grey get into debt, it will be well to be rid of
them. This would be a good argument, if the lands were secured for at
least a certain term of years from being transferred to any other than
Natives. Probably the Legislature would be will­ing to look at this
matter next session, and grant some additional security to Native
holders of title. Native locations there must be, centres to which they
can resort when not looking for hire; and it is not for the good of the
community that they should be liable to be deprived of portions of such
locations, and the locations should perhaps by-and-by be broken up by
the intrusion of white men into them. This whole matter, and any other
objections which can be made to the Act, will how-ever, I am told, have
the careful attention of Mr. Rhodes, who purposes to make a stay of some
months in the Transkei, on his return from Matebeleland.

Tie Eastern Province Herald (Port Elizabath) gives publicity to the
following letter-: —

SIR,—Recently a most important measure, called “The Glen Grey Bill,” was
forced through Parliament, dealing most unfairly with the Natives, and,
worst of all, the Natives to whom the Bill will apply, were never given
a single chance to express their opinions. Natives know perfectly well
what was done in the House of Assembly to be unconstitu­tional, and, in
consequence, they begin to doubt the honesty of white men.

In spite of three powerful and brilliant speeches, full of facts taken
from statistics, delivered by the three leading stars in the Opposition,
the Bill was passed. The speeches were read and appreciated by the
Natives, but they did not think for one moment that facts so vividly put
by Messrs. Merriman, lunes, and Sauer could have been overlooked by the
Prime Minister. The attitude and action of the Premier has caused
considerable doubt in the Native mind whether Europeans care for the
truth. Vote is not known to the Natives. In their Councils they speak
and plead whole days till they get at the bottom of the truth.

When Pondoland was annexed and the Glen Grey Bill passed, it reminded me
of a speech delivered some time ago by the Premier at a banquet of the
Bond Con­gress in Kimberley, when he was reported to have said he
preferred land to Natives, or something approximate.

Pondos have no more rule in their country. The Glen Grey Bill is a nice
way of robbing the Natives of their lands.

The Premier said in moving for the second reading of his Glen Grey Bill,
“ This is a Native Bill for Africa.” That has to be seen yet. He said, “
They were sitting on judgment for Africa.”

I hope to live and see the working of his Native Bill for Africa and see
the effect and result of the so-called judgment delivered to the
aborigines by the noto­rious session of Parliament of 1891.

Will the Bill be acceptable to the Na­tive? Is it for their good? No, I
am inclined to think it is a Bill of oppression, a Bill to encourage
slavery, the effects o which will be felt heavily by the inhabit­ants of
the land, not Natives but the judges, or more precise by the legislators
of the country.

It was rightly said by Mr. Merriman in his brilliant speech on the
second reading of the Glen Grey Bill. “The question is a most difficult
one. We can do some­thing to make it more difficult, but at the same
time we can do something to make it more easy. The way to make it more
easy is to fling away our prejudices, and deal with it in a spirit of
right and justice.” I say after that Amen ? for that is the only way of
solving this Native problem. We are now watching the attitude of Her
Majesty the Queen to­wards various petitions sent to Her Majesty by her
poor and law-abiding subjects, the aborigines of Africa.

Ina still later issue the Eastern Province Herald writes :—

Mr. Don [Ross?] again writes us on this question, and throwing a glance
into futurity declares that the operation of the Act will be to create a
mulatto population in the Cape Colony. We cannot but think that the fear
is somewhat farfetched, for the operation of the Act, even if Natives
were dispossessed of their hold­ings would not be to create a class of
small white settlers in the midst of a location. The evil would be
confined to the fact that certain Natives would be bereft of their
holdings and that the title would pass into the hands of Europeans to
whom the Natives would be beholden as tenants. From the Native point of
view, that we take it is the grievance. From the point of view of the
Colonists ac large, the grievance is that the dispos­sessed Native would
be thrown upon the country without means and without occupation, and
that he would be driven to swell the ranks of the criminal clas6. If the
Natives stick closely to their point they should win, and they have the
power­ful circumstance in their favour that the Prime Minister thinks
with them. Under the old law they held their land by an unalienable
communal title. Under the new law their land is liable to seizure for
debt. They fear therefore that traders will be tempted to give them
credit first and seize their lands afterwards. That is a real and valid
objection to the law. and there is no real and valid reason why it
should not be removed.
